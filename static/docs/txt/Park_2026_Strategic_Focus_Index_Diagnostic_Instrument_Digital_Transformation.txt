Abstract
Digital transformation has become a central strategic priority as organizations increasingly
relyondigitaltechnologiestoredesignbusinessprocesses, governancestructures, andvalue
creation mechanisms in digitally evolving environments. However, existing approaches
to digital transformation readiness often rely on additive maturity models or capability
inventories that assume transformation capacity increases through cumulative capability
development. Such approaches overlook how strategic emphasis must be distributed
across transformation domains under governance and resource constraints. This study
addresses this limitation by conceptualizing digital transformation readiness as a problem
of strategic prioritization rather than cumulative capability accumulation. To operationalize
this perspective, the study develops the Strategic Focus Index (SFI), a governance-aligned
diagnostic instrument that evaluates how organizations distribute strategic attention across
interdependent transformation domains. The index is constructed through a two-round
Delphi study involving 53 experts from industry, academia, and the public sector, followed
by statistical validation and an illustrative diagnostic application. The findings demonstrate
how domain-level prioritization patterns can be systematically interpreted to identify
potential imbalances in transformation efforts. By reframing readiness assessment as a
prioritization-based diagnostic rather than a linear maturity measure, this study contributes
a structured approach for evaluating digital transformation in digital business and platform-
based environments.

orchestration [4,5]. Under such conditions, transformation extends beyond technology
adoption and requires the realignment of strategic priorities, organizational structures, and
governance mechanisms.
Digital business transformation therefore entails organizational transformation.
Firms must redesign processes, reallocate decision rights, and coordinate multiple
domains—including customer experience, innovation capacity, regulatory alignment, and

structural pattern through which strategic emphasis is allocated across interdependent
domains under constraint. This formulation highlights how domain importance and
potential underemphasis can be jointly interpreted within the distribution of strategic
attention. Accordingly, organizations can identify underemphasized domains, prioritize
corrective actions under resource constraints, and assess how prioritization patterns vary
across contexts.

2. Theoretical Background
2.1. Digital Transformation as Multi-Domain Organizational Change Under Constraint
Digital transformation has commonly been defined as the redesign of business pro-
cesses, organizational structures, and value creation mechanisms enabled by digital tech-
nologies [1,2]. Prior research emphasizes strategic renewal through digital innovation
and technological integration [3,21]. While these perspectives advance understanding of
digital-enabled change, they primarily frame transformation as internal capability develop-
ment [22,23].
In practice, digital transformation spans multiple organizational domains. Firms must
simultaneously address technological infrastructure, organizational processes, customer
engagement, and regulatory requirements to support digitally enabled value creation [7,24].
These domains are analytically distinct and jointly shape transformation outcomes.
Organizations operate under constraint. Managerial attention is limited, and re-
sources cannot be uniformly allocated across all domains. External conditions—such as
platform rules, coordination requirements, and ecosystem governance structures—further
shape how transformation can be implemented [25–27]. As a result, firms must allocate
attention and resources selectively across domains instead of developing all domains
simultaneously [17,28].
Digital transformation, therefore, is not a process of uniform capability accumulation.
It depends on how organizations prioritize and sequence their efforts across domains [1,4].
Accordingly, readiness is reflected not in accumulated capabilities but in how strategic
priorities are structured under constraint. Misaligned prioritization can undermine trans-
formation outcomes even when individual domain capabilities are well developed [29–31].
The distribution of strategic attention is therefore central to transformation effectiveness.
However, existing approaches provide limited means to systematically capture and inter-
pret such distributional patterns across interdependent domains, indicating the need for a
structured diagnostic instrument.
2.2. Interdependence and Complementarity in Digital Transformation
Digital transformation involves coordinated change across multiple organizational
domains [1,2]. Prior research shows that technological infrastructures, organizational
processes, customer engagement mechanisms, and governance arrangements jointly shape
transformation outcomes, rather than operating independently [3,32]. These domains are
analytically distinguishable but functionally interdependent, such that changes in one
domain alter the effectiveness of others [8,29].
Complementarity theory provides a formal explanation for this interdependence.
When activities are complementary, the effect of improving one domain depends on the

in a given domain varies depending on how related domains are configured. Isolated
improvements generate limited impact when supporting domains are underdeveloped,
whereas coordinated adjustments across domains produce disproportionately greater
effects [8,33].
This interaction logic implies that domain-level changes cannot be evaluated inde-
pendently, as their effects emerge from how they are combined with other domains. Mis-
alignment across domains can therefore diminish transformation effectiveness even when
individual domains are well developed, as complementarities remain unrealized [30,34].
Accordingly, transformation outcomes depend not only on domain-level capabilities but
also on how emphasis is distributed across domains [5,33]. Consequently, prioritization
across domains becomes a central analytical concern, as the effectiveness of any given
domain depends on its alignment with others. However, existing approaches provide
limited means to systematically capture such interdependencies in assessing transforma-
tion readiness, highlighting the need for a diagnostic instrument capable of capturing
cross-domain prioritization structures.
2.3. Measurement Limitations in Capturing Cross-Domain Interdependence
Existing studies on digital transformation predominantly operationalize organiza-
tional conditions through maturity stages, capability inventories, or composite indices
that aggregate domain-level indicators into unified scores [1,2,35,36]. Some studies ex-
plicitly model digital transformation as a function of digital orientation and capability
development, emphasizing the accumulation of organizational capabilities as a key driver
of transformation outcomes [37]. While such approaches enhance comparability, they
implicitly assume that higher cumulative capability corresponds to stronger transforma-
tion capacity [18,19,38]. For instance, recent studies conceptualize organizational digital
transformation readiness through multidimensional assessment frameworks that capture
diverse readiness conditions [16].
This aggregation logic, including recent extensions that incorporate ecosystem dynam-
ics and broader transformation dimensions [7,39], tends to underrepresent cross-domain
interaction. It also overlooks how uneven development across domains can create systemic
imbalance even when individual capabilities improve [8,30,34]. In particular, most mea-
surement frameworks adopt additive structures in which domains are treated as separable
contributors to an overall readiness score, consistent with conventional composite indi-
cator construction logic [18,19]. Other recent studies emphasize specific technological or
resource-based drivers, such as computing power investment, as key enablers of digital
transformation outcomes [40].
Such approaches rely on implicit assumptions of compensability across dimensions,
whereby deficiencies in one domain can be offset by strengths in others [18,39]. However,
in interdependent systems, domain-level effects are not independent but mutually con-
ditioning. Under such conditions, underdevelopment in one domain can constrain the
effectiveness of others, leading to non-linear and asymmetric transformation outcomes [19].
Consequently, additive measurement approaches provide limited visibility into struc-
tural imbalance across domains [34,36]. By reducing transformation to a single aggregated
score, they obscure how uneven domain development shapes outcomes and limit the
identification of domain-level bottlenecks that may constrain overall transformation perfor-
mance [39].
This limitation highlights the need for measurement approaches that move beyond ag-
gregate scoring and capture how emphasis is distributed across domains. Such approaches
enable the identification of imbalance patterns and reveal how strategic emphasis is struc-

2.4. Conceptual Foundations of the Strategic Focus Index
Building on the preceding discussion, the Strategic Focus Index (SFI) is conceptualized
as a diagnostic prioritization index that captures how strategic attention is distributed
across interdependent domains in digital transformation [41,42]. It defines transformation
readiness as the distribution of domain-level strategic emphasis under constraint, rather
than as aggregated capability levels.
The core premise of the SFI is that transformation outcomes are not determined by the
absolute level of capabilities within individual domains, but by how strategic emphasis
is structured across interdependent domains. When domains are interdependent, the
effectiveness of any given domain depends on its alignment with others. As a result,
transformation effectiveness cannot be inferred from isolated capability assessments and
must be understood in terms of cross-domain alignment [43,44].
To operationalize this perspective, the SFI evaluates the relative distribution of strate-
gic emphasis across domains rather than aggregating heterogeneous indicators into a
single composite score [18,19]. It generates a domain-level prioritization profile that re-
flects how attention is allocated across transformation areas. This approach adopts a
non-compensatory logic, where under-emphasis in one domain cannot be offset by strength
in others [1,36]. Because domains are structurally interdependent, imbalance across do-
mains can constrain overall transformation effectiveness even when individual capabilities
are well developed [1,39].
Accordingly, the SFI enables the systematic identification of underemphasized
and overemphasized domains—patterns that remain obscured in aggregate scoring ap-
proaches [36,39]. This diagnostic perspective provides a structured basis for prioritizing
transformation efforts under constraint [1,42], by highlighting potential underempha-
sis in relation to domain importance, informing the allocation of managerial attention
and resources.
The SFI complements existing measurement approaches by providing a diagnostic
lens on how transformation efforts are distributed across domains and where structural
imbalances may arise [1,7]. In this way, the SFI supports the diagnosis of structural imbal-
ance and guides prioritization decisions. By making the distribution of strategic attention
analytically visible, the SFI establishes a distinct conceptual foundation for understanding
and guiding digital transformation.
2.5. Distinguishing the SFI from Existing Measurement Approaches
Existing approaches to digital transformation readiness, including maturity models,
composite indices, and multidimensional frameworks, provide useful insights but remain
limited in capturing how strategic emphasis is distributed across interdependent domains
under constraint.

development [12,13,15]. They assume a linear and cumulative path and therefore cannot
capture how uneven strategic emphasis shapes transformation outcomes. For example,

development across organizational contexts [45].
Composite indices aggregate multiple dimensions into a single score using additive
and often compensatory logic [18,46]. This structure assumes that deficiencies in one
domain can be offset by strengths in others. In interdependent systems, this assumption
does not hold, as underdevelopment in one domain can constrain the effectiveness of

3. Methodology
3.1. Research Design
This study adopts a structured research design to develop and validate the Strate-
gic Focus Index (SFI), a governance-aligned diagnostic instrument that captures how
strategic attention is distributed across interdependent domains in digital transformation.
Consistent with established instrument development principles in information systems
research [47], the objective is not to construct a reflective latent scale but to capture the
distribution of strategic emphasis across domains [48]. In line with formative and compos-
ite construct reasoning [49], the SFI preserves domain separability rather than reducing
transformation to a single-dimensional construct. The domain structure and associated
strategic imperatives adopted in this study build on prior research that systematically iden-
tified and validated digital transformation domains through structured literature synthesis
and expert-based validation. Rather than redefining these elements, the study uses the
established domain–imperative structure as a conceptual foundation and operationalizes it
into a diagnostic prioritization index.
Digital transformation domains were derived from a structured synthesis of prior
digital transformation and digital business literature and subsequently refined through ex-
pert review to ensure conceptual separability while preserving functional interdependence.
Expert selection prioritized individuals with direct involvement in digital transformation–
relateddecision-makingandimplementation, ensuringtherelevanceandvalidityofprioriti-
zationjudgments. Tostabilizeprioritizationjudgmentsacrossheterogeneousorganizational
contexts, a two-round Delphi procedure was employed. Delphi methods are particularly
appropriate when structured expert judgment is required to evaluate multidimensional and
interdependent phenomena [20,48]. Because the SFI models relative strategic prioritization,
expert consensus provides a suitable basis for deriving stable domain-level emphasis.
Stabilized evaluations were translated into a composite index using rank-based weight-
ing combined with normalized importance scores. In accordance with established com-
posite indicator construction principles [18,19], the aggregation structure preserves the
interaction between prioritization order and evaluative intensity, avoiding the assumption
of additive independence. This construction allows the index to reflect both the relative
ordering of strategic priorities and their perceived importance, thereby capturing the
structure of emphasis.
The approach does not assume full compensability and instead reflects how priori-
tization differences shape the overall configuration of transformation efforts. To ensure
the validity of this structure, subsequent analyses assess the stability and robustness of the
prioritization patterns under alternative weighting assumptions.

3.3. Delphi Design and Panel Selection
A two-round Delphi study was designed to derive a stable and context-sensitive
prioritization structure across transformation domains. Delphi techniques are appropriate
for evaluating complex, multi-dimensional phenomena where objective benchmarks are not
readily available [20,48], particularly when capturing the distribution of strategic emphasis
rather than observable behavior.
The expert panel consisted of 53 participants drawn from academia, industry, and
public institutions, ensuring cross-sectoral representation across manufacturing, services,
ICT, and policy domains. While panel size contributes to the stability of Delphi-based
judgment, panel appropriateness is determined primarily by the relevance of partici-
pants’ roles and their direct exposure to digital transformation–related decision-making
and implementation contexts. Participants were recruited from a pool of profession-
als with formal training in business and management and direct exposure to organiza-
tional decision-making contexts, ensuring baseline managerial literacy while capturing
practice-informed judgments.
Digital transformation decisions are distributed across multiple organizational levels,
including both strategic and operational roles [62]. Accordingly, expertise in this study
is defined by direct engagement with transformation processes and exposure to decision-

relevant contexts, rather than by hierarchical seniority alone, consistent with established
Delphi research emphasizing relevance over formal position [20,63].
To ensure rigor in participant selection, a two-stage screening procedure was applied.
First, role-based filtering identified individuals working in functions directly related to
digital transformation, such as strategy, IT infrastructure, operations, or organizational
change. Second, experience-based filtering excluded respondents without direct involve-
ment in transformation-related projects or decision-making. Inclusion required direct
involvement in digital transformation activities, supported by either a minimum of three
years of relevant professional experience or advanced academic training (e.g., master’s or
doctoral-level training), ensuring sufficient domain familiarity and informed judgment.
A total of 87 potential participants were invited, of whom 65 expressed willingness
to participate. After applying the screening criteria, 53 experts were retained for the final
Delphi panel.
Several procedural safeguards were implemented to reduce potential bias. The panel
included participants from diverse industries and organizational contexts to limit sector-
specific bias. In addition, all responses were collected anonymously to minimize social
desirability bias and hierarchical influence, supporting independent judgment.
While heterogeneity is essential to capture diverse perspectives, differences in domain-
specific experience are also relevant for interpreting prioritization judgments [20,48]. Ac-
cordingly, experience is incorporated as a weighting factor during aggregation, reflecting
variation in experiential depth without affecting participant inclusion.
Following panel construction, the Delphi procedure was implemented to elicit and
stabilize domain-level prioritization judgments.
In Round 1, experts evaluated each transformation domain and its associated strategic
imperatives in terms of both importance and priority. Importance was measured using a
five-point Likert scale, while priority was elicited through ranking tasks to capture relative
strategic emphasis. This dual design distinguishes intrinsic relevance from comparative
prioritization, enabling transformation to be modeled as a problem of strategic attention
allocation under resource constraints [18,19]. Qualitative feedback was also collected to
identify ambiguities, redundancies, and potential gaps, supporting content validity and
construct refinement [47].
Consensus stabilization was assessed using Kendall’s coefficient of concordance (W)
and interquartile range (IQR) thresholds. The observed level of agreement (W ≈ 0.51)
indicates moderate convergence, which is acceptable in Delphi studies addressing com-
plex and multidimensional phenomena where full consensus is neither expected nor de-
sirable [48,63]. Accordingly, the objective of the Delphi process was to derive a stable
and interpretable prioritization structure. Once convergence was observed after the sec-
ond round, additional iterations were not pursued, consistent with established Delphi
stopping criteria.

compensability, multiplicative aggregation limits the extent to which high values in one

defines domain-level emphasis in interdependent systems [18,43,64].

but as a bounded adjustment factor reflecting variation in respondents’ exposure to digital
transformation contexts. The weighting variable is normalized and applied symmetri-
cally across respondents, and robustness analyses confirm that the prioritization structure
remains stable under alternative specifications, including unweighted scenarios. This is
consistent with the role of experiential knowledge in shaping strategic judgment in complex
organizational settings [20,65].
The SFI is interpreted as a domain-level prioritization profile rather than a single
aggregated readiness score. This interpretation aligns with the conceptualization of dig-
ital transformation readiness in this study, where readiness reflects the distribution of
strategic emphasis across interdependent domains under constraint. Accordingly, diag-
nostic interpretation focuses on the relative distribution of domain scores rather than their
absolute magnitude.
In this context, domains exhibiting relatively lower scores indicate potential areas of
underemphasis that may constrain transformation outcomes, particularly when considered
alongside their structural importance. Conversely, domains with relatively higher scores
reflect areas of concentrated strategic attention. This interpretation supports the identifica-
tion of imbalance patterns across domains and informs prioritization of corrective actions,
rather than serving as a definitive measure of transformation performance.

Validation assesses the stability, robustness, and interpretability of domain-level prior-
itization patterns, consistent with the non-reflective and configurational nature of the SFI.
Unlike reflective measurement approaches that assume an underlying latent construct, the
SFI conceptualizes digital transformation readiness as a structured distribution of strategic

attention across interdependent domains. Accordingly, validation focuses on the structural
consistency and meaningfulness of prioritization patterns rather than latent dimensional
convergence [66].
Four complementary mechanisms are employed: structural stability, robustness to
aggregation assumptions, convergent alignment with underlying imperative structures,
and contextual differentiation across organizational settings. These mechanisms reflect key
validation requirements for non-reflective and configurational measures, where validity is
established through structural consistency, robustness, and contextual sensitivity rather
than latent construct coherence.
Structural stability is assessed using Kendall’s coefficient of concordance (W) in con-
junction with interquartile range (IQR) thresholds to evaluate convergence in expert judg-
ments across Delphi rounds. This approach captures both aggregate agreement and item-
level dispersion, ensuring that the resulting prioritization structure reflects stabilized
collective judgment rather than individual variability [20].
Robustness to aggregation assumptions is examined through alternative weighting
specifications, including equal weighting, entropy-based weights, and controlled perturba-
tions of baseline weights. Rank-order correlations across these scenarios assess whether
the prioritization structure remains stable under different aggregation logics. This step is
critical given the sensitivity of composite indices to weighting choices and ensures that
observed prioritization patterns are not artifacts of specific parameter settings [18].
Convergent alignment is evaluated by examining the directional consistency between
domain-level prioritization scores and aggregated imperative-level evaluations using rank-
order correlations. This analysis assesses whether domain-level scores reflect underlying
patterns in imperative-level distributions, supporting the interpretation that the SFI cap-
tures meaningful domain structures rather than arbitrary rankings.
Contextual differentiation is assessed by computing group-specific SFI profiles and
examining variations in domain-level prioritization across organizational contexts. Rather
than testing isolated differences, this approach evaluates whether the overall configuration
of strategic emphasis shifts systematically across contexts. This perspective is consistent
with configurational theories, which emphasize that organizational phenomena are better
understood as patterned combinations of interdependent elements rather than additive
effects of independent variables [43,64].
Collectively, these results provide consistent evidence that the SFI captures stable,
non-arbitrary, and context-sensitive prioritization patterns, supporting its validity as a
configurational diagnostic instrument.

5. Discussion
5.1. The SFI as a Diagnostic Prioritization Instrument
Digital transformation has been widely conceptualized as a multi-domain and interde-
pendent process involving coordinated changes across technological, organizational, and
value-creation dimensions rather than a linear accumulation of capabilities [2,67]. However,

many existing readiness and maturity frameworks continue to operationalize transfor-
mation as an additive progression across domains, implicitly assuming that strengths in
one area can compensate for weaknesses in others [12,15]. Such additive assumptions
overlook the structural interdependencies that characterize digital transformation and the
constraints under which strategic attention must be allocated [1].
The empirical results indicate that domain-level emphasis is not uniformly distributed
but follows a stable and differentiated prioritization structure across domains. As shown
in the Delphi results and SFI weight configurations, certain domains (e.g., Customer
and Value) systematically receive greater emphasis, whereas others (e.g., Regulation and
Competition) are comparatively deprioritized yet remain structurally necessary. The
observed prioritization pattern suggests that transformation outcomes are shaped not only
by the level of capability development but by how strategic attention is selectively allocated
across interdependent domains [17].
The SFI differs from conventional readiness and maturity frameworks in that it
captures transformation readiness as a configuration of prioritization under constraint
rather than as an additive composite score [6,7]. By modeling readiness as a structured
distribution of strategic emphasis, the SFI makes visible structural imbalances that re-
main obscured in aggregate assessments and cannot be identified through compensatory
measurement approaches.
In this context, the SFI is best understood as a diagnostic prioritization instrument
rather than a composite readiness score. Instead of aggregating domain-level conditions
into a single measure, the SFI represents readiness as a structured distribution of strategic
attention. Preserving domain-level differentiation enables the identification of misaligned
prioritization patterns that may constrain transformation effectiveness.
Viewing readiness as a prioritization structure clarifies how transformation assessment
can inform decision-making. The SFI provides a basis for identifying domains where
strategic attention may be misaligned with their structural importance. This enables
organizations to identify potential areas of reprioritization, where underemphasized but
structurally critical domains can be addressed and overemphasized domains reconsidered.

Accordingly, the focus of transformation assessment shifts from capability magnitude
to prioritization structure. Rather than assessing how digitally advanced an organization is,
the SFI enables an examination of whether strategic attention is distributed in a balanced or
potentially misaligned manner. Transformation is therefore interpreted as a configuration
of interdependent priorities rather than a unidimensional state.
This reframing extends current approaches by introducing a distributional and
configuration-based view of readiness. By treating readiness as a property of how strategic
attention is structured under constraint, the study highlights the importance of prioritiza-
tion and cross-domain alignment [6,7]. This perspective aligns with emerging views on
digital strategic agility, where the ability to dynamically reconfigure priorities is critical in
complex and evolving environments.

Existing research on digital transformation has predominantly conceptualized readi-
ness as an aggregate function of capabilities distributed across multiple domains [1,2,35].
This perspective assumes that transformation capacity increases as organizations accumu-
late capabilities and that deficiencies in one domain can be compensated for by strengths
in others [39]. However, this assumption limits the ability to capture how transformation
unfolds under conditions of interdependence and constraint.

The primary theoretical contribution of this study lies in reconceptualizing digital

that shape prioritization under constraint. In digital business environments character-

strategic attention under constraint. Third, it establishes a configuration-based diagnostic
approach that enables the identification of structurally significant imbalances across inter-
dependent domains, providing a distinct analytical framework for studying transformation
in complex and context-dependent environments.
5.3. Managerial Implications
The findings suggest that effective digital transformation management requires a
shift from capability accumulation toward prioritization alignment. In interdependent
transformation contexts, uneven allocation of strategic attention may influence overall
transformation effectiveness, even when aggregate capability levels appear sufficient. This
challenge has also been observed in practice, where organizations often appear digitally
advanced in aggregate yet struggle to achieve coherent transformation outcomes due
to uneven alignment across domains [68,69]. The SFI addresses this gap by making the
distribution of strategic attention across domains analytically visible, providing a structured
basis for managerial decision-making.
Prioritization decisions should consider both the magnitude of domain-level gaps
and their structural importance. Domains with higher structural weights and substantial
gaps may require earlier intervention, while lower-weight domains may warrant targeted
adjustment depending on their interaction effects with other domains. This implies that

prioritization is shaped not only by gap size but by how gaps are embedded within the

From a managerial perspective, SFI profiles should be interpreted as diagnostic signals
rather than performance scores. Domains receiving relatively low emphasis may indi-

in other domains, while disproportionately emphasized domains may reflect potential
over-allocation relative to their strategic role. Transformation challenges may therefore arise

one domain may affect the contribution of others. For example, strong technological
capabilities may not fully translate into transformation outcomes if customer engagement,
value configuration, or regulatory alignment receive comparatively less attention. By
representing transformation as a distribution of strategic emphasis, the SFI makes such

across domains. Increasing emphasis in one domain necessarily implies relatively reduced

can adjust resource allocation by linking domain-level imbalances to specific strategic
imperatives, enabling more informed consideration of corrective actions.
These prioritization decisions can be operationalized through concrete managerial
interventions. In practice, this may involve reallocating investment portfolios toward
underemphasized domains, redesigning cross-functional coordination mechanisms to
better align interdependent activities, and adjusting governance structures to ensure that
structurally critical domains are sufficiently integrated into transformation initiatives. In
this way, the SFI translates diagnostic insight into actionable organizational change.
This structured approach shifts the objective of transformation management from
maximizing capability levels across all domains to aligning the distribution of strategic
emphasis with the organization’s specific contextual constraints. This perspective is con-
sistent with observations that digital transformation increasingly requires the integration
of interconnected products, services, and systems rather than isolated capability develop-

5.4. Limitations and Future Research
The Strategic Focus Index (SFI) is derived from a structured expert-based prioritization
process that captures stabilized evaluations of strategic emphasis across domains. While
such an approach is appropriate for examining complex and multidimensional phenomena,
it reflects assessed prioritization rather than observed organizational behavior [72]. Ac-
cordingly, the findings are based on cross-sectional, expert-elicited evaluations, which may
not fully reflect how prioritization is enacted in real organizational settings. The SFI there-
fore captures how strategic emphasis is evaluated, not how it is implemented in practice,
which may introduce a gap between assessed prioritization and observed transformation
outcomes [68].
In addition, the study relies on a single methodological approach based on expert-
driven assessment and index construction. While this design is suitable for capturing
structured prioritization patterns, it limits the ability to draw causal inferences regarding
https://doi.org/10.3390/jtaer21050134

--- PAGE 21 ---
J. Theor. Appl. Electron. Commer. Res. 2026, 21, 134 21 of 29
the relationship between domain-level prioritization and transformation outcomes. The
findings should therefore be interpreted as indicative of structural patterns rather than
causal effects.
The SFI represents digital transformation as a pattern of domain-level prioritization
at a given point in time. While this enables structured diagnosis, it does not capture how
prioritization evolves in response to changing external conditions. Digital transformation
unfolds in environments shaped by evolving platform rules, regulatory requirements, and
technological trajectories [1,2,5,35,69]. Future research can adopt longitudinal designs to
examine how prioritization patterns evolve over time and how such dynamics influence
transformation outcomes.
Furthermore, future studies can employ experimental or quasi-experimental designs
to examine causal relationships between domain-level prioritization and transformation
performance. Such approaches allow researchers to isolate the effects of prioritization
structures and assess their impact under controlled or comparable conditions, thereby
strengthening the causal validity of the SFI framework.
The strategic imperatives in this study are not intended to function as independently
validated measurement items, but rather as structured inputs informing domain-level
evaluation. This reflects a deliberate design choice aligned with the configurational nature
of the SFI. While this ensures analytical coherence, it may not fully capture all context-
specific nuances of digital transformation, and alternative specifications or refinements can
be explored in future research.
Finally, the domain structure adopted in this study is intentionally parsimonious to
preserve conceptual clarity and analytical separability. At the same time, digital trans-
formation manifests differently across industries, organizational forms, and institutional
environments [3,36,42]. Future research can refine or extend the domain architecture to ex-
amine boundary conditions under which specific prioritization patterns are most effective,
while preserving the diagnostic logic of the SFI.
6. Conclusions
This study introduces the Strategic Focus Index (SFI) as a diagnostic instrument
for digital transformation in digital business environments. Moving beyond additive
maturity models, the SFI conceptualizes transformation as the distribution of strategic
emphasis across interdependent domains under governance constraints. Unlike prior
approaches that treat readiness as a cumulative function of capabilities, the findings
show that such assumptions can obscure structurally meaningful imbalances across
domains. By combining expert-based Delphi stabilization with structured index con-
struction, the study provides a transparent and reproducible approach for examining
cross-domain prioritization.
The findings indicate that digital transformation cannot be fully explained by cumu-
lative capability accumulation alone. Instead, transformation outcomes are associated
with how strategic emphasis is distributed across domains. The stability of domain-level
prioritization across alternative specifications suggests that these patterns reflect consis-
tent prioritization structures rather than modeling artifacts. The illustrative application
further demonstrates how different configurations of strategic emphasis emerge across
organizational contexts.

reframes readiness as a domain-level prioritization problem under constraint rather than
as a function of accumulated capabilities. Second, it introduces a distributional perspective
that captures how strategic attention is allocated across interdependent domains while

The SFI reflects stabilized expert-based prioritization rather than directly observed
organizational behavior. Future research can extend this approach through longitudinal
applications, context-specific adaptation, and empirical examination of how prioritization
patterns relate to transformation outcomes under evolving governance conditions.
From a practical standpoint, the SFI offers a decision-oriented framework that supports
prioritization under constraint by guiding attention and resources toward domains that are
both underemphasized and structurally consequential. Digital transformation is therefore
not solely a matter of capability advancement, but of how strategic attention is allocated
across interdependent domains under constraint.
