Keywords: C-ITS, Interoperability, Standard Lock-in, Enterprise Architecture, Governance.
Abstract: This paper introduces a methodology to support automated governance in complex, standards-based system
architectures. In many critical sectors, long-term interoperability is hindered by the absence of governance
frameworks that span the lifecycle of evolving standards. Static compliance alone often leads to standard
lock-in, increased management overhead, and fragmentation across stakeholders. To address this, we propose
an architecture-driven approach inspired by practices from domains such as healthcare, energy, and Indus-
try 4.0. The methodology builds on the IHE architectural model, which enables modular system design and
embeds governance rules directly into machine-readable architectural specifications. By organizing systems
into interoperable building blocks with well-defined interfaces, the approach isolates changes and facilitates
automated verification of compliance. The methodology is demonstrated through a proof-of-concept use case
in the Cooperative Intelligent Transport Systems (C-ITS) sector, illustrating its applicability to real-world,
multi-stakeholder environments. This method offers a scalable and sustainable path for maintaining interoper-
ability over time, reducing reliance on manual coordination and enabling consistent policy enforcement across
diverse systems and vendors.
1 INTRODUCTION
From the early days of the Information and Commu-
nication Technology (ICT), one of the main problems
hampering system evolution has been vendor lock-in
(Farber, 2012). This occurs when organizations be-
come dependent on particular technology providers
for software, hardware, or services, making switching
prohibitively costly or technically challenging. Over
time, such dependency constrains the ability of sys-
tems to evolve, adapt to new technologies, and re-
main economically sustainable, particularly in critical
infrastructures where long-term resilience and trans-
parency are essential. Vendor lock-in is often rein-
forced by proprietary solutions, such as non-standard
data formats or undocumented APIs, which hinder in-
teroperability and migration (Abhishek and Siwach,

Open standards offer a well-established mitigation

2 METHODOLOGY
The methodology proposed in this paper addresses
standard lock-in by embedding governance principles
directly into the system architecture.2 It introduces
a domain-driven approach in which interoperability
is achieved through the definition of INTEGRATION
PROFILES that structure and constrain the use of stan-
dards.3 Building on the IHE architectural model,
the methodology enables the specification of inter-
operable building blocks and their interactions in a
machine-readable form, supporting automated verifi-
cation of compliance and governance rules. The ap-
proach is illustrated through its application to the C-
ITS domain.
2.1 IHE Technical Framework
Integrating the Healthcare Enterprise (IHE) promotes
the coordinated use of existing standards to achieve
interoperability across heterogeneous systems (ITI
Technical Committee, 2019). It features an ar-
chitectural model that includes functional compo-
nents, known as ACTORS, and specifies their interac-
tions through standardized TRANSACTIONS, grouped
into functional units called INTEGRATION PROFILES.
These profiles represent reusable functional units that
address specific interoperability requirements. They

A key strength of IHE lies in its ability to
express system architectures in a structured and

where vendors test interoperability in practice, foster-

this study, we adopt the core IHE concepts - profiles,
domains, and conformance verification - as the foun-
dation for a governance-aware architectural frame-
work that supports automated compliance and long-
term interoperability.
2.2 How the IHE Process Works
The IHE governance model is based on three core
principles: selection of relevant standards, defini-
tion of integration profiles that constrain their use,
and validation through interoperability testing. Build-
ing on this foundation, our methodology defines a
governance-aware architectural process in which pro-
files are used as the primary mechanism to structure
systems and manage standards evolution.
The process begins with the identification of a do-
main use case. A profile is then created that addresses
the use case. Profiles are defined by selecting and
constraining existing standards, specifying ACTORS,
TRANSACTIONS, and optional variations. Depen-
dencies between profiles are expressed as GROUPING
RULES; profiles may be composed by choice or to sat-
isfy such dependencies, as reusable building blocks,
to build solution architectures. Interoperability is rig-
orously validated at CONNECTATHONS, where candi-
date solutions are required to successfully interoper-
ate with at least three independent vendor implemen-
tations. Successfulresultsarepublishedas INTEGRA-
TION STATEMENTS, which stakeholders can use in
procurement and for system design.
To support a smooth and sustainable evolution
over time, the methodology introduces CHANGE PRO-
POSALS, in which proponents define the rationale for
a change in a profile (IHE, 2020a). Change proposals
may introduce new work items (e.g., new profiles) or
evolve existing standards, while preserving backward
compatibility. This ensures that system architectures

2.3 The Rationale for C-ITS as an IHE
Domain
The Cooperative Intelligent Transport Systems (C-
ITS) domain provides a relevant and challenging con-
text for applying governance-aware architectures, as
it relies on a complex ecosystem of interoperating
componentsandmultiplestandardizationbodies(e.g.,
ETSI, ISO, IEEE). While standards enable interop-
erability, their independent evolution and heteroge-
neous adoption across stakeholders introduce frag-
mentation, increasing coordination effort and risking
compatibility issues across systems and regions.
Existing standardization efforts have addressed
interoperability at the technical level, but gover-
nance over the lifecycle of evolving standards re-
mains limited. In particular, maintaining backward
and forward compatibility, aligning security require-
ments, and ensuring consistent implementation across
vendors present significant challenges. These is-
sues are further amplified by the integration of di-
verse communication schemes - vehicle-to-vehicle
(V2V), infrastructure-to-vehicle (I2V), and vehicle-
to-infrastructure (V2I) - and the coexistence of re-
gional and global standards.
By introducing C-ITS as an IHE domain, we pro-
vide a structured framework to manage these chal-
lenges through the definition of INTEGRATION PRO-
FILES and governance rules. This approach enables
the systematic organization of standards, supports
consistent policy enforcement, and facilitates interop-
erability over time, reducing fragmentation and im-
proving the sustainability of C-ITS deployments.
2.4 Introducing C-ITS as a New IHE
Domain
To create a new domain, the initial step involves
defining a set of profiles, each encapsulating specific
functionalities pertinent to Vehicle-to-Infrastructure
(V2I) and Infrastructure-to-Vehicle (I2V) communi-
cation. These profiles encompass various communi-
cation protocols already established in C-ITS. Pro-
files are organized into a TECHNICAL FRAMEWORK,
consisting of two volumes: VOLUME 1 provides an
overview of USE CASE(s), ACTORS, and TRANSAC-
TIONS, whereas VOLUME 2 specifies the underlying
TRANSACTIONS and standards (IHE, 2019). Subse-
quently, a set of TEST ASSERTIONS is formulated to
facilitate the creation of hypothetical CONNECT-A-
THON events, pivotal for the definition of the neces-
sary quality assurance measures. Domains serve as
structured communities in which technical and busi-
ness stakeholders convene to exchange insights and
prioritize information sharing, as presented in Sec-
tion 4. For the effective implementation of the C-ITS
domain, the establishment of TECHNICAL and PLAN-
NING COMMITTEES is of critical importance. The
TECHNICAL COMMITTEE is tasked with developing
and documenting solutions, primarily INTEGRATION
PROFILES. Conversely, the PLANNING COMMITTEE
is responsible for long-term strategic planning, sus-
tainability initiatives, and overseeing deployment ac-
tivities, such as testing and educational events (IHE,
2020b).

3.2 Profile Selection
Following the guidelines outlined by IHE for the cre-
ation of new domains and to foster consistency in
standardization procedures within the realm of C-ITS,
we devise the following profiles essential for the ap-
plication of our methodology:
• Audit Trail and Node Authentication (ATNA),
defining the security environment within which
the ITS-S (Intelligent Transport Systems - Sta-
tions) operate4.
• Exchange of ITS-S Messages (EIM), the proto-
col for message exchange over DSRC, offering an
alternative option for “5G sharing”;
• Road Works Warning (RWW), designed to
specify the data elements of the Decentralized En-
vironmental Notification Message (DENM) and
relevant infrastructural considerations, such as
firewall configurations to prevent the transmission
of potentially malicious messages;
• Share Kinematic Data (SKD), delineating the
structure of Cooperative Awareness Messages
(CAM) and the associated infrastructure require-
ments for sharing vehicle kinematic data; and
• RSU-to-Backend communication (R2B), estab-
lishing an ASN.1 structure for facilitating mes-
sage exchange between Road Side Units (RSUs)
and IT backend systems.

Message creation takes place within a controlled
environment, typically the operator’s internal IT in-
frastructure, where data remains restricted. Con-
versely, during transmission, messages are dissemi-
nated publicly as part of the Decentralized Environ-
mental Notification Message (DENM) standard. This
distinction leads to different security requirements
across the message lifecycle. Accordingly, we define
the security goals as follows: Confidentiality, for the
creation phase, and Availability, for the transmission
phase.
During the architectural analysis, it emerges that
queueing mechanisms are not covered by any existing
profile within the C-ITS domain. To address this gap,
affecting the Availability security goal, we introduce
a temporary profile, denoted as π′, and reassess the
architecture once more. This addition is found not
to affect other stakeholder concerns, while ensuring
that all necessary cyber-security countermeasures are
explicitly captured. In the resulting architecture, TLS
is enforced through the ATNA profile, while queueing
mechanisms are included via π′. The final solution
architecture is thus defined as <RWW, EIM, ATNA,
π′ >, completing the methodology.

4.3 A Connect-a-Thon
A key element of the proposed methodology is the
ability to verify interoperability and governance re-
quirements in a systematic and reproducible man-
ner. Within the IHE framework, this is achieved
throughConnectathonevents, whereimplementations
are tested against predefined test assertions derived
from integration profiles. Test assertions formalize
the requirements specified in the profiles by translat-
ing them into verifiable conditions, following estab-
lished guidelines (OASIS, 2013; S. Bradner, 1997).
Each assertion defines a normative source (e.g., a pro-
file or transaction), a prescription level (e.g., manda-
tory or optional), and a specific execution context.
For example, a requirement from the ATNA profile
may state that messages exchanged in V2I and I2V
communications must be signed according to ETSI
TS 102 940 v1.3.1. Such requirements are expressed
as assertions that can be evaluated during testing.
In our methodology, these validation mechanisms
play a central role by operationalizing the governance
rules embedded in the architecture. Since profiles
encode both interoperability and security constraints,
conformance testing ensures that implementations ad-
here to the intended architectural behavior across ven-
dors and deployments. This enables automated or
semi-automated verification of compliance, reducing
reliance on manual coordination and supporting con-
sistent enforcement of policies. The use of standard-
ized testing frameworks, such as those promoted by
the European Commission (CEN, 2020), ensures in-
teroperability not only at the system level but also
across testing environments. By integrating confor-

5 RELATED WORK
Ensuring compliance of C-ITS systems with stan-
dards is a critical requirement, typically verified
through independent audits. However, existing stud-
ies highlight significant shortcomings and inconsis-
tencies in current standards, indicating the need for
more robust and coherent approaches to interoper-
ability and security (Takahito Yoshizawa et al., 2022;
Brian Fabrgue, 2023). Governance approaches based
on Enterprise Architecture (EA) have been widely ap-
plied in critical sectors. Frameworks such as TO-
GAF (The Open Group, 2020), Zachman (Gerber
et al., 2020), and SAIF (HL7, 2020) provide struc-
tured methods to support sustainability and interop-
erability. Despite these efforts, standard lock-in re-
mains a persistent issue, largely due to the lack of
harmonized security architectures and lifecycle gov-
ernance mechanisms. This increases the complexity
of securing C-ITS deployments and calls for scalable
solutions capable of managing evolving standards and
heterogeneous implementations.
Recent works on cellular V2X technologies and
architectural approaches further highlight the increas-
ing complexity of integrating heterogeneous com-
munication paradigms and standards, particularly as
3GPP-basedsolutionsevolvetosupportdiversesafety
and traffic efficiency applications (Soto et al., 2022;
Singh et al., 2019). These studies emphasize the chal-
lenges related to interoperability and standard align-
ment, reinforcing the need for governance-driven ar-
chitectural approaches. In addition, C-ITS use cases
have been extensively studied (C-ITS Platform, 2016;
Lu et al., 2018; Santa et al., 2014). ETSI clas-
sifies them into four application classes and multi-
ple applications, identifying a broad set of scenar-
ios for deployment (ETSI, 2018). More recently, C-
Roads has refined this classification by organizing
use cases into service-oriented groupings to improve
clarity and deployment alignment (C-Roads, 2020).
These efforts provide a comprehensive foundation of
use cases but do not address the governance and life-
cycle challenges associated with evolving standards,
which are the primary focus of this work. Closely
related to our work is Integrating the Energy System
(IES) project (Gottschalk et al., 2018; Franzl et al.,
2019), which addresses interoperability in Smart Grid
systems using an IHE-based approach. While shar-
ing a similar foundation, our work extends this direc-
tion by embedding governance directly into the archi-
tecture and enabling automated definition of security
countermeasures along the asset lifecycle.
6 CONCLUSIONS
The methodology proposed in this paper offers a path
to designing interoperable and sustainable systems.
A key objective is to reduce both the risks of ven-
dor lock-in and standard lock-in by promoting the use
of modular and open security standards. By viewing
standards as governed architectural assets, we reduce
manual coordination, improve resilience, and support
system evolution. The IHE model demonstrates that
structured governance enables automation, promotes
vendor neutrality, and strengthens multi-stakeholder
collaboration principles we now adapt for C-ITS.
The framework supports the verification of compli-
ance with standards and governance policies, help-
ing to ensure interoperability, architectural flexibil-
ity, and long-term sustainability. By encoding confor-
mance rules into machine-readable specifications, the
methodology enables automated compliance checks
and systematic governance. Standards are no longer
merely referenced; they are made operational within
the architecture, facilitating interoperability and long-
term sustainability across different C-ITS actors.
