A B S T R A C T
This paper systematically reviews the available empirical insights on the interplay between financial governance
and information technology governance and their impact on the performance of institutions in digital-intensive
sectors. Using a structured search approach among the 2015–2024 periods, forty-six empirical studies were
retrieved and assessed through a qualitative meta-synthesis using bibliometric and text-mining analyses. The
findings show that the synergy between financial governance structures and mechanisms, such as governance
indices, and IT governance structures and frameworks, such as COBIT, committees, and boards’ IT competencies,
and other related variables, forms an architecture that has the ability to leverage performance. Through the forty-
six studies, there was consistency in the performance gains through the synergy of the governance structures and
variables. These performance gains include better financial performance, better organizational ability, and better
digital performance. The findings further explain that classical financial controls rely on IT-enabled structures in
their continued aims for transparency and quality in management and decision-making. There exists an unful­
filled knowledge gap in the studies concerning the possible relation between the performance measures in
financial governance and IT governance. In an effort to develop and provide a meta-synthesis approach, the
paper recommends the “Synergistic Governance Mechanism Model” and proposes five clusters, namely, “struc­
tural oversight, risk and compliance, information integrity, alignment and strategic integration, and dynamic
capability” that addresses the synergy between performance and the five proposed clusters. The model and the
approach help in the development and awareness pertinent to the synergy approach on governance. The paper
develops the theory on the synergy approach and addresses the need for specialization in the domain. The paper
aims to inspire and recommend the development and implementation process using the synergy approach.
Upcoming studies should explore the different domain studies using the approach. The studies aiming to create
awareness and the need for development should contain evidence beyond 2023. The need and development in
the paper are and should be measurable.
1. Introduction
In digitally intensive sectors, the effectiveness of financial gover­
nance increasingly depends on information technology (IT)-enabled
infrastructures. Financial reporting, internal control, risk management,
and audit processes are now embedded in enterprise information sys­
tems and data-driven platforms so that financial integrity and techno­
logical reliability are intertwined rather than separable concerns
(Hamdan et al., 2019; Lunardi et al., 2014). Yet academic and regulatory
debates typically continue to treat “financial governance” and “IT
governance” as distinct domains, each with its theories, indicators, and
assessment frameworks. This separation obscures how configurations of
financial and IT governance mechanisms operate together to influence
institutional performance. Moreover, a large body of research docu­
ments how financial and corporate governance mechanisms such as

This fragmented treatment produces three specific gapsthat limitour
understanding of governance in digitally intensive institutions. First, we
lack an integrated view of how financial and IT governance mechanisms
co-occur and interact within the sameorganisations. Existing work tends
to focus either on financial/corporate governance performance re­

on their joint configurations. Second, there is minimal systematic evi­

structures (e.g. boards, audit committees, disclosure indices) with IT
governance mechanisms (e.g. IT steering committees, COBIT-based
controls, IT-enabled capabilities) and on how such alignment is linked
to institutional performance. Third, there is no consolidated,
mechanism-based framework that conceptualizes “synergistic gover­
nance” by specifying which clusters of governance mechanisms jointly
underpin financial, institutional, and digital performance outcomes.
Addressing these gaps requires examining financial governance and
IT governance within a single analytical lens. In practice, financial
governance mechanisms increasingly rely on IT-enabled structures: in­
ternal controls are supported by automated audit trails and risk dash­
boards; disclosure and transparency depend on data governance and
information integrity processes; and board oversight of financial risk
presupposes a minimum level of digital literacy and IT governance
maturity. Conversely, IT governance only generates value when it in­
tegrates and aligns with financial accountability and strategic control
structures. Understanding institutional performance in digital environ­
ments therefore calls for a synergistic perspective that treats financial
and IT governance not as separate silos, but as mutually reinforcing
components of an integrated governance architecture.
This paper responds to that need by conducting a systematic review
of Scopus-indexed empirical studies published between 2015 and 2024
that examine relationships between governance mechanisms and insti­
tutional performance across financial, corporate, and IT domains.
Applying Cronin's five-step review framework and PRISMA 2020
guidelines, we identify and synthesize 46 empirical studies spanning
four governance performance configurations: (i) corporate/financial
governance and corporate performance, (ii) corporate/financial gover­
nance and financial performance, (iii) IT governance and corporate
performance, and (iv) IT governance and financial performance.
Therefore, guided by this evidence base, the study addresses the
following research questions.
RQ1: What mechanisms enable the alignment between financial
governance and IT governance within organizational settings?
RQ2: How does the alignment of these governance domains influ­
ence institutional performance?
Answering RQ1, we consolidate dispersed findings into a unified set
of governance mechanism families that jointly link financial and IT
governance: board and ownership architecture, internal-control and risk
structures, IT governance and digital capability mechanisms, and cross-
domain configurations that integrate financial, corporate, and techno­
logical oversight. Addressing RQ2, we map how these mechanisms
relate to financial performance (e.g. ROA, ROE, Tobin's Q, liquidity and
capital adequacy), broader institutional or corporate performance (e.g.
firm value, innovation, efficiency, strategic use of IT), and IT-related
performance (e.g. IT maturity, agility, digital capabilities). Building on
this synthesis, the paper's main contribution is the Synergistic Gover­
nance Mechanism Model (SGMM). The model organizes the empirical
evidence into five interlocking clusters structural oversight, risk and
compliance, information integrity, alignment and strategic integration,
and dynamic capability that together describe how financial and IT
governance mechanisms are configured to support institutional perfor­
mance in digitally intensive environments. In doing so, the study (i)
integrates previously disconnected literatures on financial/corporate
governance and IT governance; (ii) specifies the mechanism level
foundations of synergistic governance; and (iii) identifies underexplored
areas and directions for future research, particularly concerning data-
governance mechanisms, digital risk controls, and context-dependent
ownership and leadership configurations.

future research.
2. Literature review
2.1. Financial governance in digitally intensive environments
Financial governance is typically defined as the set of structures,
processes, and control mechanisms that safeguard financial trans­
parency, the reliability of reporting, and accountability to stakeholders
within modern economic systems (Germain, 2010; Grabel, 2017; Weiss,
2000). In corporate-governance research, this has been operationalized
through board structures (size, independence, diversity), ownership
configurations, audit and risk committees, disclosure practices, and
composite corporate-governance indices, and then linked to accounting-
and market-based performance indicators such as ROA, ROE, Tobin's Q,
leverage, and capital adequacy (Almoneef & Samontaray, 2019; Bui &
Krajcsák, 2024; Dao & Nguyen, 2020; Fekadu, 2015; Haldar & Rao,
2015; Hamdan et al., 2019; Zarefar & Narsa, 2023).
Across a broad range of sectors and institutional contexts, these
mechanisms are generally associated with improved financial perfor­
mance, consistent with agency theoretic and resource dependence ar­
guments that stress monitoring, expertise, and information access (Bui &
Krajcsák, 2024; Dao & Nguyen, 2020; Jensen & Meckling, 1976; Pfeffer
& Salancik, 1978). At the same time, the evidence is not uniformly
positive: concentrated ownership, CEO duality, and certain board con­
figurations often weaken or neutralize the performance effects of formal
governance structures, especially in emerging markets and
family-dominated or bank-based systems (Al-Matari, 2022; Alabdullah
et al., 2022; Almoneef & Samontaray, 2019; Benvenuto et al., 2021;
Zuniga-Jara & Sjoberg-Tapia, 2021). This suggests that the effectiveness
of financial governance mechanisms is contingent on broader institu­
tional conditions rather than purely their formal design (Giovanna &
Lucio, 2012; Gulcemal, 2021).
What is increasingly clear from recent work is that financial gover­
nance no longer operates on a purely “financial” substrate. Financial
reporting, internal control, and risk management processes are now
embedded in ERP systems, data warehouses, automated audit trails, and
digital disclosure platforms. COSO's Internal Control Integrated Frame­
work and Enterprise Risk Management guidance explicitly recognize
information systems, IT general controls, and technology-dependent
control activities as core components of internal control over financial
reporting (Riva & Provasi, 2015). In practice, key financial governance

2.2. IT governance and its boundaries with financial governance
In parallel, the IT-governance literature has largely developed within
the information systems and technology management domains. It ex­
amines how structures such as IT steering committees, CIO/CTO roles,
board-level IT expertise, and formal IT-governance frameworks shape
the alignment between IT and business strategy, manage technology-
related risks, and enhance information quality, agility, and innovation
(Van Grembergen, 2004; Zhang et al., 2016; Uysal & Çetinkaya, 2021;
Wang et al.,2022). Frameworks suchas COBIT,ITIL,and ISO/IEC 38500
provide guidance on decision rights, control objectives, and principles
for the governance of enterprise IT, emphasizing alignment, value de­
livery, risk management, and resource optimization (Campbell et al.,
2011; Gërvalla, 2018; Radovanović et al., 2011).
Empirical studies generally find that higher IT-governance maturity
and more developed IT capabilities are associated with better opera­
tional performance, greater business–IT alignment, and, in some con­
texts, improved financial outcomes (Uysal & Çetinkaya, 2021; Wamba
et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2022; Yudatama et al., 2019; Zhang et al.,
2016). However, IT-governance research typically conceptualizes “per­
formance” in broad terms (e.g. firm performance, business performance,
IT performance) without systematically distinguishing between finan­
cial performance, corporate/institutional performance, and digital per­
formance. Even when financial metrics are included, they are often
treated as downstream outcomes of IT governance rather than as part of
a mutually reinforcing governance architecture that includes financial
controls, ownership structures, and board-level oversight (Ako-Nai &
Singh, 2019; Uysal & Çetinkaya, 2021; Wamba et al., 2018).
In other words, IT-governance research has tended to adopt a tech­
nology centric lens: financial controls and audit processes are
acknowledged as important, but they are treated as environmental or
contextual variables, not as co-constitutive elements of the governance
system. This mirrors the finance-centric lens of corporate and financial
governance research, where IT is rarely theorized as an explicit gover­
nance mechanism even though it is central to how governance is
implemented in practice (Germain, 2010; Grabel, 2017).
2.3. Why the two streams remained separate and what we lose by keeping
them apart
The persistence of separate financial- and IT-governance literatures
is not accidental. It reflects at least three reinforcing forces.
First, disciplinary origins and audiences differ. Financial gover­
nance research is rooted in corporate finance, accounting, and law,
where agency theory and shareholder value maximization have been
dominant, and where journals focus on board composition, ownership,
and financial disclosure (Bui & Krajcsák, 2024; Dao & Nguyen, 2020;
Jensen & Meckling, 1976). IT-governance research, by contrast,emerges
from information-systems and technology-management communities,
where business IT alignment, IT capabilities, and digital transformation
are central (Uysal & Çetinkaya, 2021; Van Grembergen, 2004; Zhang
et al., 2016). These communities publish in different journals, use
different canonical models, and speak to different practitioner audiences
(boards, regulators, and auditors vs. CIOs, IT managers, and digital
strategists).
Second, measurement traditions and data availability have
driven the fields apart. Financial governance studies rely heavily on
archival data such as board characteristics, ownership structures, and
financial statements, which are readily observable at the firm level and
comparable across large samples (Al-Matari, 2022; Almoneef &
Samontaray, 2019; Dao & Nguyen, 2020; Haldar & Rao, 2015).
IT-governance constructs such as IT decision rights, IT investment pro­
cesses, or dynamic IT capabilities are much harder to observe in sec­
ondary data and often require survey-based, case-based, or
mixed-method designs (Uysal & Çetinkaya, 2021; Vejseli et al., 2022;
Yudatama et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2016). This has encouraged re­
searchers to model one domain at a time, rather than the full joint
architecture.
Third, there is a latent theoretical tension between the dominant
logics of the two fields. Financial governance emphasizes control,
constraint, and the reduction of agency costs through formal structures
and monitoring mechanisms (Acharya et al., 2011; Baker, 2010; Jensen
& Meckling, 1976), whereas IT-governance and dynamic-capability
perspectives highlight agility, experimentation, and the ability to
reconfigure resources in response to technological change (Teece et al.,
1997; Van Grembergen, 2004; Vejseli et al., 2022). This conceptual split
frames financial governance as a mechanism for discipline and stability,
while IT governance is framed as a mechanism for flexibility and
innovation.
Maintaining this separation has at least three analytical
consequences.
1. Invisible complementarities. We do not systematically model how
financial governance mechanisms depend on IT governance to
function. Internal control systems and disclosure quality, for
example, increasingly rely on IT-enabled audit trails, data-validation
routines, cybersecurity controls, and data-governance processes
(Dittenhofer, 1995; Riva & Provasi, 2015; Wamba et al., 2018; Wang
et al., 2022). If these IT mechanisms fail, the quality of financial
governance is compromised, regardless of formal board structures.
2. Under-specified performance mechanisms. Studies that link IT
governance to “firm performance” often do not distinguish whether
improvements are driven by financial outcomes (e.g. profitability,
risk profile), institutional outcomes (e.g. market value, innovation,
productivity), or IT-specific outcomes (e.g. IT agility, digital capa­
bility) (Uysal & Çetinkaya, 2021; Wang et al., 2022; Zhang et al.,
2016). Conversely, financial-governance studies rarely model the
IT-enabled pathways such as analytics capability, automation, or
digital risk management through which governance is translated into
performance (Alabdullah et al., 2022; Zarefar & Narsa, 2023; Zuni­
ga-Jara & Sjoberg-Tapia, 2021).
3. Limited guidance for practice in digitally intensive sectors.
Boards and regulators in banking, financial services, and other data-
intensive industries increasingly face governance questions that cut
across financial and IT domains for example, the integration of
board-level IT expertise into audit committees, the embedding of
data-governance controls into financial governance codes, or the
alignment of IT investment decisions with risk-weighted capital re­
quirements (Alkazali et al., 2021; Benvenuto et al., 2021; Wamba
et al., 2018). A literature that treats these domains separately offers
limited conceptual guidance on how to design and assess such inte­
grated governance architectures.
These blind spots motivate a shift from parallel literatures to an
explicitly synergistic governance perspective that takes the joint
configuration of financial governance and IT-governance mechanisms as
the central unit of analysis.

2.4. Re-examining COBIT, ISO/IEC 38500, and COSO through a
synergistic lens
The separation between financial and IT governance in academic
research stands in contrast to the increasing convergence suggested by
leading governance frameworks. COSO's internal control and enterprise
risk management frameworks, COBIT's IT-governance guidance, and
ISO/IEC 38500's principles for the governance of IT all recognize, in
different ways, that technology and financial oversight are intertwined
(Campbell et al., 2011; Gërvalla, 2018; Radovanović et al., 2011; Riva &
Provasi, 2015). However, a closer, more critical reading reveals that
these frameworks still fall short of providing a genuinely synergistic
view. COSO centers on internal control over financial reporting and
enterprise risk management, defining components such as the control
environment, risk assessment, control activities, information and
communication, and monitoring (Riva & Provasi, 2015). While recent
iterations acknowledge IT controls and information systems as critical
components, IT often appears as a supporting layer an “IT environment”
that must be controlled so that financial reporting can be trusted rather
than as a co-equal governance domain with its own structures (e.g. IT
steering committees, CIO reporting lines) that interact with board and
audit mechanisms. As a result, COSO-based empirical studies usually
operationalize IT only indirectly (e.g. as the presence of ERP systems or
IT internal controls), without considering how specific IT-governance
choices condition the effectiveness of financial controls (Dittenhofer,
1995; Holloway, 2023). COBIT and ISO/IEC 38500, by contrast, start
from the IT side. COBIT is designed to ensure that IT is aligned with
enterprise goals, delivers value, manages risk, and optimizes resources,
thereby positioning itself as an overarching framework for the gover­
nance and management of enterprise IT (Radovanović et al., 2011; Van
Grembergen, 2004). ISO/IEC 38500 defines high-level principles for the
governance of IT as a domain of organizational governance, empha­
sizing responsibility, strategy, acquisition, performance, conformance,
and human behavior (Campbell et al., 2011; ISO, 2015/2024). Both
frameworks recognize the need to connect IT decisions to organizational
strategy and compliance requirements, and COBIT explicitly acknowl­
edges links to COSO-style risk and control structures (Barafort, 2002;
Radovanović et al., 2011).
From a synergistic governance viewpoint, the limitation of these
frameworks is not that they ignore the other domain altogether, but that
they provide parallel, only loosely coupled templates. COSO tells
boards how to assure internal control and risk management; COBIT and
ISO/IEC 38500 tell CIOs and IT leaders how to assure IT value and risk
management. What they do not specify is how board composition, audit-
committee design, ownership structures, IT steering committees, data-
governance controls, and IT-enabled dynamic capabilities should be
jointly configured to support financial, institutional, and digital per­
formance outcomes. Several of the empirical studies in our review
implicitly combine elements from these frameworks for example, by
examining the interaction between corporate governanceindices and IT-
governance maturity, or between board IT expertise and traditional
financial-governance structures (Uysal & Çetinkaya, 2021; Vejseli et al.,
2022; Wamba et al., 2018; Zhang et al., 2016). Yet, because the un­
derlying theoretical framing remains either
financial-governance-centric or IT-governance-centric, these studies
have not been interpreted as evidence for a more general
synergistic-governance architecture. Interpreting them through such a
lens enables us to derive a mechanism-based model the Synergistic
Governance Mechanism Model (SGMM) that explicitly maps how
structural oversight, risk and compliance, information integrity, align­
ment and strategic integration, and dynamic capabilities cut across
financial and IT-governance domains.
2.5. Theoretical foundations and tensions underpinning synergistic
governance
The synergistic governance perspective developed in this paper rests
on three main theoretical pillars.
First, agency theory views governance structures boards, audit
committees, disclosure mechanisms, and ownership configurations as
instruments to reduce information asymmetry and align managerial
behavior with shareholders’ interests (Bhattacharya, 1979; Dao &
Nguyen, 2020; Jensen & Meckling, 1976). From this standpoint,
IT-enabled controls (e.g. automated audit trails, real-time risk dash­
boards, and data-governance routines) are not merely technical artifacts
but extensions of monitoring capability, strengthening the ability of
financial governance to detect misreporting, fraud, or excessive
risk-taking (Dittenhofer, 1995; Riva & Provasi, 2015; Wamba et al.,
2018).
Second, resource dependence theory emphasizes the role of boards
and governance structures in providing access to critical resources,
knowledge, and external linkages (Pfeffer & Salancik, 1978).
Board-level IT expertise, CIO participation in top management, and IT
steering committees represent governance mechanisms that bring digi­
tal knowledge and external technology networks into the core of
financial decision making, thereby shaping how institutions interpret
technological trends and benchmark IT investments (Van Grembergen,
2004; Vejseli et al., 2022; Yudatama et al., 2019).
Third, dynamic capability and IT-capability perspectives high­
light the ability of organisations to sense, seize, and reconfigure re­
sources in response to technological and environmental change (Teece
et al., 1997; Vejseli et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2022). IT-governance
mechanisms, when effectively designed, underpin IT-enabled dynamic
capabilities that allow institutions to adapt their financial controls,
risk-management processes, and performance-measurement systems as
digital technologies evolve (Uysal & Çetinkaya, 2021; Wamba et al.,
2018; Zhang et al., 2016).
Bringing these perspectives together surfaces several theoretical
tensions that the existing literature has not systematically addressed.
• A tension between control and agility: Agency theory stresses the
need for strong control and monitoring, while dynamic capability
theory stresses flexibility and experimentation (Jensen & Meckling,
1976; Teece et al., 1997; Vejseli et al., 2022). Synergistic governance
must reconcile the need for robust financial controls with the need
for rapid IT-enabled adaptation.
• A tension between compliance and innovation: Regulatory and
compliance frameworks (e.g. COSO) emphasize standardization and
risk avoidance, whereas IT-governance frameworks and digital-
transformation agendas emphasize innovation, new business
models, and experimentation with data-intensive technologies (Riva
& Provasi, 2015; Van Grembergen, 2004; Wamba et al., 2018).
• A tension between centralized and distributed governance:
Financial governance tends to centralize decision rights at the board
and top-management levels, whereas effective IT governance often
requires distributed decision-making and collaboration across busi­
ness units, IT, and risk functions (Uysal & Çetinkaya, 2021; Van
Grembergen, 2004; Zhang et al., 2016).
• A tension between static and dynamic measures of governance
quality: Traditional governance indices are largely structural and
static (e.g. board size, independence, number of committees),
whereas IT-enabled capabilities and dynamic capabilities are inher­
ently process-oriented and evolving (Bui & Krajcsák, 2024; Dao &
Nguyen, 2020; Teece et al., 1997; Vejseli et al., 2022).
The Synergistic Governance Mechanism Model (SGMM) proposed in
this study is designed to systematize how organisations navigate these
tensions. By organizing empirical findings into five interlocking clusters
structural oversight, risk and compliance, information integrity,

3. Research methodology
This study employs a systematic literature review with mixed bib­
liometric and qualitative synthesis, drawing upon Scopus-indexed pub­
lications within a defined time window. The methodological process
follows Cronin's five-step review framework and uses the PRISMA 2020
statement (Page et al., 2021) as a reporting guideline for study identi­
fication, screening, eligibility, and inclusion. PRISMA items that are
specific to intervention studies and quantitative meta-analyses (e.g.
effect-size synthesis, clinical risk-of-bias tools) are not applicable to our
conceptual and bibliometric design. This study employs a systematic
literature review approach, drawing upon Scopus-indexed publications
published between 2015 and 2024. The methodological process follows
Cronin's five-step review framework and is aligned, where applicable,
with the updated PRISMA 2020 guidelines (Page et al., 2021) to enhance
transparency and replicability. The initial search generated 2804 re­
cords, which were filtered through successive screening and eligibility
stages until a final set of 46 studies was retained for structured quali­
tative and bibliometric synthesis. Given the heterogeneity of research
designs, measures, and effect-size reporting across the 46 studies, we did
not conduct a formal statistical meta-analysis. Instead, we adopted a
concept-centric and mechanism-based narrative synthesis that is widely
recommended for complex governance and management phenomena
where quantitative pooling is not feasible but theory-building from
diverse evidence is possible.
Inclusion and exclusion criteria were explicitly defined to ensure
methodological rigor. Studies were included if they: (1) examined at
least two variables capturing the governance–performance nexus, (2)
provided empirical evidence rather than purely conceptual discussions,
and (3) were published in peer-reviewed journals indexed in Scopus
during the 2015 and 2024 period. Studies were excluded if they: (1) did
not establish a direct link between financial or IT governance and
institutional performance, (2) relied solely onsingle-variable descriptive
analyses, or (3) consisted of editorials, commentaries, or duplicate re­
cords. This multi-stage filtering process produced the final dataset of 46
studies.
The bibliometric indicators (keyword frequencies, TF-IDF, and co-
occurrence patterns) were therefore not interpreted in isolation but
were used to triangulate the qualitative reading of the 46 studies. In
particular, the clustering of keywords around board structures, risk and
compliance, information quality, alignment, and IT capabilities
informed the five mechanism clusters that are formalized in the Syner­
gistic Governance Mechanism Model (SGMM). The performance results
emerging in the 46 empirical studies show that governance alignment,
in terms of financial, corporate, and IT governance structures and sys­
tems, correlates with improved financial performance (FP) and corpo­
rate performance (CP). Referring to Table 3, the performance measures
applied in the studies include a large variance, ranging from accounting
measures like ROA and ROE, and including market measures like Tobin's
Q and Market Value, risk and stability measures like Liquidity and
Capital Adequacy, and extending to institutional-level performance
measures like Innovation Investments, Operational Efficiency, Strategic
Use of IT, and Institutional Maturity on IT.
The literature search was conducted exclusively in the Scopus
database, one of the most comprehensive and authoritative sources for
peer-reviewed academic publications. Scopus was selected because it
provides consistent indexing standards, unified subject classifications,
and broad international coverage, ensuring replicability. Additionally,
Scopus covers the majority of journals that also appear in the Web of
Science Core Collection, which reduces the risk of excluding key studies.
Nevertheless, future updates of the review may incorporate Web of
Science to expand the evidence base if newer studies warrant inclusion.
In line with this, we now explicitly report Scopus as the primary source
of evidence and treat the omission of additional databases such as Web
of Science as a boundary condition for the generalizability of our find­
ings rather than a claim of exhaustive coverage.
A structured query was developed to identify studies explicitly
addressing the intersection of financial governance, IT governance, and
institutional performance. The search string combined multiple

4.2. Governance mechanisms enabling alignment between financial and
IT governance (RQ1)
To identify how financial governance and IT governance become
aligned within organizational settings, this review synthesizes evidence
from the 46 included studies into a unified set of governance mecha­
nisms. Rather than treating governance as a set of isolated structural
components, the findings reveal that alignment emerges through the
interaction of four interconnected mechanism families: (i) board-level
and ownership architecture, (ii) internal-control, disclosure, and risk-
management structures, (iii) IT governance and digital-capability
mechanisms, and (iv) cross-domain governance configurations that
integrate financial, corporate, and technological oversight within the
same system. Table 4 consolidates these mechanisms and their dominant
performance effects.
The integrated evidence has revealed that the alignment in gover­
nance between the financial and IT areas emerges when the process and
technological aspects come together in a single governance framework.
The characteristics and structure of the board, board independence, and
audit committees constitute the institutional foundation underlying the
financial governance structure, at the same time influencing the related
technological issues. The systems related to the internal controls and
transparency tools such as the CGI, CGQ, ICG index, and the CG
Disclosure Index help create the conditions necessary in the process of
embedding the technological controls, such as audit trails and risk
dashboards.
In addition, other IT governance tools, such as the use of COBIT
frameworks, IT boards, the role of the CIO/CTO, alignment between
business and IT, and dynamic IT capabilities, together with others, help
improve the integrity and responsiveness of the system. These tools in­
crease the effectiveness of the financial system in a digital setting.
Perhaps the strongest indication of synergy in the domain-spanning
studies appears in the findings on the use of corporate governance
controls together with either board-level IT knowledge, ITG, and other
related controls, leading to better outcomes.
In light of the evidence, the first research question RQ1, which
sought evidence on what the alignment between financial governance
and IT governance enables, has been adequately addressed. The evi­
dence revealed that the alignment process between financial governance
and IT governance enables multi-layered mechanisms, and that risk
management, transparency, strategic alignment, and technology-
enabled performance are all improved.
4.3. Effects of governance alignment on institutional performance (RQ2)
The performance results emerging from the 46 empirical studies
conducted highlight the fact that alignment in governance, whether in
terms of board design, internal controls, IT governance, and integration
across various governance areas, has added dimensions in terms of
impacting positively corporate performance in general, and specifically
in terms of both financial performance (FP) and corporate performance
(CP). The performance measures studied in the various studies scruti­
nized, and their primary empirical contributions, are listed in Table 5.
The bibliometric indicators (keyword frequencies, TF-IDF, and co-
occurrence patterns) were therefore not interpreted in isolation but
were used to triangulate the qualitative reading of the 46 studies. In
particular, the clustering of keywords around board structures, risk and
compliance, information quality, alignment, and IT capabilities
informed the five mechanism clusters that are formalized in the Syner­
gistic Governance Mechanism Model (SGMM). The performance results
emerging in the 46 empirical studies show that governance alignment,
in terms of financial, corporate, and IT governance structures and sys­
tems, correlates with improved financial performance (FP) and corpo­
rate performance (CP). Referring to Table 3, the performance measures
applied in the studies include a large variance, ranging from accounting
measures like ROA and ROE, and including market measures like Tobin's
Q and Market Value, risk and stability measures like Liquidity and
Capital Adequacy, and extending to institutional-level performance
measures like Innovation Investments, Operational Efficiency, Strategic
Use of IT, and Institutional Maturity on IT.
In various environments in Malaysia, India, GCC, Romania, Italy,
Indonesia, and other emerging economies, the better governance
structures like properly constituted boards, better governance quality
and transparency indices (CGI, CGQ, ICG, CGdis), proactive audit and
control committees, and stricter ownership structures are invariably
linked with improved financial performance. The positive relationship
between governance structures and financial performance is manifest in
terms of increased profitability, increased market value, enhanced
liquidity and capital adequacy ratios, and optimal financial structures.
Conversely, some evidence shows mixed results and weakened gover­
nance structures in the presence of family ownership and strong duality
in the CEOs, implying that imbalances in power distribution at the upper
echelons could weaken the effectiveness of better governance structures.
Besides the financial aspects, there are other findings from studies
that highlight the importance of governing in improving broader insti­
tutional capacities. The evidence from the settings in Germany, Brazil,
China, Pakistan, Chile, Ecuador, and the EU corporate world reveals that
the quality of governance and the development of boards and IT
governance structures are important factors in improving the intensity
of innovative investments, the value and stock market value, effective­
ness, and the use of strategic IT. Agile governance, alignment, and IT-
enabled dynamic capacities remain the driving factors in the process
through which the structures of governance are transformed into in­
novations and agility.

The strongest evidence about the synergistic nature of governance
emerges within the framework of studies where IT governance can be
systematically combined with corporate and digital governance frame­
works. By blending knowledge on the IT governance side with the
typical corporate governance controls, institutions experience better
financial and non-financial performance of IT. By the same token, sce­

and corporate governance in the technology realm isn't carried out in a
vacuum, but rather becomes even more effective through better infor­

4.4. Synergistic Governance Mechanism Model (SGMM)
Based on the synthesis of 46 empirical studies, this paper develops
the Synergistic Governance Mechanism Model (SGMM) as an empiri­
cally informed conceptual framework. The model does not report a new,
statistically estimated structural or causal model; instead, it organizes
recurring combinations of governance mechanisms and performance
outcomes identified across the evidence base into five interrelated
clusters. SGMM is therefore proposed as a mid-range, mechanism-based
theory that integrates existing findings on financial and IT governance
and formulates propositions to be examined in future empirical work.
Based on the synthesis of 46 empirical studies, this paper has proposed
the Synergistic Governance Mechanism Model (SGMM) that conceptu­
alizes the workings of financial governance and IT governance in five
interlocking sets of mechanisms. Contrary to the treatment of financial
and IT governance as distinct governance fields, the evidence has indi­
cated the integration process through an organized framework of
oversight, compliance, information integrity, alignment, and capability-
building activities. Five sets of mechanisms have been outlined in the
model, and they include
(1) The Structural Oversight Mechanisms include board structure
and independence, audit and control committees, institutional
ownership, and executive oversight. These are the managerial
structures responsible for the formal governance architecture, in
which the accountability in finance and oversight in technology
are realized.
(2) Risk & Compliance These include risk and compliance frame­
works that identify governance indices such as the CGI, CGQ, and
ICG, risk and compliance frameworks that identify regulatory
structures such as the SR, RF, and TD, and risk and compliance
frameworks that identify conditions in the macro-governance
environment such as the IQ, CORR, and POLST.
(3) Information-Integrity Mechanisms involving disclosure and
transparency (CGdisc), data governance controls, reliability in­
dicators (AIC, TRB), and IT audit trails. These ensure consistency
in data, accuracy in reporting, and information integrity condi­
tions in terms of alignment between financial controls and IT
governance.
(4) Alignment & Strategic Integration Alignment and integration
mechanisms related to the structures of alignment and integra­
tion between business and IT in an organization (STR, PRC, REL,
A-STR, A-PRC, A-REL), boards' IT knowledge, ITG frameworks in
relation to the use of the COBIT framework and strategic use of IT
in an organization (STR-IT).
(5) Dynamic Capabilities, categorized under Dynamic Capability
Mechanisms, include IT-enabled dynamic capabilities, agility
processes, innovation paths, and performance mediation struc­
tures. These dynamic capability mechanisms align governance,
facilitating adaptive, innovative, and value-creating
performance.
Throughout the evidence base, the five factors work together as an
overall synergy system that combines financial governance and IT
governance. Together, they support and sustain transparency, risk
management, data quality, and strategic alignment. With the structure
illustrated in Fig. 3, the collective power of financial and information
technology governance drives the combined impact on the performance
related to finance (ROA, ROE, and Tobin's Q) and the corporation, and
then the performance on information technology (agility, dependability,
and performance). The SGMM provides proof and knowledge about the
process aspects related to governance alignment. The five clusters are
therefore not proposed as an abstract checklist but as an abstraction of
recurring configurations observed across the 46 studies, including cases
where particular mechanisms fail in the absence of complementary el­
ements from other clusters (e.g. strong structural oversight without
reliable IT-based information, or advanced IT capabilities without basic
risk and compliance controls)

5. Discussion
The integration of results from the 46 studies included in the evi­
dence base confirms the cumulative and distinct role of the alignment
between financial governanceand IT governance and provides empirical
evidence supporting the synergistic governance approach. In their
broader setting, the evidence confirms the continued significant and
positive role in financial performance played by the classical structures
related to financial governance, such as board composition, audit and
control committees, governance indices, ownership structure, and
quality of disclosure, in line with what has been reported in the corpo­
rate governance literature in general (Benvenuto et al., 2021; Garad
et al., 2021). Nevertheless, the present review also confirms that all
those structures are progressively also relying on the role provided by
the digital infrastructure and the related IT-control systems.
The patterns reported in Tables 4 and 5 show that positive outcomes
are consistently associated with combinations of clusters for instance,
structural board mechanisms together with risk and compliance
frameworks and IT-based information integrity rather than with any
single governance mechanism in isolation. The first cross-study pattern
reveals that the factors of board structure (BS), board independence
(IND), and the quality of governance measures (GQ, CGdisc, ICG) sys­
tematically and positively relate to ROA, ROE, and Tobin's Q in
Malaysia, Vietnam, Romania, Italy, and the MENA countries. These
findings are consistent with the frameworks proposed by the agency and
resource dependence theory, where the primary factors in reducing in­
formation asymmetry and improving decision-making quality are
monitoring intensity and the application of different forms and levels of
expertise. However, evidence from technology-intensive settings con­
firms that the effectiveness of the first-mentioned factors, set forth in the
frameworks, can be optimized when combined with the use of IT
governance in a way that aims to guarantee the quality and timeliness of
the digital information available for financial systems. In other words,
financial governance relies on IT governance.
The other pattern revolves around ownership structures. The
convergence on the negative impact on family ownership (FAMOWN-),
foreign ownership (FO-), and managerial ownership (MGO-) across
Indonesia, Pakistan, and Bangladesh provides evidence that a lack in
monitoring structures and transparency results from concentrated
ownership structures. The role of this review lies in the observation that
these flaws can now be exacerbated in the digital space, where the
alignment on financial controls and oversight, monitoring, and IT in
firms with concentrated ownership structures yields performance vari­
ability. The institutional ownership structure (INSTOWN+) and
governance-efficiency factors (GEFF+) always have uniformly positive
affects because they instill disciplined monitoring structures in the
framework, ensuring the institutionalization process of IT-governance.
The most prominent evidence on synergistic governance has been
observed in studies where IT governance has been explicitly combined
with financial and corporate governance models. Al Romaihi et al., in
2023 confirmed the positive impact on ROA and ROE ratios, where
corporate governance controls become stronger with the effect brought
by the role of IT knowledge on the company board. Vejseli et al., in 2022
argued that both general and agile models bring performance
improvement through alignment, and their work supported the model
focusing on alignment ability. Similarly, Ilmudeen in 2022 has also
added evidence in favor of synergistic governance, where IT-enabled
dynamic capabilities have played an important role in improving per­
formance and agility.
Across the 46 studies, these clusters do not appear as isolated ele­
ments but as configurations that co-occur in practice. For example,
several banking and corporate-governance studies report that indepen­
dent boards, active audit committees, and ownership structures (struc­
tural oversight) are associated with better financial outcomes only when
combined with robust internal-control and risk-management frame­
works (risk & compliance) that are supported by reliable, IT-enabled
reporting and disclosure (information integrity). In higher education
and service organisations, improved institutional performance and
innovation are observed where IT and business strategies are aligned
(alignment & strategic integration) and where organisations have
developed IT-enabled agility and innovation capabilities (dynamic ca­
pabilities) on top of basic financial and information controls. These
cross-study patterns underpin the grouping of mechanisms into the five
SGMM clusters and the claim that the clusters work synergistically
rather than independently.
There are also mixed and context-dependent results. The negative
and insignificant coefficients on CEO duality (CEOD) in Malaysia,
Vietnam, Bangladesh, and other countries like Ethiopia and Pakistan
again and again reveal that having power in the leadership affects the
relationship between financial governance and the IT decision process.
The results on board independence (IND) and gender diversity in the
boardroom (GDIV) reveal positive and insignificant coefficients,
implying that the governance system has limitations in affecting per­
formance and hence needs an institutionalized digital control system.

Together, the findings suggest that the optimal performance of in­
stitutions arises when financial governance and IT governance operate
in a reciprocal subsystem relationship. While financial governance en­
ables the concept of accountability, monitoring, and transparency, IT
governance establishes the technological platform necessary to imple­
ment these in complex technological settings. When combined, their
dynamic interplay has the ability to manage risk, protect the integrity of
information, and create innovative outcomes beyond the capacities of
either domain.
Significantly, these conclusions are synthesized in the Synergistic
Governance Mechanism Model proposed in this paper, grouping the
evidence into five thematic clusters related specifically to governance
alignment, namely, Structural Oversight, Risk and Compliance, Infor­
mation Integrity, Strategic Alignment, and Dynamic Capabilities. In ef­
fect, the proposed model provides an organized platform on which
governance alignment theory and related studies could be further
advanced.
5.1. Conflicting findings and boundary conditions
Although the majority of studies in the corpus report positive asso­
ciations between governance mechanisms and performance outcomes,
the evidence is far from uniform. Several studies find non-significant or
mixed effects for specific mechanisms such as board size, board inde­
pendence, and certain ownership structures, particularly in emerging
markets or in firms with concentrated ownership. In bank-based sys­
tems, for example, the presence of formal auditand risk committeesdoes
not always translate into superior performance when enforcement is
weak or when controlling shareholders can override board decisions.
Conversely, in more strongly regulated or market-based environments,
similar structures are more consistently associated with improved
financial performance.
Mixed findings are also observed for IT-governance mechanisms.
While higher IT-governance maturity and IT-enabled capabilities are
generally linked to better financial or institutional performance, this
relationship weakens or disappears in contexts where information
integrity is low or where IT is not effectively integrated into core
governance processes. In such cases, investments in IT governance and
digital capabilities may increase complexity and risk without delivering
commensurate benefits. Taken together, these divergent results indicate
that the effects of governance mechanisms are contingent on boundary
conditions such as regulatory quality, enforcement, ownership concen­
tration, sectoral characteristics, and the maturity of IT and data in­
frastructures. The SGMM therefore does not assume that each cluster has
universally positive effects. Rather, it conceptualizes how structural
oversight, risk and compliance, information integrity, alignment and
strategic integration, and dynamic capabilities must be configured and
balanced under different contextual conditions to support financial,
institutional, and digital performance. While Tables 4 and 5 report the
distribution of positive, mixed, and non-significant findings, our inter­
pretation goes beyond simple frequency counts. The pattern that
emerges is that similar governance mechanisms can have different ef­
fects depending on institutional context, regulatory environment,
ownership structure, and the presence or absence of IT-related controls
and capabilities. For instance, independent boards and audit committees
tend to be associated with better financial performance in strongly
regulated settings, but their effect is attenuated when information sys­
tems are weak or when ownership is highly concentrated. Similarly, IT-
governance mechanisms and digital capabilities appear to reinforce
governance outcomes primarily when they are embedded in robust risk
and compliance structures and supported by high information integrity.
These observations inform the configuration logic of the SGMM clusters
discussed in Section 3.4.
However, the future empirical testing of the Synergistic Governance
Mechanism Model (SGMM). The Synergistic Governance Mechanism
Model (SGMM) developed in this paper is an empirically informed
conceptual framework rather than a statistically estimated structural
model. It synthesizes recurring configurations of governance mecha­
nisms observed across 46 studies into five interrelated clusters: struc­
tural oversight, risk and compliance, information integrity, alignment
and strategic integration, and dynamic capabilities. Future research is
therefore needed to subject the SGMM to systematic empirical testing.
First, survey- or archival-based studies could operationalize the five
clusters as latent constructs and examine their direct and interaction
effects on financial, institutional, and digital performance outcomes
using structural equation modelling or related multivariate techniques.
This would allow researchers to test whether configurations predicted
by SGMM (e.g. structural oversight combined with risk and compliance
and information integrity) are empirically associated with superior
performance compared with partial or unbalanced configurations. Sec­
ond, configurational approaches such as fuzzy-set qualitative compar­
ative analysis (fsQCA) or coincidence analysis could be used to identify
combinations of governance mechanisms that are sufficient or necessary
for high performance in different institutional contexts. Such designs
would be particularly well suited to assess whether multiple “equifinal”
governance configurations, consistent with SGMM, can lead to similar
financial or institutional outcomes across countries, sectors, or owner­
ship structures. Third, longitudinal and mixed-method designs could
explore how the five clusters co-evolve over time as organisations un­
dergo digital transformation, regulatory change, or crisis. Case-based
and process-tracing studies in banks, universities, and other data-
intensive organisations would deepen understanding of how structural
oversight, risk controls, information integrity, alignment, and dynamic
capabilities are practicallyintegrated, adapted, ordecoupled in response
to shocks. By outlining these avenues, the paper positionsSGMM not as a
final, fully validated model, but as a mechanism-based framework that
can guide subsequent empirical testing of how financial and IT gover­
nance interact to influence performance in digitally intensive
environments.
6. Conclusion
In this review, evidence from 46 studies has been synthesized sys­
tematically, focusing on the relationship and alignment between finan­
cial governance and IT governance, and the resulting impact on
performance. The results reveal that neither financial governance nor IT
governance works properly on its own, and performance optimization
can be realized when both systems work in an interconnected fashion.
While aspects like the company board, audit and control committees,
ownership, and governance indices in financial governance create con­
ditions concerning accountability and reporting quality, the application
of structures like the COBIT framework, the company board, alignment
tools, and dynamic IT capabilities in IT governance provides assurance
related to the quality and integrity of information. The evidence
together confirms that their alignment enables the optimal performance
of the organization in terms of finance and technology by making
monitoring better, reducing asymmetry related to information, aligning
strategy, and improving innovations and agility. The Synergistic
Governance Mechanism Model proposed in this review integrates all the
aspects under five different mechanism sets, and the model provides a

6.1. Practical implications
The results in this review form the basis for clear evidence-informed
advice on improving the effectiveness of governance in digitally inten­
sive organisations.
− First, boards need to reorganize their governance structures in such a
way that they include technology experts in their audit and risk
committees, since technology knowledge on the board has been
proven to improve the effectiveness of financial controls and the
company's performance.
− Second, organisations need an integrated financial and IT control
structure. This would involve the integration of IT-enabled audit
trails, data-validation checks, and quality disclosure systems within
the financial reporting process. There has now been evidence that
mechanisms supporting the integrity of information are essential in
ensuring financial transparency.
− Third, the five-cluster Synergistic Governance Mechanism Model
(SGMM) provides a useful roadmap within the framework of
governance codes, and the demands on financial governance can be
strengthened through the enactment of ITG practices, digital risk
assessment, and the disclosure of IT competency on the boards in
technology-dependent sectors where the financial domain relies on
technology.
− Fourth, aligning the governance and management structures by
setting up a corporate governance committee and an asset manage­
ment committee, and ensuring alignment in the company's executive
management structure, would help in aligning the company's tech­
nology structure.
− Fifth, the organisations functioning in the emerging markets need an
integrated training session in which they should include their
financial controllers, IT managers, and internal auditors together, so
they can understand each other in relation to the risks and gover­
nance demands posed by technology.
6.2. Theoretical implications
The conclusions and results in the present review make the following
contributions in the theory of governance, particularly in understanding
the relationship and interplay between financial governance and IT
governance.
− First, the synthesis pushes forward the field of corporate governance
studies because it shows that classic corporate governance structures
like the board, the audit committee, the quality of corporate
disclosure, and ownership structure rely, in part, on the possession of
IT-governance capacities. This approach develops the theory of
agency and the theory of resource dependence because it shows that
the effectiveness of monitoring in high-technology sectors is
contingent not only on board composition and monitoring process,
but also on the technology capacities available in the organisation.
− Second, the review helps in IT governance theory development by
showing the evidence that IT governance has evolved from focusing
on the value and risk aspects of technology to becoming an important
factor in influencing the quality of financial reporting, risk, and
controls. Models, board-level IT knowledge, and alignment in IT
strategies help highlight the importance of considering IT gover­
nance within the overall structure and framework of organisational
governance, and hence, focusing on it from an organisational
perspective.
− Third, the integration approach adopted in this thesis enables the
amalgamation of the findings from the 46 studies, supporting the
development of a “synergistic governance” perspective, in which
financial and IT governance factors together play a part in
determining performance through alignment, information integrity,
and dynamic capabilities. This builds on the earlier models on
financial and IT governance and performance by the integration of
the multi-layered perspective on the governance and performance
relationship in the Synergistic Governance Mechanism Model.
− Fourth, the review discusses the mediator role of alignment in the
governance-IT domain. The contribution made here is the demon­
stration that improvements in performance are, in fact, mediated by
increased agility, innovation, and alignment. This latter contribution
specifically enriches the field's knowledge on the nature and process
of capability development and digital transformation, by introducing
alignment as an antecedent condition.
− Fifth, the cross-contextual evidence underlines the contingent role in
governance alignment. Different levels and forms of ownership
concentration, regulatory factors, and the extent of digital maturity
create conditions under which either synergies or contradictions in
the alignment between financial governance and IT governance
appear. Thus, there arises the need for the deployment of contextual
moderators in governance theory, particularly in emerging markets.
6.3. Future research directions
The evidence synthesis has uncovered some evidence gaps that
highlight interesting avenues of work in developing the theory and
practices concerning governance alignment.
− First, the dominant studies in the field remain the use of cross-
sectional designs, which make it difficult to capture the co-
evolution process in relation to financial governance and IT gover­
nance. Longitudinal studies need to be conducted in order to identify
the dynamic relationship between the different governance struc­
tures, the development of different capabilities, and performance.
− Second, most existing studies on financial governance and IT
governance focus on each type in isolation, even when they co-exist
in the same setting. Future studies need to make use of multi-
dimensional models that take into account the interaction effects,
the mediating process, and the relationship among the structures in a
holistic manner. Models using structural equation models, fsQCA,
and multi-level models would help better understand the synergy
and the process involved.
− Third, the present knowledge base has a strong geographical focus in
the emerging economies in Asia, the Middle East, and Africa.
Comparative studies in different institutional settings like the EU, the
Americas, and the trans-Atlantic space would enable researchers to
examine the contextual contingency of governance alignment and
the role of quality, digitalization, and sophisticated markets in
enabling and constraining different governance models.
− Fourth, there are some SGMM model-defined mechanisms, like data
governance systems, digital risk controls, and IT-enabled audit trails,
that need to be researched further using empirical studies. These
variables need to be measured through objective indices in order to
understand their single and combined impacts on financial integrity,
innovation capacities, and digital performance. Experimental and
quasi-experimental studies could help break causal homogeneity in
such cases.
− Fifth, studies on ownership structures and leadership compositions
(family ownership, foreign ownership, duality, and others) imply
that power concentration has moderating effects on the relationship
between financial and IT governance alignment. Future studies
should explore moderating variables using governance and behav­
ioural theories, focusing on the role of leadership cognition, digital
literacy, and risk-taking propensity in promoting the coordination
and integration of financial and technological controls.
− Sixth, in light of the adoption and integration of holistic enterprise-
wide governance structures, such as the integration framework be­
tween COSO and COBIT, ITIL risk management frameworks, among

6.4. Limitations
This review is subject to several limitations.
− First, the synthesis is restricted to 46 empirical studies that met the
inclusion criteria, which may exclude relevant conceptual or quali­
tative evidence.
− Second, most included studies rely on cross-sectional designs and
heterogeneous measures of governance and performance, limiting
causal interpretation and reducing comparability across contexts.
− Third, the majority of evidence originates from emerging markets,
which may constrain the generalizability of findings to highly digi­
talised or strictly regulated environments.
− Fourth, the review provides high transparency in methodology
through the use of detailed search strategies and the use of inclusion
and exclusion criteria. At the same time, the corpus is restricted to
Scopus-indexed journal articles published within the stated time
frame, so we do not claim to capture every single study on gover­
nance and performance beyond this period or outside this database.
− Finally, the absence of unified models that examine financial and IT
governance simultaneously in primary studies limits the depth of
integration achievable in this review and underscores the frag­
mented nature of the existing literature.
