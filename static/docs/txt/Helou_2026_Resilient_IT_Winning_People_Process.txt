ABSTRACT
In today's dynamic environment, IT organisations face numerous challenges from disruptions such as natural disasters, cyber-
security threats, international conflicts, evolving regulations, pandemics and recent global incidents such as the CrowdStrike
outage. To navigate these complexities and maintain operational continuity, companies must build IT organisational resilience,
ensuring they can adapt, recover swiftly and continue delivering critical services. This study surveyed 157 IT leaders across
various industries to explore how IT service quality and IT governance practices can bolster IT organisational resilience to face
these turbulent events. Our findings indicate that both IT service quality and IT governance maturity are crucial building blocks
of IT organisational resilience. The study shows that during environmental turbulence, leveraging these factors can help miti-
gate impacts and effectively address challenges. We also provide IT leadership with guidance on how these ‘people’ and ‘process’
approaches can be leveraged to build IT organisational resilience.
IT Organisational Resilience—A Practitioner's Dilemma
As the world began emerging from the pandemic in 2021, the IT
team at a midwestern organisation found itself exhausted, over-
worked and depleted—an experience shared by many during
that time. The pandemic had introduced significant uncertainty,
stress, and, in many unfortunate cases, personal grief. According
to the McKinsey Global Survey of executives, the COVID-­ 19 cri-
sis has brought about years of change in the way companies in
all sectors and regions do business (The New Digital Edge:
Rethinking Strategy for the Postpandemic Era, McKinsey and
Company 2021). Initiatives that would typically unfold over sev-
eral months were suddenly executed in a matter of days, with
minimal planning and a high demand for flawless execution.
The intensity and urgency of these tasks contributed to significant
burnout across the department.
Amid this turmoil, an interim CIO was assigned the responsibil-
ity of guiding a highly capable yet fatigued team through an un-
certain period. What soon became apparent was that the end of
the pandemic did not signal the end of turbulent times. The crisis
had permanently altered business strategies, workforce retention
practices, remote work expectations, customer needs and the cy-
bersecurity landscape. Although the peak of the pandemic had
passed, its long-­ term effects had only just begun to surface.
The accelerated adoption of technology during the pandemic
transformed employee and customer expectations. IT depart-
ments were now at the center of meeting these evolving needs.
Leadership recognised the department would need to rebuild its
strength, restore energy and foster IT organisational resilience.
Building resilience began with prioritising people, then processes.
The department needed to become agile, adaptable and equipped
with efficient tools and procedures to face future challenges.
Reenergising the department started with rebuilding a sense
of community and shared purpose. A series of formal IT ser-
vice quality workshops were organised to bring the team to-
gether, as well as customer service skills for both management
and staff. The guiding principle was that resilient departments
are built upon well-­ trained and supported employees. Ongoing
training ensured that staff were prepared to handle adversity
while maintaining a high standard of service. Regular one-­ on-­
one meetings between leadership and staff ensured consistent

1   |   Introduction
As highlighted in the opening vignette, IT organisations deal
with a constantly shifting world. This turbulence was high-
lighted by COVID but is certainly not restricted to just that. IT
organisationsfacearangeofnewchallengesduetoenvironmen-
tal disruptions such as natural disasters, cybersecurity threats,
international conflicts, changing regulations, etc. IT organisa-
tions in the context of this article are defined as a service organ-
isation or IT providers supporting the rest of the organisation's
departments like human resources, finance, etc. Chief informa-
tion officers (CIOs) continue to face the challenge of accelerat-
ing their digital transformation efforts and building resilient IT
departments. As these challenges grow more complex, the ef-
fectiveness of their IT people and IT processes becomes increas-
ingly crucial. Resilience in an IT organisation is forged through
the synergy of people and process; to meet evolving demands,
IT organisational resilience, defined as the capacity to endure
disruptionsandmaintainnormaloperations,isvitalforsurvival
(Signé and Almond 2021).
Based on the CIO's lived experience during the COVID-­ 19
pandemic, IT service quality strengthened the people dimen-
sion in the IT department by fostering staff adaptability, con-
fidence and responsiveness under pressure. In parallel, IT
governance reinforced the process dimension by embedding
clarity, accountability and flexible decision-­ making into or-
ganisational operations. From this perspective, resilient peo-
ple supported by IT service quality and resilient processes
supported by IT governance together form the core mecha-
nisms of IT organisational resilience.
As turbulence continues to challenge companies, a key shift is
unfolding in the role of the CIO and how they view technol-
ogy. As mentioned in Forbes, the leadership teams steering
the course for companies are witnessing the rise of CIOs to
more prominent roles, with their responsibilities expanding
in response to the growing complexity of technology and its
strategic importance (Daoudi 2023). In this era of unpredict-
ability, the spotlight is now on IT functions and their strategic
value, with CIOs at the helm, tasked to chart a new course
forward (Gewald and Wagner 2022). During periods of tur-
bulence, the IT function becomes even more pivotal, tasked
not only with adapting to change but also with leading efforts
to foster resilience. In the dynamic landscape where unprec-
edented challenges shape the narrative, the role of CIOs tran-
scends traditional boundaries asking the question: How can
CIOs build IT organisational resilience to overcome environ-
mental turbulence?
In the face of turbulence, IT organisations cannot simply brace
themselves for every possible disruption; instead, they must
cultivate a dynamic repertoire of capabilities centered around
IT people and IT processes to weather uncertainty and build
IT organisational resilience. As part of nearly all business pro-
cesses in a company, the IT department plays a central role in
building resilience at a company level, which forms an import-
ant strategic advantage (Reeves et al. 2022). Many strategies
have been identified to build organisational resilience such as
having a clear statement of purpose (Eccles et al. 2023), digital
transformation (Browder et al. 2023), entrepreneurial strategic
thinking (Mohammadyari and Sharafi 2024), to state a few. In
this quantitative study we examine how IT service quality and
IT governance, two essential IT capabilities rooted in people
and processes respectively, collaboratively build IT organisa-
tional resilience. These concepts will be developed throughout
the paper.
There is often a bias towards coming up with new strategies
to overcome unexpected turbulence, but in this research, we
focused on reducing complexity and reaffirming that the best
approach is to lean into already established approaches and
tweak them where needed. The contribution of this research
will emphasise how mature ITG practices and IT service qual-
ity practices play a key role in how IT organisations can proac-
tively build resilience to face adversity. Based on our research,
we identify three findings to help organisations navigate these
challenging times.
Finding 1: Environmental Turbulence Fuels IT Service Quality
and IT Governance Maturity.
Finding 2: Mastering IT Governance Drives IT Service Quality.
Finding 3: IT Service Quality and IT Governance Maturity are
two Pillars of IT Organisational Resilience.
The rest of the article will be structured into several sections:
A brief overview of the main concepts, what we did (methods),
findings of the study, recommendations, a practical example of
applying the framework to generative artificial intelligence (AI)
and conclusion.

2.1   |   Organisational Resilience
Resilience has been studied at the individual, departmental, or-
ganisational and societal levels (i.e., from micro to macro levels).
Resilience has been studied across a range of fields including
psychology and social science, ecosystem science, economics,
management, supply chain management and information sys-
tems. In information systems, Nielsen et al. (2023) examined
how a university responded to the COVID-­ 19 lockdown by rap-
idly shifting to virtual teaching, showing that organisational re-
silience unfolds as a process in which actors progressively pivot,
adapt and normalise their use of digital resources. The study
demonstrates that resilience is shaped not just by technology it-
self, but by how digital resources (e.g., technologies, skills and
practices) are mobilised and reconfigured over time in response
to environmental turbulence.
Overall, the common theme in all definitions is that resilience
comprises both stakeholder and system capabilities to bounce
back from environmental turbulence through processes and
plans such as disaster recovery procedures and business con-
tinuity planning. Organisational resilience is an organisation's
ability to withstand and recover from disruptions or challenges
while maintaining its core functions and purpose (Kantur and
Say 2015). IT organisational resilience is often dependent on the
type of turbulence that occurs (Trieu et al. 2023). Being able to
answer the question, ‘Resilient to what?’ is essential, especially
as turbulence can take on countless forms (Martin-­ Breen and
Anderies 2011).
Resilience could present differently in an array of turbulent
scenarios, such as ransomware attacks, terrorism attacks, nat-
ural disasters, pandemics, and so forth. Resilience in each of
these scenarios means something different. However, as Zhang
et al. (2025) noted, technology alone is not enough; agile pro-
cesses and empowered people also play a critical role in how
effectively an organisation can respond to change and sustain
performance. Trieu et al. (2023) found that small and medium-­
sized enterprises (SMEs) should invest in information technol-
ogy (IT) not only to enhance operational efficiency but also as a
strategic resource that fosters organisational ambidexterity and
resilience, which are critical for sustaining high performance in
dynamic and competitive environments.
2.2   |   IT Service Quality (People)
As IT departments increasingly focus on service-­ oriented out-
puts, measuring IT service quality becomes crucial. IT service
quality has a long and well-­ established history as a construct,
referring to the gap between the quality of service delivered
and the customer's expectations of what should have been re-
ceived (Abdullah et al. 2024). High IT service quality provides
numerous benefits that significantly enhance organisational
effectiveness. Primarily, it boosts user satisfaction by having
trained people delivering services that meet or exceed expec-
tations, which, in turn, improves productivity as users expe-
rience fewer disruptions and can focus on their core tasks.
This increased satisfaction also leads to better organisational
performance by ensuring smooth and effective process exe-
cution, positively impacting overall business outcomes. Mao
et al. (2023) found that high IT service quality significantly
increases user satisfaction and continued use of digital ser-
vices, which collectively enhance organisational resilience
by fostering trust and sustained engagement with critical IT
systems. Moreover, robust IT service quality supports better
risk management with comprehensive security measures and
reliable support, reducing the risks of data breaches and sys-
tem failures (Kumar et al. 2021). It also facilitates informed
decision-­ making with accurate, timely information and en-
hances employee morale by reducing frustrations and dis-
ruptions, leading to improved job satisfaction and workplace
contentment.
Despite its many benefits, high service quality in IT can pres-
ent several drawbacks. Achieving and maintaining top-­ tier
service quality often entails significant costs, including in-
vestments in technology, training and personnel, which can
strain an organisation's budget (Mao et al. 2023). Managing
service quality can also be complex and resource-­ intensive,
potentially leading to administrative burdens (Abdullah
et al. 2024). Additionally, improvements beyond a certain
point might offer diminishing returns, making further in-
vestment less efficient. The pressure to maintain these high
standards of IT service quality can impact employee morale
and increase turnover (Kumar et al. 2021). Resistance to pro-
cess changes and the risk of complacency after reaching high
service levels can also pose challenges, potentially undermin-
ing long-­ term service quality. Success lies in developing a bal-
anced approach to training employees on service quality. A
resilient department is built upon a well-­ trained and resilient
staff. One study found that while system, information, and
service quality all influence performance, IT service quality
is the most reliable indicator, as it best reflects the combined
impact on customer satisfaction and operational efficiency
(Abdullah et al. 2024).
2.2.1   |   IT Service Quality Measure
In this research we adopted the well-­ known IT service quality
instrument developed by Pitt et al. (1995). This IT service qual-
ity measure comprehensively measures this construct through
multiple dimensions consisting of tangibles, reliability, respon-
siveness, assurance and empathy, which cultivates a culture
of excellence among employees. This measure was reviewed,
adapted and validated by five IT experts confirming no modi-
fications were necessary, as the scale adequately captured the
general context for which it was originally developed.
2.3   |   IT Governance (Process)
Informationtechnologygovernance(ITG)hasrisenconsistently
in importance over the years, especially as technology's role has
become more complex, embedded and costly. Effective ITG en-
sures that IT aligns with business objectives and mitigates risks.
ITG is defined as the alignment of objectives between the busi-
ness and its IT department (ISACA 2019). ITG can be seen as
‘specifying the decision rights and accountability framework
to encourage desirable behavior in the use of IT’ (Weill and
Ross 2005).

The advantages of establishing good governance structures

Despite its numerous advantages, building and customising
ITG practices within an organisation remain a challenging
endeavour. Miyamoto (2022) found that 20% of respondents
indicated that ITG processes are often restricting rules and
regulations, 33% of the respondents felt it is not particularly
necessary for their firms, and 17% recognised its necessity but
were unsure how to approach it. Setyadji et al. (2023) discuss
the ineffective implementation of ITG in risk management

adequate resources and implementation challenges playing an
essential role in ineffectiveness. Many factors can contribute
to the failure of an ITG practice, and its successful implemen-
tation is by no means a straightforward undertaking. That's
why building a mature ITG practice remains a challenge,
and its ongoing issues continue to undermine our resilience
in times of turbulence. In this research we adopted the well-­
known COBIT framework as it transcends industry and com-
pany size as an ITG framework.
2.3.1   |   COBIT Framework
COBIT is a framework for IT governance and management
designed to help organisations meet their business objectives
(ISACA 2019). The COBIT framework is often used as a refer-
ence point by IT professionals looking for guidelines regarding
organisationalITgovernanceandmanagementprocesses(Putra
and Fianty 2023). For example, the COBIT maturity model can
be used to assess the development of management processes in
an IT organisation. The COBIT framework can also be used to
understand and manage all substantial IT risk types. In this
study we use COBIT to measure the level of process maturity in
the IT organisation within a company.
COBIT supports the development, improvement, implementa-
tion and governance of information technology within an or-
ganisation (ISACA 2019). COBIT offers a detailed framework
that ensures IT processes are aligned with business objec-
tives, thereby supporting and advancing organisational goals
(Rusman et al. 2022). By establishing clear roles, responsibil-
ities and performance metrics, COBIT enhances both trans-
parency and accountability within IT operations. It also aids
in risk management by pinpointing potential IT-­ related risks
and implementing controls to address them. Moreover, COBIT
fosters consistency and standardisation across IT practices,
which boosts overall efficiency and effectiveness. Its emphasis
on continuous improvement and best practices helps organisa-
tionsadapttotechnologicaladvancementsandshiftingbusiness
demands. In essence, COBIT's structured methodology enables
better control over IT resources, leading to improved decision-­
making and greater value delivery (Rusman et al. 2022).
However, it is important to note that organisations must often
customise COBIT to meet their specific needs, which can be a
limitationfromapractitioner'sperspective.Despiteitsstrengths,
COBIT has some limitations. Its complexity can make imple-
mentation difficult, especially for smaller organisations with
limited IT resources (Putra and Fianty 2023). The framework's
comprehensivenaturemayalsoleadtosubstantialtimeandcost
investments, as organisations need to thoroughly understand
and integrate its detailed processes and controls. Additionally,
COBIT's broad approach might result in a lack of specificity in
certain areas, requiring further customisation to meet specific
organisational needs. The framework's dependence on existing
IT infrastructure can be a challenge for organisations with out-
dated or incompatible systems, potentially hindering alignment
withCOBITstandards(Rusmanetal.2022).Furthermore,while
COBIT offers a structured methodology, it may not fully address
all emerging technologies and business trends, requiring on-
going updates and adjustments to stay current. While not all
organisations directly utilise COBIT, this framework provides
tools to assess the maturity of an organisation ITG maturity.

3   |   Methods
3.1   |   Design
The research study utilised quantitative methods to examine the
influence of environmental turbulence, IT service quality and
IT governance maturity on IT organisational resilience. While
this study primarily employed a quantitative approach, the re-
sultswerefurthersupplementedbylimitedqualitativefollow-­up
providing practitioner insights and context. The core data was
collected and analysed through a survey utilising previously de-
veloped scales for the primary constructs. A pre-­ test of the sur-
vey was carried out to improve the face and content validity of
the instrument. A team of 5 experts in various IT fields and posi-
tions completed and critiqued the survey and the scale. Two cur-
rent CIOs, one prior CIO in a research firm, an academic faculty
member in the field of IT, and a medical doctor closely coupled
with IT implementations in a healthcare setting all agreed that
the survey represented the constructs appropriately.
3.2   |   Sample
The final sample was 157 IT managers or executives. Within
this sample, there were more women (56.0%) than men. The
highest proportion of respondents was in the age range of 40–49
(33%). The average tenure for IT managers in the study was
over 15years. The demographics of the sample can be found in
Appendix A: Tables A1–A3.

accommodates both measurement and structural models, (ii) is
suitable for testing models with smaller sample sizes and (iii) is
ideal for exploratory research due to its ability to estimate less
restricted models (Sarstedt et al. 2017). We evaluated the mea-
surement model by examining individual item reliabilities, in-
ternal consistency and discriminant validity before assessing
the structural model. The structural model provides insights
into how well the theoretical model predicts the hypothesised
pathsorrelationships,estimatedbythepathcoefficientsandthe
R-­squared values.

4   |   Findings of the Study
Many strategies have been identified to build IT organisational
resilience. In this study, based on the established literature and
discussions with CIOs and IT leaders, we anticipated a direct
relationship between environmental turbulence and IT organ-
isational resilience, but to our surprise the relationship was in-
significant (β=0.034, p>0.05). Reference the research model in
Figure 1. Despite environmental turbulence not directly influ-
encing IT organisational resilience, IT organisations can't just
wait for disruptions and react. Merely reacting to turbulence
does not build IT organisational resilience. Our research finds
that environmental turbulence does not directly influence IT
organisational resilience (β=0.034, p>0.05). Instead, IT or-
ganisational resilience is proactively built through a flexible
set of capabilities specifically, strong IT governance maturity
and high-­ quality IT service practices. These functions enable
organisations to navigate uncertainty more effectively, thereby
strengthening their resilience over time. The rest of the relation-
ships in the model were found to be significant and are sum-
marised in the following section.

4.2   |   Finding 2: Mastering IT Governance Drives
IT Service Quality
Our research shows that superior IT governance practices di-
rectly increase IT service quality (β=0.258, p<0.01) across the
organisation. IT service quality is at the core of customer service
at an organisation, consisting of the dimensions: tangible assets
and personnel appearance, reliability of service, level of respon-
siveness, level of assurance and level of empathy (Leachman
and Scheibenreif 2023). IT governance and quality of service go
hand in hand, ensuring IT services are seamless, superior and
a guarantee across the organisation. Strengthen and mature IT
governance structures to systematically improve service deliv-
ery, accountability and user satisfaction. The third quote illus-
trates the positive path from ITG maturity to IT service quality.
A CIO executive partner in a prominent
research firm and prior CIO himself stated: “It is
no surprise that IT governance helps create better IT
services… I'd say better IT governance is at the core of
most IT capabilities as it has matured since like the
90's to encompass a lot of IT areas/functions”
IT governance lies at the heart of numerous IT capabilities, in-
cluding IT service quality. Our research suggests that enhancing
IT service quality begins with the strategic customisation of the
IT governance model particularly during periods of disruption.
Tailoring governance structures to the institutional context not
only accelerates the maturation of IT governance practices but
also strengthens the foundation of IT service delivery. A well-­
alignedgovernanceframeworkenablestheorganisationtorealise
compoundedbenefitsbyfosteringbothoperationalexcellenceand
strategic resilience. Ultimately, this alignment serves as a critical
enabler, equipping institutions to navigate environmental turbu-
lence, regardless of its form, with greater agility and confidence.
This finding justifies IT leadership to invest in maturing IT
governance practices to build IT service quality. CIOs need to
ensure IT governance processes are directly tied to service qual-
ity outcomes like response times, uptime, customer satisfaction
and user experience. IT governance is at the core of many de-
partmental and organisational benefits, IT leadership needs to
customise it to realise the maximum benefits (Leachman and
Scheibenreif 2023).

We have multiple takeaways in this study that highlight specific

help organisations prevail in the face of environmental turbu-
lence. You don't need to learn the hard way; don't wait for failure
to spur action. IT leaders must proactively build and mature the
mechanisms that will carry the organisation through challeng-
ing times, leveraging both people and processes. It is this resil-
ience, developed upfront, that ultimately defines who succeeds

5.3   |   Focus on the Fundamentals
In one sense, a key takeaway for IT leaders is to keep it simple!
In accordance with the first finding, when faced with the inevi-
table turbulence, from inside or outside the institution, focus on
IT governance and IT service quality to help navigate the path.
Focusing on the basics is often missed trying to navigate the
mostrecentturbulence.Weareallfamiliarwith‘reinventingthe
wheel’. The solution lies in the foundational elements and this
research showed that across industries and company sizes, IT
leadership can build IT organisational resilience by focusing on
foundational and already established mechanisms like IT gov-
ernance and IT service quality. This research indicates that the
greatest return on investment can be achieved by guiding the
IT team to refocus on the fundamental practices of managing
an IT department. While new innovative solutions are desirable,
not every time a company faces turbulence should it discard
all the foundational mechanisms it has in pursuit of new ones.
Sometimes modifying a mechanism to adjust to the new land-
scape is the simplest and most effective way to move forward.
The CIO should remain calm, stay focused on the established
path, and maintain simplicity in their approach.

5.4   |   From Concept to Practice (Generative AI)
Fast forward to November 30, 2022, when OpenAI released
ChatGPT, and the world suddenly found itself grappling with
how to understand its value, potential uses, and applicability
within their organisations. We will share the experience of a
CIO at a mid-­ sized higher education institution, showing how
he adapted IT people and IT processes to meet this disruption
head-­ on and build lasting IT organisational resilience.
Such emerging technologies, particularly generative artificial
intelligence (AI), are introducing new forms of disruption that
test the adaptability and responsiveness of IT organisations in
companies. Generative AI is a subset of AI that creates new con-
tent such as text, images, audio, or code based on patterns it has
learned from existing data. Instead of just recognising or pre-
dicting, it produces original outputs (e.g., ChatGPT generating
answers, or DALL·E creating images). Generative AI serves as a
powerful and timely example of disruptive technology, allowing
us to demonstrate how our aforementioned framework around
IT governance and IT service quality drive IT organisational
resilience.
As generative AI-­ driven tools and systems are rapidly integrated
intooperations,theCIOrecognisedtheneedforITgovernance
structures to evolve in order to address new challenges such as
ethical use, data privacy, and alignment with strategic objec-
tives, etc. Simultaneously, the CIO also realised the need for IT
service quality to extend beyond technical delivery to encom-
pass empathetic, user-­ focused support, as stakeholders navigate
unfamiliar tools and shifting workflows. Incorporating genera-
tive AI into the resilience framework underscores the need for
IT governance models that are both agile and inclusive, and for
IT staff who are equipped to manage change with both techni-
cal and interpersonal competence. This alignment the CIO led

6   |   Conclusion
Organisations have been preached at in terms of ITG maturity
and IT service quality for years, however the importance of
these in establishing IT organisational resilience to withstand
environmental turbulence is largely overlooked. While organi-
sations pay lip service to these concepts because they have been
around for a long time, they may lose sight in how they form the
foundation of IT organisational resilience. Fundamentals mat-
ter and they matter in ways that we haven't been thinking of.
Too often organisations either fail to recognise turbulence and
do nothing or worse overreact to it, our goal is to help you in-
stead respond with balance and intention.

IT Governance
(6-­ p oint scale)
‘[E]nsures that
stakeholder needs,
conditions and options are
evaluated to determine
balanced, agreed- ­ o n
enterprise objectives to be
achieved; setting direction
through prioritisation
and decision making; and
monitoring performance
and compliance against
agreed-­ o n direction and

EDM01: Ensured Governance Framework Setting and Maintenance
1. EDM01.01 The IT department continually identifies and engages with the enterprise's stakeholders, documents an understanding of the
requirements and evaluates the current and future design of governance of enterprise IT.
2. EDM01.02 The IT department informs leaders on IT governance principles and obtains their support, buy-­ i n and commitment. The IT
department also guides the structures, processes and practices for the governance of IT in line with the agreed governance principles,
decision-­ m aking models and authority levels. The IT department defines the information required for informed decision making.
3. EDM01.03 The IT department monitors the effectiveness and performance of the enterprise's governance of IT. The IT department assesses
whether the governance system and implemented mechanisms (including structures, principles and processes) are operating effectively and
provide appropriate oversight of IT to enable value creation.
EDM02: Ensured Benefits Delivery
4. EDM02.01 The IT department reviews and ensures clarity of the enterprise and IT strategies and current services. The IT department defines
an appropriate investment mix based on cost, alignment with strategy, type of benefit for the programs in the portfolio, degree of risk and
financial measures, such as cost and expected return on investment (ROI) over the full economic life cycle. The IT department adjusts the
enterprise and IT strategies where necessary.
5. EDM02.02 The IT department continually evaluates the portfolio of IT-­ e nabled investments, services and assets to determine the likelihood
of achieving enterprise objectives and delivering value. The IT department identifies and evaluates any changes in direction to management
that will optimise value creation.
6. EDM02.03 The IT department directs value management principles and practices to enable optimal value realisation from IT-­ e nabled
investments throughout their full economic life cycle
7. EDM02.04 The IT department monitors key goals and metrics to determine whether the enterprise receives expected value and benefit from
I&T-­ e nabled investments and services. Identify significant issues and consider corrective actions.
EDM03: Ensured Risk Optimisation
8. EDM03.01 The IT department continually examines and evaluates the effect of risk on the current and future use of IT in the enterprise. The
IT department considers whether the enterprise's risk appetite is appropriate and ensures that risk to enterprise value related to the use of
IT is identified and managed.
9. EDM03.02 The IT department directs the establishment of risk management practices to provide reasonable assurance that IT risk
management practices are appropriate and that actual IT risk does not exceed the board's risk appetite.
10. EDM03.03 The IT department monitors the key goals and metrics of the risk management processes. The IT department determines how
deviations or problems will be identified, tracked and reported for remediation.
EDM04: Ensured Resource Optimisation
11. EDM04.01 The IT department continually examines and evaluates the current and future need for business and IT resources (financial
and human), options for resourcing (including sourcing strategies) and allocation and management principles to meet the needs of the
enterprise in the optimal manner.
12. EDM04.02 The IT department ensures the adoption of resource management principles to enable optimal use of business and IT resources
throughout their full economic life cycle.
13. EDM04.03 The IT department monitors the key goals and metrics of the resource management processes. The IT department determines
how deviations or problems will be identified, tracked and reported for remediation.
EDM05: Ensured Stakeholder Engagement
14. EDM05.01 The IT department continually examines and evaluates current and future requirements for stakeholder engagement and
reporting (including reporting mandated by regulatory requirements) and communication to other stakeholders. The IT department
establishes principles for engaging and communicating with stakeholders.
15. EDM05.02 The IT department ensures the establishment of effective stakeholder involvement, communication and reporting, including
mechanisms for ensuring the quality and completeness of information, overseeing mandatory reporting and creating a communication
strategy for stakeholders.
16. EDM05.03 The IT department monitors stakeholder engagement levels and the effectiveness of stakeholder communication. The IT
department assesses mechanisms for ensuring accuracy, reliability and effectiveness and ascertain whether the requirements of different
stakeholders in terms of reporting and communication are met.
