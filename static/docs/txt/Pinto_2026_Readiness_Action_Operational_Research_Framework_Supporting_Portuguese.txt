Abstract: Small and Medium-Sized Enterprises (SMEs) play a critical role in economic growth, yet struggle to adopt
digital transformation due to limited resources, lack of expertise, and operational constraints. Existing matu-
rity models are either too generic or fail to provide actionable guidance tailored to SMEs. This paper proposes
a structured self-assessment framework that evaluates digital maturity across seven dimensions and generates
practical technological and strategic recommendations. The framework was developed using a design-science
research approach combining a systematic literature review with empirical data collected from SMEs through
a quantitative survey and iterative user testing. The resulting tool enables SMEs to assess readiness, prioritize
initiatives, and align digitalization strategy with operational goals. Validation results show that SMEs score
higher in digital culture and openness to transformation but remain significantly underdeveloped in data an-
alytics, cybersecurity, and Industry 4.0 technologies. Feedback from pilot usage confirms the framework’s
usability and relevance, with 87% of respondents stating it supports decision-making. The proposed frame-
work bridges the gap between theory and practice and contributes a practical, replicable artifact for guiding
SME digital transformation.

differentiators (Ghobakhloo and Fathi, 2019; Zhang
et al., 2022). Digital transformation (Romero and
Mammadov, 2025), understood as the strategic in-
tegration of digital technologies into business pro-
cesses, organizational models, and value creation, has
therefore become essential for competitiveness and
long-term survival (Birkel and Wehrle, 2024; Inan,
2024; Tung, 2024).
However, SMEs face deeper challenges than large
enterprises when engaging in digital transforma-

2 BACKGROUND & RELATED
WORK
Digital transformation has emerged as a strategic im-
perative for organizations seeking to improve oper-
ational efficiency, responsiveness, and competitive-
ness (Qi et al., 2023). While large enterprises of-
ten lead digital adoption, SMEs face a fundamen-
tallydifferentreality: limitedresources, lackofexper-
tise, andshort-termoperationalpressuresmakedigital
transformation a constrained decision problem rather
than a strategic open-end exploration. Understanding
SME-specific barriers and identifying mechanisms
that support structured decision-making is therefore
essential.
2.1 Digital Transformation in SMEs
Digital transformation involves the integration of dig-
ital technologies to improve business models, pro-
cesses, customer experience, and internal capabili-
ties (Alam et al., 2022; Tung, 2024). In SMEs, the
process is shaped by three core constraints: (1) lim-
ited resources (financial, human, technological); (2)
lack of internal digital skills or knowledge; and (3)
organizational inertia and resistance to change.
Although SMEs generally acknowledge the im-
portance of digital transformation, many initiatives
fail due to insufficient planning, lack of strategic
alignment, or inability to measure readiness. Unlike
larger organizations, SMEs rarely possess dedicated
innovationteamsortransformationmanagers, making
it difficult to assess where to start and how to priori-
tize investments (Volf et al., 2024; Teng et al., 2022).
At the same time, digital transformation of-
fers relevant benefits to SMEs, including efficiency
gains through automation, cost reduction, better cus-
tomer interaction, and access to data-driven decision-
making. Emerging technologies — such as cloud
platforms (Ray, 2016), Internet of Things (IoT) (Lima
and Pinto, 2025; Sobin, 2020), data analytics (Duan
and Da Xu, 2024), cybersecurity (Dritsas and Trigka,
2025), and Artificial Intelligence (AI) (Zha et al.,
2025) — can increase productivity and support scal-
ability. Yet adoption often occurs opportunistically,
driven by urgency instead of strategy, resulting in iso-
lated tools that fail to produce long-term value.
2.2 Existing Digital Maturity Models
Several digital maturity models exist in the literature
to support organizations in assessing readiness and
structuring transformation initiatives (Karadağ et al.,
2024; Tiwari, 2024). Most models follow a staged
progression (e.g., initial; developing; optimized) and
evaluate dimensions such as strategy, culture, pro-
cesses, technology, or customer focus (AlMulhim,
2021; Ranjan, 2024). However, three clear limitations
emerge when analyzing existing maturity models:
• Models are generic or designed for large enter-
prises: They often fail to capture SME-specific
constraints such as limited investment capacity,
lack of dedicated IT staff, and lower baseline dig-
ital readiness.
• Models provide assessment but not guidance:
Most frameworks allow SMEs to identify their
maturity level, but do not offer concrete recom-
mendationsorprioritizationstrategiesfortechnol-
ogy adoption.

• Frameworks are static and conceptual: Many

Most maturity frameworks answer the question
”Where are we?” but not ”What should we do next?”.
From a decision-analysis perspective, maturity as-
sessment should also support resource prioritization
under constraints, linking identified gaps to concrete
actions rather than providing measurement alone.
2.3 Research Gap
The key gap identified in the literature is the absence
of a decision-support maturity framework tailored to
SMEs that: (1) captures current digital readiness; (2)
prioritizes actions considering resource constraints;
(3) links assessment results to recommended tech-
nological and organizational actions; (4) is validated
through real usage rather than remaining conceptual.
While prior work has produced models for digital
maturity assessment, none provide a practical, data-
driven, and dynamically generated set of transforma-
tion paths that SMEs can immediately apply.
This research addresses this gap by proposing a
self-assessment digital maturity framework that not
only evaluates readiness across seven dimensions but
alsogeneratesactionablerecommendationstosupport
decision-making and prioritization—turning maturity
assessment into a transformation enabler.
3 METHODOLOGY
This research followed a structured and iterative ap-
proach combining literature analysis, empirical data
collection, and framework development to design a
practical self-assessment tool for SME digital trans-
formation. An in-depth literature review identi-
fied key barriers, drivers, and limitations of exist-
ing maturity models, particularly their lack of ac-
tionable decision-support for SMEs, which informed
the conceptual foundation of the framework. These
findings were complemented by empirical data col-
lected through a structured online survey distributed
to SMEs across manufacturing, consulting, and tech-
nology sectors, capturing perceptions of digital readi-
ness, benefits, and obstacles. Descriptive statistical
analysis revealed recurring maturity gaps that guided
the definition of seven maturity dimensions, diagnos-
tic indicators, and recommendation logic. The re-
sulting framework was refined through iterative user
feedback to ensure clarity, usability, and practical rel-
evance, yielding a validated operational tool aligned
with SMEs’ decision-making needs.
As the second step of the methodology, empirical
data were collected to identify digital maturity gaps
and contextualize the findings from the literature. A
structured survey entitled ”Digital Transformation in
Companies” was conducted among Portuguese SMEs
from manufacturing, consulting, and technology sec-
tors, targeting current practices, perceived barriers
and drivers, and organizational readiness for digital
transformation. The questionnaire covered company
characterization, technology adoption, barriers and
motivations, and workforce development.
A total of 53 valid responses were obtained, pre-
dominantly from micro and small enterprises, reflect-
ing the national business structure. Descriptive sta-
tistical analysis revealed that although most SMEs
recognize the strategic importance of digital transfor-
mation, implementation remains uneven and concen-
trated in foundational technologies. More than 70%
of respondents reported adopting at least one digital
solution—such as cloud computing, ERP/MES sys-
tems, or digital communication tools—whereas fewer
than 25% had implemented advanced technologies
such as IoT, artificial intelligence, or robotics, high-
lighting a clear maturity gap between basic digitaliza-
tion and transformative adoption (Figure 1a).
The analysis further identified financial con-
straints (62%), shortage of qualified personnel (57%),
and resistance to change (48%) as the most significant
barriers to digital transformation (Figure 1b). In con-
trast, competitiveness, efficiency improvement, and
customer satisfaction emerged as the primary drivers,
with competitiveness cited by nearly half of the re-
spondents. Workforce development also revealed a
critical gap, as 68% of SMEs acknowledged the need
for continuous upskilling, yet only 40% had imple-
mented structured training programs. Finally, while
public and European funding programs were recog-
nized as relevant enablers, limited awareness of avail-
able incentives persisted.
Overall, these empirical findings exposed consis-
tent digital maturity gaps across technology adop-
tion, skills, and resource availability, reinforcing the
need for a structured assessment framework capable
of supporting SMEs in evaluating readiness and pri-
oritizing transformation actions.

4.1 Framework Overview
The framework consists of three core modules: (1)
Assessment Module – presents a structured question-
naire measuring digital readiness across seven dimen-
sions; (2) Analytics Engine – calculates and visual-
izes maturity scores through automatic aggregation of
user responses; (3) Recommendation Module – gener-
ates customized strategic and technological guidance
based on detected maturity gaps. The interface was
implemented in a web-based environment that auto-
matically stores and processes user responses. The
modular design allows easy adaptation to different
sectors and organizational sizes while remaining ac-
cessible to non-technical users.
The framework evaluates SME digital maturity
across seven interrelated dimensions that capture
strategic, operational, technological, and human en-
ablers of digital transformation. Each dimension is
assessed using weighted indicators scored on a five-
point Likert scale and normalized to a 0–100 range.
Table 1 summarizes the evaluated dimensions and
their respective focus areas, providing a concise and
comprehensive representation of the digital maturity
model, namely Digital Strategy & Leadership, Op-
erations & Automation, Data & Analytics, Customer
Experience & Engagement, Infrastructure & Cyber-
security, Industry 4.0 & Advanced Technologies, and
Digital Culture & Skills.
In the current implementation, all dimensions are
equally weighted to ensure transparency and inter-
pretability for SMEs; adaptive weighting and sensi-
tivity analysis are left for future work.

Upon completion, users receive an interactive report

specific maturity. Figure 2 illustrates an example of

An integrated feedback option enables partici-

accuracy of results. This feedback is continuously
incorporated to refine question phrasing, weighting
factors, and recommendation rules, ensuring that the
framework evolves with SME needs.
5 RESULTS & VALIDATION
Empirical validation of the proposed framework is
presented, based on post-assessment responses col-
lected after SMEs experimented with the tool. The
objectives were to characterize the digital matu-
rity profiles of participating SMEs and to evaluate
the framework’s perceived usefulness, usability, and
decision-support value. A total of 19 SMEs from
manufacturing, energy, consulting, and technology
sectors completed the assessment and feedback pro-
cess.
5.1 Descriptive Statistics
The participating SMEs exhibit an intermediate level
of digital readiness, with an average Overall Digital
Maturity (ODM) of 58.3%. As summarized in Ta-
ble 2, higher maturity levels are observed in organiza-
tional and customer-facing dimensions, while lower
scores persist in data-related, cybersecurity, and ad-
vanced technology domains. These results indicate
that although most SMEs have initiated their digital
transformation journey, significant gaps remain in an-
alytical and technological capabilities. Lower matu-
rity scores in data analytics and cybersecurity consis-
tently co-occur with reported skill shortages and in-
vestment constraints, reinforcing the link between ca-
pability gaps and structural barriers.
5.2 Barriers and Drivers
The analysis revealed consistent patterns linking ma-
turity gaps to structural constraints. Financial limita-
tions, shortage of qualified personnel, uncertainty re-
garding return on investment, and cybersecurity con-
cerns emerged as the most significant barriers to digi-
tal transformation. Conversely, competitiveness, effi-
ciency improvement, customer satisfaction, and lead-
ership commitment were identified as the primary
drivers. These findings reinforce that digital transfor-
mation success depends on both technological adop-
tion and organizational readiness.

6 DISCUSSION
The results indicate that digital transformation in
SMEs is characterized by uneven progress across do-
mains and persistent structural constraints. While cul-
tural readiness and leadership commitment are rela-
tively mature, analytical and technological capabili-
ties remain limited. These asymmetries confirm that
digital transformation should be viewed as a multi-
dimensional decision problem, requiring SMEs to
balanceinvestmentpriorities, capabilitydevelopment,
and risk under resource constraints.

From a managerial perspective, the proposed
framework supports structured decision-making by
quantifying digital maturity and linking identified
gaps to actionable recommendations. This enables
decision-makers to prioritize high-impact initiatives,
allocate scarce financial and human resources more
effectively, and benchmark progress over time. The
visual maturity reports further facilitate communi-
cation between technical and non-technical stake-
holders, translating abstract transformation goals into
measurable and interpretable objectives.
From an academic standpoint, the framework con-
ceptualizes digital maturity as a multi-criteria de-
cision problem, where each dimension represents
an organizational capability and the overall maturity
score (Eq. 2) serves as a weighted aggregation mea-
sure. This structure aligns naturally with decision-
analysisapproaches, providingafoundationforfuture
methodological extensions.
In particular, the framework could be extended
through: (i) optimization models recommending re-
source allocation across maturity dimensions; (ii) pre-
dictive analytics linking maturity improvements to
business performance indicators; and (iii) simula-
tion models for evaluating alternative transformation
pathways under budget constraints. Such extensions
would strengthen the quantitative decision-analytic
foundations of digital-transformation research and
support more robust strategic planning.
6.1 Study Limitations
Despite its contributions, this study has limitations.
The sample size is relatively small and geographically
concentrated, which may limit generalizability. As
the framework relies on self-assessment data, results
may be affected by perceptual bias. Moreover, the
cross-sectional design prevents analysis of maturity
evolution over time and its impact on performance.
Finally, thecurrentrecommendationlogicisheuristic;
future work could enhance adaptability and person-
alization through machine-learning or optimization-
based methods.
Acknowledging these limitations provides clear
directions for future research aimed at refining and
scaling the framework across diverse industrial and
geographical contexts.
7 CONCLUSION & FUTURE
WORK
This paper presented a digital-transformation self-
assessment framework tailored to the context of
SMEs. The framework enables organizations to eval-
uate digital maturity across seven strategic dimen-
sions and provides data-driven recommendations to
support efficiency, competitiveness, and technology
adoption. By integrating structured self-assessment,
automated scoring, and tailored guidance, it trans-
forms maturity evaluation into a practical decision-
support process.
Empirical validation with participating SMEs in-
dicates that most organizations are at intermediate
stages of digitalization, with stronger performance in
cultural and strategic dimensions and persistent gaps
in data analytics, cybersecurity, and advanced tech-
nologies. User feedback confirmed the framework’s
usability and relevance, supporting its effectiveness
for prioritizing digital initiatives under resource con-
straints.
From an academic perspective, the framework
conceptualizes digital maturity as a multi-criteria de-
cision problem and provides a quantitative basis for
future methodological extensions. Future work will
focus on longitudinal assessments to track maturity
evolution, the integration of data-driven and machine-
learning-based recommendation mechanisms, and the
development of sector-specific modules. Linking
the framework with policy dashboards and open-data
platforms may further enable large-scale monitoring
of digital maturity trends and support evidence-based
digitalization strategies.
Overall, the proposed framework offers a concise
and actionable contribution to SME digital transfor-
mation by supporting informed decision-making and
continuous improvement while establishing a foun-
dation for more advanced quantitative and decision-
analytic extensions.
