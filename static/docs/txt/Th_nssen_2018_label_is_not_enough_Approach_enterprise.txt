A Label is not enough – Approach for an Enterprise Architecture
Role Description Framework
Barbara Thönssen barbara.thoenssen@fhnw.ch,
Mark von Dewitz mark@vondewitz.ch*
University of Applied Sciences of Northwestern Switzerland, Riggenbachstrasse 16, 4600 Olten, Switzerland
von Dewitz Consulting, Ackerstrasse 11, 4800 Zofingen, Switzerland
Abstract
Enterprise architecture management is the linchpin of digital transformation. Implementation requires clear tasks, roles and skills.
Although different frameworks, such as TOGAF and SFIA, provide help for this, none is suitable to completely cover the needs in
practice. In our approach we have tried to close this gap by adding SFIA to TOGAF. The assignment was recorded in the form of
an Excel table. Based on the Excel table, a role fat sheet - which we have also developed - can then be filled automatically. This
allows companies to describe roles, such as the Enterprise Architecture Manager, in a way that they are consistent, comparable and
complete. We have demonstrated the benefits of our approach in three use cases. For our research we applied Design Science
Research methodology. The evaluation was carried out with the help of a focus group, which also critically assessed interim results
of our work. We can saythat with our approach we have satisfactorilysolved a practical problemand with the extension ofTOGAF
by SFIA we have contributed to closing the existing gap in the classification of terms that are relevant for Enterprise Architecture
management.

1. Introduction
Searching at the Swiss job market† for ‘Enterprise Architect’ lists 156 hits. Checking the details reveals that there
is no common understandingof what Enterprise Architecture is, whichroles belongto it [1]. Companies have different
understandings ofthe Enterprise Architecture practice. This is partiallybased on the fact, that the role ofthe Enterprise
Architect has and is evolving over time [2]. This unclear understanding persists, even though the well-established
enterprise architecture standard TOGAF 9.1 [1] provides classifications of roles (e.g. enterprise architecture manager)
needed to perform Enterprise Architecture. Within this standard, The Open Group has explicitly recognized this issue
by stating: «Enterprise Architecture and Enterprise Architect are widely used but poorly defined terms in industry
today. Theyare used to denote a varietyof practices and skills applied in a wide varietyof architecture domains. There
is a need for better classification to enable more implicit understanding of what type of architecture/architect is being
described» [1]. Thus, companies and organizations struggle to find qualified staff in the domain of Enterprise
Architecture [3] because the requirements for the vacancies might be imprecise or homespun and potential applicants
or job seekers have no references to describe their skills and position.
To our knowledge no approach exists that describes Enterprise Architecture roles with an explicit linkage to the
aspects tasks, responsibilities, skills and skill levels. While TOGAF 9.1 provides an Architecture Skills Framework,
this framework appears to lack comprehensiveness in multiple areas.
In search of improvement possibilities, we considered the skills framework SFIA 6 [4] (Skills Framework for the
Information Age) was chosen to provide input and address the identified lacks of TOGAF’s Architecture Skills
Framework. Therefore, we will show how the Architecture Skills Framework of TOGAF 9.1 can be enhanced by
SFIA 6 to provide comprehensive and commonly acceptable descriptions of roles in the Enterprise Architecture
domain. Furthermore, an EA Role Fact Sheet is developed that allows to uniquely describe a role in order to exploit
the improvements made to TOGAF’s Architecture Skills Framework and thus help organizations in thoroughly
defining their Enterprise Architecture roles.
For our work we applied Design Science Research (DSR) [5] and the developed artefacts are the Extended
Architecture Skills Framework (EASF) and the aforementioned EA Role Fact Sheet for roles descriptions (EARF).
The paper is structured as follows: In section 2 we will introduce the business problem in depth by describing three
use case scenarios. Section 3 will give an overview on related work and elaborate the research gap. In section 4 we
will briefly introduce how DSR was applied, followed by section 5 in which we detail our approach. In section 6
evaluation of our approach is discussed. We close our paper in section 6 with the conclusion and future work.
2. Use Case Scenarios
Derived from the general situation described in the introduction, this section provides three use cases to give more
detailed evidence of the problem our research addresses.

The recruitment of personnel is part of the responsibility of the Human Resources department within an
organization. The fact that Enterprise Architecture has and still is evolving and is more of a set of multi-dimensional

2.2. Standardizing Skill Descriptions (UC2)
Another use case fortheEASF fromthe domainofHumanResourcesispeopledevelopment. Overtime,employees
develop their skills and gain experience. In a time in which change becomes the only constant, it is important to keep
track of employees’ professional development. Hence, skill profiles should be continuously extended by somehow
standardized description in order to support searchability and comparability. If the EASF is used to define
requirements that must be met to fill an open position, eligible candidates may be identified more easily.
2.3. Developing Enterprise Architecture Capability (UC3)
A core concept of the TOGAF 9.1 standard is the Architecture Development Cycle. This cycle contains a
preliminary phase, which describes the steps to be taken to develop an Architecture Capability desired by the
organization. One step to be taken within this phase is «Define and establish enterprise architecture team and
organization» [1]. The EASF can support this step in several ways. First of all, it can be used to define required skills,
tasks to be performed and responsibilities to be taken to reach the desired Architecture Capability. When this has been
defined, the organization can look for roles and employees that are able to contribute towards covering these skills,
tasks and responsibilities as described in UC2. While it may be difficult to achieve the desired Architecture Capability
without recruiting additional staff or hiring consultants – at least contemporarily - promoting existing employees can
both reduce costs and bring in long-term know-how and experience from within the organization. Having a clear
vision what skills, tasks and responsibilities can be covered the EASF can be used to identify gaps and develop a
training plan.
While none of these use cases (UC1-UC3) can be adequately addressed with the existing possibilities, like the
TOGAF 9.1 standard, we show in section 5.4, how the EASF supports these use cases.
3. Related Work
This chapter provides an overview on related work and summarises important sources of the EASF.
3.1. TOGAF Architecture Skills Framework
As a part of its Architecture Capability Framework, TOGAF provides an Architecture Skills Framework. The
Architecture Capability Framework is one of eight parts of the TOGAF 9.1 standard. It defines a set of roles and
attributes several sets of skills to these roles while classifying proficiency levels for each attribution (for an example
refer to Fig. 1). The numbers within the cells represent the proficiency level required by the roles in the respective
row applied to the respective skill.
As mentioned in the introduction, we identified several lacks within TOGAF’s Architecture Skills Framework.
While the framework provides nine roles, only one role is described specifically. The other roles are described in a
generic characterization. Another aspect of TOGAF’s Architecture Skills Framework presenting a lack is the fact, that
all of the tasks are assigned to all of the nine roles of the framework in matrices. This implies, that every role must at
least have a basic level in all presented skills. Fig. 1 provides an example of the mapping between the roles and skills
provided by TOGAF’s Architecture Skills Framework.

3.2. Skills Framework for the Information Age (SFIA 6)
The SFIA framework was established in 2000 and has been regularly updated and developed since then. It has
become «the globally accepted common language for the skills and competencies required in the digital world» [8].
SFIA 6 provides 6 categories, 13 sub-categories, 97 skills and a total 362 skill levels. SFIA 6 defines responsibility
with the four aspects Autonomy, Influence, Complexity and Business skills.
3.3. SFIA – c-CF Comparison & Review Mapping
SFIA has been used in other research to extend the framework e-CF (European Competency Framework) through
a comparisonand mappingbySeward [9].This mappingdoes however have a rather different scope. Bothframeworks
describe skills and competences without putting these into the scope of roles. Unlike SFIA or e-CF, TOGAF defines
skills and roles. Seward encountered the problem of mismatching levels and overlaps while mapping the
aforementioned frameworks, which makes a clear comparison very difficult [9]. Another finding raised by Seward is
the fact, that the mapping between frameworks requires a considerable amount of interpretation. Similar to Seward,
we also encountered the problem of mismatching levels and overlaps during our research.
3.4. Enterprise Architecture Career Path Development
The Federation of Enterprise Architecture Professional Organization (FEAPO) defines architectural roles
commonly recognized in organizations within its Guide to Careers in Enterprise Architecture [10][11]. FEAPO takes
a similar approach to defining EA roles by defining competencies, competency groups, roles and levels. These levels
are applied to competencies for the different roles. The aspects of tasks and responsibilities are however not explicitly
addressed. FEAPO also consideredTOGAF byemployingthe model ofthe Enterprise Architecture domains Business,
Applications, Information, Technology. However, the domain Security was added to this model, since FEAPO deems
TOGAF’s model to not consider this aspect sufficiently. SFIA was also considered by FEAPO by using the skill level
scale of SFIA containing seven skill levels. The naming and definition of the skills defined by FEAPO deviate from
the skills defined within SFIA. It would require additional work and analyses to identify overlaps, similarities and
differences between the skills defined by FEAPO and those defined by SFIA.
3.5. The Changing Role of the Enterprise Architect
Gøtze argues that there are different types of Enterprise Architects with quite different roles [12]. The author
mentions the three types Boundary-Focused Architect, Dialogic Architects and Enterprising Architects. The
Boundary-Focused Architects transmit, translate and transform knowledge across boundaries. These boundaries may
be between business silos, customer and vendor or between the classic business and IT boundary. The Dialogic

initiative leaders and condense the results of this into a strategic roadmap. Finally, the Enterprising Architects are

also human interaction, motivation and perception. The author argues, that the Enterprising Architects should achieve
this by bringing together «analytical mastery of architects and the intuitive originality of designers» in a mix of the
disciplines Enterprise Architecture and Design Thinking [12]. The author does however state, that the discipline of
Enterprise Architecture is evolving and the role of the Enterprise Architect is changing with it.
3.6. Role of the Enterprise Architect in the context of Business IT-Alignment
Wagner and Moshtaf [13] highlight the role ofthe Enterprise Architect inthe context of the Business-IT-Alignment
and IT-Governance processes. The authors state that «the Enterprise Architect is responsible for maintaining the
Enterprise Architecture that describes essential components of a business and their interrelationships» [13]. The
authors also state, that implementation of Enterprise Architecture is an endless, complex and interdisciplinaryprocess.
Therefore, organizations require highly skilled Enterprise Architects with various competencies. The research of
Wagner and Moshtaf has yielded a list of competencies, which were mentioned most frequently during their data
collection. Namely, these competencies are: Broad Work Experience, Business Process Design, Business Strategy,
Systems, Communication, Openness, Project Management, Technical Acumen, Design / Architecture Development IT
Strategy and Relationship Management. Similar to Gøtze the authors also state that the role of the Enterprise Architect
is changing.
4. Research Method
The Design Science Research [14][5] was applied to develop the EASF. For our work, we identified three research
questions. Research question 1 asks: «Which tasks do roles within enterprise architecture perform?» research question
2: «Which responsibilities are contained within enterprise architecture roles?» and research question 3: «Which skills
are required to carry out the tasks and responsibilities of the roles defined within enterprise architecture?».

5. EASF and EARF
Result of our work is the Extended Architecture Skills Framework (EASF) and an Enterprise Architecture Role
Fact Sheet (EARF). The EASF, which is basically a mapping between TOGAF and SFIA (described in section 5.1) is
recorded in an Excel workbook. The EARF is a template that provides the possibility to automatically generate a role
description based on this Excel workbook.
Several steps were taken to create the EASF and the EARF. This describes the approach and the delivery items.

5.1. Mapping between TOGAF and SFIA
The first step to the EASF was to create a mapping between TOGAF and SFIA. This step consisted of considering
skills defined by TOGAF and searching for a corresponding skill in SFIA. Due to the fact, that the Architecture Skills
Framework does not provide descriptions on what the mentioned skills include, this comparison mainly focused on
finding skills with identical or similar designations and analyzing the skill descriptions of SFIA. While some skills
designations such as Project Management exist in both frameworks, this is not the case for all skills defined by
TOGAF. The goal of this step was to find and match a skill in SFIA for every skill defined by TOGAF. The second
part consisted of mapping the skill levels of TOGAF and SFIA. Since the frameworks utilize different scales for the
skill levels, this had to be done individually for every skill. This mapping was performed for the skills within the
category Enterprise Architecture Skills defined in TOGAF, as the goal of the mapping was to provide a proof of
concept on how the mapping between TOGAF and SFIA can be achieved (see Fig. 3).
Fig. 3 Schematic illustration of the mapping between TOGAF and SFIA
This mapping posed an intermediary result and was reviewed by an expert involved in the development of SFIA
and member of the SFIA council (refer to chapter 5.5 for more information on the evaluation).
The skill level descriptions in SFIA resemble, what a role should be able to perform at a given skill level [16].
Based on this fact, these skill level descriptions were used to describe the tasks of the roles defined by TOGAF.
Apart from skills and skill levels, SFIA defines levels of responsibility. TOGAF generically describes
responsibilities of an Architect. Unlike the skills within TOGAF, these responsibilities are not mapped to the roles but
are limited to the previously mentioned generic descriptions. Again, a proof of concept was conducted to demonstrate,
how responsibilities of TOGAF and SFIA can be mapped. This proof of concept included the analysis of the
genericallydescribed responsibilities within TOGAF and mapping suitable levels of responsibility of SFIA consisting
of the categories Autonomy, Influence, Complexity and Business Skills. This mapping was performed by the role
Enterprise Architecture Manager defined by TOGAF. This proof of concept was also reviewed by the same expert as
in the previous mapping. Fig. 4 summarizes the previously described mappings.

5.2. Excel workbook of the EASF
The Excel Workbook of the EASF contains the mapping detailed in the previous section 5.1. It can be used for a
variety of purposes. Firstly, it supports the automated creation of the EA Role Fact Sheets. Then, it provides a broad
range from customization opportunities. This ranges from creating additional organization specific roles, modifying
the roles defined by TOGAF to specific needs of an organization to customizing the underlying mapping to modify
multiple roles at once.
5.3. EA Role Fact Sheet (EARF)
The EA Role Fact Sheet provides an automated possibility to generate a document-based report of the role
descriptions, which can be shared easily. It is based on the Excel workbook and therefore the EASF. The EARF
contains the role designation with possible synonymous role designations, a general description, an introduction, as
well as responsibilities, required skills and tasks. The EARF can be created for the roles defined by TOGAF and also
for custom roles created by users.
5.4. Improved Use Cases with EASF and EARF
By creating the EASF, the use cases described in chapter 2 can be improved in a structured manner.
UC1 Casting Enterprise Architecture Roles: The EARF provides a comprehensive and yet compact role
descriptionina document format, that canbe used inthe process ofcastingEA roles. It provides a simple and effective
way for the recruiting business department to describe their requirements towards candidates in terms of skills, tasks
and responsibilities.
UC2 Standardizing Skill Descriptions: Due to the fact that EASF is based on TOGAF and SFIA, it provides a
broad range of definitions on skills, tasks and responsibilities. EASF can therefore be used to describe many roles
within an organization in the field of Enterprise Architecture. EASF therefore enables organizations to describe their
EA roles in a standardized way, which enhances comparability and increases the quality of the role descriptions. In
addition to that, the role descriptions can be adapted to changes both in the environment but also towards the
developing skills and experience of the EA staff.
UC3 Developing Enterprise Architecture Capability: EASF can be used to define necessary skills, tasks and
responsibilities to achieve a desired Architecture Capability. In a second step, these skills, tasks and responsibilities
can be matched by promoting employees into EA roles. Whatever skills, tasks and responsibilities can’t be covered
with internal resources can be staffed by recruiting competent staff (described in UC1) or hiring the right consultants.
5.5. Evaluation
The evaluation of the EASF and the accompanying Excel Workbook as well as the EARF were evaluated at three
different occasions. Firstly, the lacks identified within TOGAF’s Architecture Skills Framework were evaluated in
interviews early on during the research process. These interviews were conducted with experts from the fields of
Enterprise Architecture and Recruiting. The findings gained fromthese interviews were used to design the EASF. The
second step in the evaluation consisted of an expert review. The skill mapping between TOGAF and SFIA was
reviewed by an expert involved in the development of SFIA. The EA Role Fact Sheet was evaluated for its usefulness
in a focus group workshop. The participants of the focus group were experts from the field of Enterprise Architecture.
6. Conclusion and Future Work
As shown up to our knowledge no approach exists that describes Enterprise Architecture roles with an explicit
linkage to the aspects tasks, responsibilities, skills and skill levels.
The lacks we identified within TOGAF’s Architecture Skills Framework are now addressed by EASF. Thanks to
the extension of TOGAF with SFIA, there are now detailed descriptions on skills, skill levels, tasks and
responsibilities. Therefore, we were able to deepen the definitions of TOGAF point out the implications of the skills

However, the EASF has been created without considering specific industries or organizations. Based on this fact

During the focus group workshop (see section 5.5), we found out that the roles, skills and skill levels assigned to

verifying this could provide more insights and potentially suggest improvements to TOGAF’s Architecture Skills

frameworks could complement and improve the EASF. An example of an additional framework that could be used
for this is e-CF.
