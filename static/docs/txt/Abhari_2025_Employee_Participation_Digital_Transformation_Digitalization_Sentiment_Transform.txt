A B S T R A C T
Digital transformation extends beyond mere technological shifts in business routines, models, or domains; it
encompasses the profound evolution of employees’ attitudes and behaviors. Prior research on the micro­
foundations of digital transformation emphasizes that the essence of transformation lies in the individuals who
drive it forward. To truly unlock this potential, organizations must harmonize sweeping changes with the in­
dividual needs and professional aspirations of their workforce. Achieving this balance is crucial to mitigating
resistance and ensuring successful transformation. This study offers an empirical examination of how these
dynamics unfold in practice. It begins by theorizing that employee sentiment toward digitalization is a key
predictor of their predispositions toward digital transformation. The findings demonstrate that positive pre­
dispositions, in turn, can significantly enhance employees’ willingness to participate in digital transformation
initiatives. This study sheds light on the individual microfoundations that underpin employee participation in
digital transformation, revealing that these drivers are rooted in fundamental psychological needs, transcending
geographical and cultural boundaries. The findings advocate for investments in employees’ psychosocial read­
iness and suggest viewing digitalization projects as strategic opportunities to cultivate positive experiences and
attitudes, paving the way for future transformation initiatives.
1. Introduction
Digital transformation is reshaping our society and redefining our
businesses, yet it remains a concept shrouded in complexity, resisting
clear definition, strategic lucidity, and any guarantee of success [1]. In
this study, we define digital transformation as the strategic integration
of digital technologies to fundamentally redefine an organization’s
structure, functions, and employee dynamics, ultimately enhancing its
identity, value proposition, and overall competitiveness. Throughout
the course of digital transformation, an organization mobilizes its stra­
tegic resources to reinvent its business processes, introduce new busi­
ness models, or launch new product and service categories, redefining
value creation, delivery, and capture in response to both internal pres­
sures and external opportunities [2,3]. However, this transformation has
been characterized by constant disruption, rejection of established
norms, costly experimentations, and even failures [4–8]. Understanding
these turbulent dynamics requires us to dive into the microfoundations of
digital transformation, which highlights that the success of these ini­
tiatives is deeply rooted in individual factors such as behaviors, com­
petencies, performance, and interpersonal interactions [9–13]. Among
the various microfoundational factors and views, this study focuses on
employee participation and its drivers [14].
Emerging microfoundation scholarship posits that systemic organi­
zational change is inherently driven by the dynamic actions and in­
teractions of individual actors within the organization [15]. This
perspective highlights the pivotal role of active employee participation,
wherein individual behaviors collectively forge the trajectory of orga­
nizational transformation [16]. Encouraging employee participation not
only empowers individual employees for successful implementation
[17–19] but also enhances the legitimacy, efficacy, and longevity of the
changes [20–22]. Employees are also profoundly affected as their roles
and routines continuously evolve to meet transformation demands [23].
Neglecting these dynamics, we contend, can lead to employee resistance
and even transformation failure.
Yet, organizations persist in treating digital transformation as a top-
down initiative, favoring macro-level strategies and technological inte­
gration whileneglecting the microfoundations of change(e.g., [24–26]).
This macro-centric bias overlooks the cumulative impact of individual
actions, attitudes, and adaptive behaviors, ultimately weakening trans­
formation efforts by failing to engage the very individuals who drive

adequate employee participation, especially from lower-level managers,
in planning, implementation, and governance [29–32]. We argue that
these challenges arise from mischaracterizations of microfoundations in
digital transformation, particularly four oversimplifications regarding
employee participation:
The Fallacy of Engineering Participation. Digital transformation stra­
tegies often misconstrue participation as a controllable output or oper­
ational condition [2,14,23], assuming it can be manufactured through
structured interventions, including incentives, training programs,
change champions, integrated communication, and procedural en­
hancements [33,34]. While these mechanisms may generate
surface-level compliance, they remain constrained by how individuals
interpret and respond to change [15,35]. This explains why current
research struggles to account for the divergent participation responses
among employees who have equal access to these interventions and
resources [36]. This oversight also leads research to misdiagnose resis­
tance and disengagement as relational failures rather than recognizing
them as manifestations of deep-seated attitudinal microfoundations
[37].1
The Oversimplification of Attitudinal Influence. Digital transformation
literature often reduces participation to observable behaviors, over­
looking the deeply ingrained attitudes that shape them [11]. This
oversight persists despite strong evidence that attitude toward change
fundamentally shapes how individualsinterpret and engagewith change
[38]. Employees do not approach change initiatives neutrally; their
willingness to participate is shaped by deeply ingrained beliefs, values,
and past experiences [39]. Before engaging, employees interpret the
change initiative, filtering it through existing mindsets and beliefs [37].
These internalized perspectives determine whether the transformationis
seen as an opportunity or a threat, a logical progression or an unsettling
disruption. This blind spot leads organizations to misinterpret resistance
to participation as a situational response rather than recognizing it as a
manifestation of long-standing attitudes and ingrained perspectives
[12]. We argue that effectively addressing participation necessitates
engaging with these underlying attitudinal inclinations—referred to as
predisposition in this study.
The Marginalization of Emotional Influence. Until recently, research
has primarily focused on cognitive explanations, often neglecting the
critical influence of emotions on participation [11,34]. Yet, attitudinal
predispositions are not purely rational; they are deeply emotional. If
employees are not emotionally inclined to embrace digital trans­
formation, no external motivator, however compelling, can genuinely
engage them [40]. Recent studies highlight how digital transformation
often induces technostress, anxiety, fatigue, mistrust, and frustration
[11,41]. These experience-driven emotions do not merely influence how
employees feel about transformation—they actively shape how they
behave toward the change [42]. We contend that ignoring these
emotional predispositions, especially before implementation, can foster
disengagement and fuel resistance, stalling innovation and trans­
formation [43].
The Underestimation of Sentiment Shaped by Past Experiences. Digital
transformation literature primarily examines behaviors during and after
transformation, failing to acknowledge how past digital experiences
shape sentiment—an accumulated appraisal of digital change [44,45].
Although we have made significant strides in understanding the opti­
mization, normalization and stabilization of digital transformation
[46–48], we still struggle to unravel the dynamics that occur
beforehand. Employees do not respond to change in isolation from their
past experiences; rather, their perceptions are filtered through a history
of interactions with digitalization. While digitalization—the gradual
adoption of digital technologies to enhance efficiency—lacks the
breadth, depth, and transformative power of digital transformation, it
often serves as a precursor. By shaping employees’ technological con­
fidence, expectations, and competencies, digitalization lays the
groundwork for more profound organizational shifts [49,50]. Em­
ployees experiencing disruption or inadequate support in prior digita­
lization efforts develop skepticism toward digital transformation due to
past frustrations [51]. Thus, we argue, employee sentiment, shaped by
past experiences, is a key determinant of participation.
Although these four mischaracterizations and oversimplifications
have been recently challenged, their critique and examination seldom
extend beyond theoretical discussions, leaving us with conceptual
speculation rather than empirical solutions [52,53]. Hence, we return to
the pivotal research question of what individual microfoundations shape
employee participation in digital transformation. Specifically, we
examine whether attitudinal predispositions—cognitive and affective­
—and experiences act as determinants and to what extent they influence
employees’ willingness to participate.
To address this question, we move beyond single-context studies and
high-level abstractions, instead centering individual employees as the
unit of analysis in two different contexts. We also focus on employees
and lower managers, as recent studies observed that their attitude to­
ward participation in digital transformation falls behind that of upper
and top management [54]. We also focus on digital transformation
“initiatives” versus digital transformation in a general sense as ongoing
processes. Narrowing our focus, we characterize the “DT initiative” as a
change initiative with clear business goals and a start date at the
introduction or early implementation stage and before the routinization
of new work practices or normalization of changes [34].2
Organizations
depend highly on employees’ participation and feedback at this crucial
and relatively unstable early stage. This approach also allows us to
collect comparable evidence using a cross-sectional rather than a lon­
gitudinal study [55].
By focusing on individual microfoundational influences, we devel­
oped our hypotheses based on a temporal experience appraisal (TEA)
model informed by the stimulus–organism–response (S-O-R) paradigm.
S-O-R posits that behavior is shaped by external stimuli processed
through internal cognitive and emotional mechanisms [56,57].
Accordingly, we argue that employee sentiment toward digitalization
(stimulus) shapes their current predispositions toward digital trans­
formation (organism), which, in turn, influences their future willingness
to participate in DT initiatives (response). Here, we define employee
predispositions toward digital transformation as employees’ attitudinal
inclination (cognitive and affective) to digitally enabled and strategic
organizational changes. Employee sentiment toward digitalization is
conceptualized as an attitudinal appraisal of experiences with digital
technologies. We operationalize employee sentiment toward digitali­
zation based on need-based expectations and self-determination (i.e.,
employee behavior is driven by the need for autonomy,competence, and
relatedness; [58]). Last, employee willingness to participate in DT ini­
tiatives is modeled as the intention to be engaged in and contribute to
digital transformation planning and implementation beyond job de­
scriptions and organizational mandates.
We tested our hypotheses in the context of medium to large-sized
enterprises in two countries— the United States and Vietnam—by ac­
counting for situational, organizational and individual factors. Our
findings revealed positive relationships between three dimensions of
employee sentiment toward digitalization and their predispositions

2. Theoretical background
Digital transformation has been explored through various perspec­
tives, yet it remains inherently complex and continuously evolving,
resisting a universally accepted definition [2,59–61]. This intellectual
fragmentation has not only diversified interpretations but also intro­
duced intricacies that significantly hinder scholarly inquiry and prac­
tical applications [1,30,62]. Moreover, the conceptual boundaries of
digital transformation have become increasingly ambiguous [63],
compounding the challenge of navigating its theoretical complexities
[60]. Consensus is gradually forming that digital transformation in­
volves more than adopting new technologies and touches nearly every
facet of a business [2,34,64,65]. This perspective on digital trans­
formation underscores its distinction from digitalization in terms of
scale, scope, and impact. While digitalization focuses primarily on
enhancing existing products, services, or processes with digital capa­
bilities, it generally does not fundamentally redefine the organization’s
value proposition or identity [3]. Therefore, while all DT initiatives
encompass elements of digitalization, not all instances of digitalization
can be considered transformational.
In this study, we recognize digital transformation as the strategic
integration and application of digital technologies3
[66]. We also
acknowledge that digital transformation is more than simply adopting
new technologies or digitalization of certain business processes; it in­
volves profound changes in organizational structure and functions [1],
recognizing its microfoundations, including employee actions and in­
teractions [10]. Bringing these elements together, we define digital
transformation as the strategic integration of digital technologies to
fundamentally redefine an organization’s structure, functions, and
employee dynamics, ultimately enhancing its identity, value proposi­
tion, and overall competitiveness. This operational definition establishes
clearer conceptual boundaries for our investigation, aligning with the
requirements outlined by Plekhanov et al. [61] and Mühlburger and
Krumay [66]—integrating the strategic, technological, and processual
elements. More importantly, it includes employee dynamics and un­
derscores the necessity of examining employee participation, a critical
yet often fragmented aspect in the existing literature.
2.1. Fragmented perspectives on employee participation in digital
transformation
Digital transformation is associated with a high failure rate4
in
delivering expected outcomes, predicted return on investment, tangible
benefits at scale, or even employee acceptance [12,29,67,68]. Many
instances of failure exhibit common employee-related factors. Employee
participation, inadequate support for employees, poor employee
collaboration, and lack of accountability among employees are the
common human factors behind this high failure rate [69–71]. Previous
studies argued that digital transformation is centered on the participa­
tion of employees who are both directed and affected by changes [72].
Nevertheless, as Kane [30], p. 46] affirmed, “It’s far too easy in the rush
to implement a new technology to forget about the importance of em­
ployees in making it work.” Neglecting employee participation can
foster resistance to change, a key impediment to successful DT initiatives
[2,30,73,74].
The existing literature offers valuable, albeit fragmented, insights
into employee participation. These insights align with three distinct
theoretical lenses: the technology-centric, the strategy-centric, and the
process-centric, outlined in Appendix A and summarized in Table 1.
While each perspective illuminates specific facets of employee partici­
pation, none fully encapsulates its multifaceted nature when viewed in
isolation. This variation stems from how different perspectives prioritize
distinct factors as key enablers or barriers to employee participation in
digital transformation—see Appendix B.
The strategy-centric view highlights organizational and managerial
barriers, emphasizing leadership, governance, and strategic misalign­
ment [12,13,23,75,76]. Meanwhile, the technology-centric and
process-centric perspectives focus more on processual, relational, and
individual factors, particularly how employees receive technological
changes and navigate the evolving transformation process (e.g., [10,42,
44,77]). These perspectives often address the participation problem by
demonstrating the effectiveness of interventions (e.g., [14,31]) and by
explainingthe lackof participationthrough mechanisticpatterns suchas
routines, adoption rates, and resistance (e.g., [78,79]) or competencies
such as literacies and technical skills (e.g., [33,80])—while overlooking
the deeper sociotechnical dimensions of participation [5,9]. Moreover,
they seldom account for the historical or cultural contexts that shape
employee attitudes toward transformation [81,82], instead treating
participation as either a functional necessity or a behavioral response to
external interventions and incentives [1]. A more nuanced under­
standing necessitates a shift toward the individuality of participation
and its microfoundations (e.g., [11,83]), recognizing that employees’
participation is not merely a reaction to imposed change but an active,
contextually driven process shaped by their experiences, perceptions,
and mindsets [84].
2.2. Employee participation as a microfoundation of digital
transformation
A microfoundation refers to the underlying individual-level factors
or elements that collectively drive broader organizational or economic
phenomena [16]. Understanding microfoundations enables organiza­
tions to analyze how individual employees interact with change pro­
cesses, providing insights into the foundational elements that drive
organizational agility, adaptability, and success [85]. Iden and Bygstad
[9] argue that the effective implementation of digital transformation
requires a deeper understanding of its microfoundations. These micro­
foundations include employee agency, skills and knowledge, compe­
tence and experience, employee engagement and participation,
interactions and relational dynamics, and feedback and learning pro­
cesses [15]. As such, microfoundations encompass both the cognitive
and behavioral aspects of individuals within an organization, which
together form the bedrock of dynamic capabilities and other organiza­
tional routines driving large-scale transformations [86]. Moreover, the
high failure rate of DT initiatives is increasingly attributed to the neglect

2.2.1. Definition and manifestations of employee participation in digital
transformation
Employee behavioral contribution to change initiatives has been
traditionally conceptualized as “participation” and studied from
different perspectives, such as employee engagement [87–89],
employee involvement [90], employee commitment [91,92], and
employee voice [93]. The literature on these concepts highlights that
sustained and meaningful individual contributions to the change
initiative are essential for organizational transformation [38,94].
Employee participation is defined as activities performed by em­
ployees in an observable, behavioral, and action-based manner [95,96].
This definition should be clearly distinguished from commitment,
involvement, and engagement. Commitment, though often expressed by
employees, does not necessarily lead to active involvement in the
change process, thereby limiting its reliability as a predictor of actual
behavior [97,98]. Involvement is a subjective psychological state
reflecting the relevance of an object or event; it is internal, cognitive,
and emotional, indicating significance without necessarily requiring
active engagement [96]. Engagement, regarded as a deeper form of
involvement, is difficult to quantify due to its complex emotional and
cognitive dimensions, making it an unreliable standalone predictor [89,
99]. In contrast, participation is a tangible concept rendering observable
behaviors and direct contributions [95]. Unlike commitment, which
reflects intent, or involvement, which signifies connection, participation
is action-oriented and measurable, making it a more reliable predictor.5
Employee participation during change initiatives is contextual and
can manifest in various ways, ranging from contributions to planning
and implementation to co-governance [81,93,100–102]. Employee
participation in digital transformation may include, for example, direct
contributions (e.g., defining new goals), feedback (e.g., voicing opin­
ions), and cross-functional support (e.g., operational or technical sup­
port). Encouraging employee participation in these opportunities at any
time creates an environment where they possess increased sense of
ownership over change initiatives [34]. However, employee participa­
tion during the early stages of digital transformation is even more
critical, as it shapes both the direction and receptivity of change
initiatives.
2.2.2. Importance of employee participation in the initial stages of digital
transformation
Employee participation in the initial stages of a DT initiative is vital
to its success. First, this importance arises from the fact that, as with any
organizational change effort, the dynamics of change are most fluid and
open to influence during the early phases [103,104]. Engaging em­
ployees from the outset allows them to actively shape the transformation
process, aligning it with their needs and expectations and fostering a
sense of ownership [105,106]. This sense of ownership is crucial, as it
not only enhances commitment but also fosters better adoption and
reduces resistance later [107]. Second, the early stages of trans­
formation are often marked by heightened resistance to change, tem­
porary declines in performance, internal conflicts, and technological
setbacks [79]. By engaging employees from the outset, organizations
can proactively address challenges and mitigate risks by leveraging
bottom-up, localized solutions. This early participation not only ensures
a smoother transition but also instills a strong sense of ownership, which
is essential for the long-term efficiency and sustainability of the
transformation.
In contrast, once changes are fully implemented and normalized
within an organization, they often become routinized, turning into fixed
procedures that employees must follow rather than dynamic processes
they can resist or influence [47]. This routinization significantly de­
creases opportunities for meaningful participation after the initial
stages, making early participation even more critical. As these routines
become entrenched, employees tend to view them as non-negotiable
aspects of their work environment, which stifles participation and
curbs innovation potential [45]. When employees are reduced to mere
executors of predetermined processes, their ability to contribute to
continuous improvement and adaptability is severely restricted. More­
over, failing to involve employees early can result in a lack of buy-in,
causing some employees to limit their efforts to the minimum for as
long as possible [23,108,109]. This disengagement can spread, with
these employees discouraging their peers from participating and, in case
of failure, placing blame on the transformation process and its executors
rather than taking responsibility themselves. This underscores the
importance of prioritizing employee participation in the early stages of
digital transformation, where securing participation can most influence
success and sustainability.

relevant. This paradigm provides distinct advantages; it provides a

The S-O-R paradigm and, by extension, the TEA model, suggest that
the willingness of employees to participate in DT initiatives depends on
how they perceive and evaluate its potential values [113]. The internal
processing (organism) during digital transformation involves an inter­
play of cognitive and affective factors. Employees form gist-based
representations rather than comprehensive analyses, relying on fuzzy
traces related to their schemas about possible impact and attitudes to­
ward change. These fuzzy, gist-based mental models, rather than
detailed assessments [57], primarily shape behavioral responses
through attitudinal activation. This highlights the role of subjective,
imprecise interpretations in mediating the link between the organiza­
tional stimulus and employee response. Notwithstanding the established
influence of individual factors on change perception and readiness
[114], the attitude toward a change initiative is fundamentally predi­
cated upon this gist-based, subjective appraisal of the potential benefits
or the anticipated absence thereof [115]. Thus, we argue that em­
ployees’ willingness to participate in DT initiatives during its early
stages of introduction is influenced more by their intrinsic tendencies
and the subjective mental images they form about the change than by
their goals or motivations [116–118]. This concept refers to the pre­
dispositions in this study, which are learned yet subjective7
and can lead
to approach or avoidance behaviors [119].
2.3.1. Employees’ predispositions toward digital transformation
Stouten et al. [38] argued that employees’ predispositions toward
change reflect their desire (attitudinal inclination) for change and their
responses to change initiatives. Therefore, we expect that the pre­
dispositions of employees signal their willingness to participate in
change and, collectively, reflect the organization’s general readiness for
transformation. Previous studies explained predispositions as “change
orientation” as a proxy factor for employees’ willingness to adapt to
change (e.g., [120,121]). From this perspective, predispositions stem
from how employees perceive the positive outcomes of change,
including their self-gains, or the negative outlook of change, including
self-threats (e.g., [116,120,122]). This perception is predominantly
influenced by personal work experiences, which serve as the most
readily accessible and subconsciously trusted reference point when
activating attitudes. For example, negative experiences in similar
contexts can lead to dispositional resistance, the tendency to reject
change [38], which, in turn, discourages participation in change ini­
tiatives [118,123].
Moreover, prior studies accounted for both cognitive and emotional
predispositions. Cognitive predispositions are associated with how an
employee justifies or rejects the rational aspects of change [124]. DT
initiatives can cognitively confront employees to think critically about
the benefits and threats of changes and accordingly rethink their current
work practices to develop new competencies to navigate the change
process. The outcome of this cognitive process leads to the development
of employees’ cognitive predispositions, which are the logical positions
of individual employees toward a new DT initiative [34]. In other words,
cognitive predispositions render how employees rationalize their posi­
tive or negative perception of the change prospects. This rationalization
can occur subconsciously and automatically but can be articulated by
employees during doxastic reflection, where they consciously evaluate
and express their beliefs about the change [125,126].
In contrast, we have emotional predispositions associated with the
emotional evaluation of change initiatives [122]. Emotional pre­
dispositions can be positive (e.g., when employees expect less stress in
decision-making due to advanced analytics) or negative (e.g., when
employees feel apprehensive about automation projects). Pre­
dispositions motivated by these experiences, regardless of polarity,
encourage employees to become involved in organizational change
initiatives and maximize their respective emotional gain [122,124].
Therefore, while emotional predispositions cannot necessarily be
rationalized, they can affect employees’ perceptions and reactions to
change initiatives.
Although employees have diverse and often contradictory in­
clinations toward both change and digital technology [108], how these
predispositions form and what influences them remains uncertain. We
argue that employee predispositions toward digital transformation
cannot be fully understood without first identifying their underlying
stimuli. If sufficient stimuli are present to support appraisal, employees
can establish or modify predispositions that later affect their behavioral
intentions. This consideration naturally leads us to ask: what constitutes
a stimulus in the digital transformation context, even before these ini­
tiatives are introduced, routines are implemented, and their outcomes
are realized?
2.3.2. The role of previous digitalization experiences
A DT initiative, in and of itself, rarely functions as a direct stimulus
due to its inherent technical complexity, dynamic evolution, and
contextual unpredictability. Its value and implications are often opaque
at the outset, making immediate comprehension unlikely upon initial
introduction. This raises a fundamental challenge: how can employees
form judgments about a transformation that has yet to materialize? The
TEA model suggests that they rely on anticipatory cues and prior en­
counters with similar changes to shape their predispositions. These early
impressions, in turn, influence their engagement with the DT initiative
as it unfolds. To explore this process, we turn to digitalization ini­
tiatives—a related but narrower phenomenon—where similar dynamics
emerge, albeit with a more limited scope and impact. Digitalization, in
this context, refers to the adoption and application of digital technolo­
gies to enhance products, services, or processes without fundamentally
altering an organization’score structure, key value offerings, or inherent
identity [49,50].
We posit that digitalization is the primary frame of reference avail­
able to employees prior to the introduction of DT initiatives [83].
Digitalization cues, functioning as external stimuli (external to DT
initiative), shape employees’ predispositions by activating their cogni­
tive and affective processing mechanisms. According to Young [57],
these cues are inherently “fuzzy” sentiments derived from prior expe­
riences, schemas, and attitudes rather than precise evaluations, yet they
play a pivotal role in shaping employees’ mental processing (organism)
within the framework. We offer three reasons supporting this claim.

2.3.3. Employee sentiments toward digitalization
Digitalization can, for example, help employees feel empowered,
valued, and connected; conversely, they can feel limited, underappre­
ciated, and disconnected. Employees reflect on these experiences to
rationalize their views and set their expectations for further applications
of digital technologies [133]. Reflections (attitudinal appraisals) yield
subjective sentiments [38]. However, the question is why these senti­
ments matter. We claim that employees’ sentiments toward digitaliza­
tion can indirectly encourage or discourage their participation in digital
transformation [34,134]. Employees may embrace digitalization if
previous efforts streamlined their work and yielded positive results
[135]. These positive experiences can lead employees to be more in­
clined to engage positively with similar initiatives [67].
We conceptualize employee sentiments toward digitalization as an
“attitudinal appraisal” of digitalization (cf., [135]), rising from the
subjective comparison between need-based expectations and past ex­
periences [136]. This appraisal reflects how employees believe their
needs have been met in work environments during and after digitali­
zation [137]. Employee sentiment, therefore, consists of two key com­
ponents: the nature of the needs and the perceived fulfillment of these
needs. We contend that the variation in employee sentiment is primarily
driven by the latter—how needs have been satisfied—since the former,
the needs themselves, are relatively universal across employees, as
supported by organizational psychology and theories such as
self-determination theory (e.g., [58,138,139]). Thus, if we can
effectively model these universal needs, we can better assess how em­
ployees perceive their fulfillment through past digitalization efforts.
At a high level, we characterize employee need-based expectations
using self-determination theory [58,140,141]. This theory helps capture
a spectrum of employee needs through a universal and comprehensive
framework. It offers a widely accepted model of needs with high pre­
dictive power, applicable across various contexts, including modern
workplaces [58,139,142]. Additionally, it offers deeper insights into the
psychological mechanisms that shape employee behavior, providing a
nuanced understanding of how lived experiences influence attitudes
[143]. Furthermore, the adaptability of the theory across different
organizational settings makes it a robust and versatile tool for analyzing
employee sentiment in our context [58].
2.3.4. The dimensions of employee sentiments toward digitalization
From the self-determination perspective, employees perceive digi­
talization as a means to fulfill or frustrate their core professional needs
regarding autonomy, competence, and relatedness [58,139,142]. These
needs are fundamental to maintaining a positive attitude and achieving
optimal performance in various contexts, including organizational set­
tings [58]. Consistent with earlier findings, this theory allows us to
understand the individual expectations employees have regarding
digitalization [144]. For instance, Meske and Junglas [109] demon­
strated that employees’ support for digital workplace transformation is
influenced by their expectations regarding autonomy, competence, and
connectedness. Additionally, earlier research has highlighted similar
factors such as mindset, competence, and relationships [145] as well as
mindset, innovation culture, and common language [59] as critical
drivers of digital transformation readiness.
In addition to its universal relevance and theoretical alignment, self-
determination theory enables us to better capture the variation in
employee sentiment, particularly the negative impacts of digitalization.
For instance, employees may fear losing control over their re­
sponsibilities to intelligent agents (lack of autonomy), feel threatened by
job replacement due to decision augmentation (lack of competence), or
experience diminished interpersonal connections due to job automation
(lack of relatedness) as artificial intelligence and other technologies
continue to advance [146,147]. These negative sentiments precede
negative predispositions toward digital transformation, ultimately
contributing to employees’ general reluctance to participate in DT ini­
tiatives [33].
Although the universality of the needs for autonomy, competence,
and relatedness is generally accepted [148], it is unclear whether their
nature and dimensionality vary depending on the context of digitaliza­
tion. Prior research has often approached employee needs in relatively
straightforward terms, typically linking autonomy to empowerment,
competence to learning, and relatedness to connection with colleagues
[149]. However, we contend that these models, while valuable, only
begin to address the complex dynamics involved. First, employees
expect digital technologies not only to preserve their sense of agency but
also to provide opportunities for creativity, such as through
problem-solving and innovation [150,151]. This expectation extends
beyond simple empowerment and is embodied in concepts such as

3. Model development
Building on the significance of employee predispositions and the
possible influence of employee sentiment toward digitalization, this
study theorizes experience-driven participation in digital trans­
formation. Grounded in the S-O-R paradigm and the TEA model, we
posit that employee sentiment, shaped by prior experiences with digi­
talization, exerts a direct influence on their predispositions toward
digital transformation, which in turn drives their willingness to partic­
ipate in DT initiatives. The impact of digitalization on three fundamental
psychological needs—autonomy, competence, and relatedness—is used
to model employee sentiment. By assessing how digitalization either
satisfies or frustrates these needs, we can predict the formation of pos­
itive or negative sentiments among employees. We conceptualize these
needs as higher-order constructs, each with two dimensions, respec­
tively: empowerment and innovation, learning and adaptability, and
cooperation and communication. Fig. 2 illustrates the relationships be­
tween these key constructs, including control variables. The model
captures employee willingness to participate in DT initiatives at a spe­
cific point in time per the TEA model, considering temporal dimensions
across three stages: the past, by assessing experience-driven sentiment
anchored to events before digital transformation, the present, by
examining current predispositions toward digital transformation, and
the future, by studying willingness to participate going forward.
In our theorization, employee participation is not merely an outcome
of external interventions such as training, incentives, or mandates but is
deeply rooted in prior digitalization experiences and the subsequent
cognitive and emotional processes that unfold as they evaluate a new DT
initiative. In other words, previous digitalization experiences, though
external to the current DT initiative, serve as the primary catalyst for
shaping employee attitudes toward digital transformation. Often, these
past digitalization experiences are the only reference points employees
have for doxastic rationalization [162] that lead them to either embrace
or resist the change [54]. For example, when employees successfully use
digital tools to manage projects autonomously and innovatively, they
develop positive predispositions toward future change initiatives driven
by similar technologies, On the contrary, if digitalization through en­
terprise social networks fail to improve communication and coopera­
tion, it becomes difficult to convince employees that a larger-scale DT
initiative could address these issues.
3.1. Employee sentiment toward digitalization and its outcome
Previous studies suggest that workplace autonomy significantly in­
fluences employees’ predispositions to change [163,164]. Digitalization
can have positive and negative impacts on the degree to which indi­
vidual employees experience the freedom to act and initiate. For
instance, digitalization efforts that include automation often aim to
simplify work practices, streamline processes, and either centralize or
decentralize organizational decision-making [147,165]. These results of
digitalization can influence the employee’s sense of autonomy in two
major ways: by improving their agency in making independent decisions
and by providing opportunities to explore innovative solutions [42,
109]. Bothconcepts, empowerment and innovation,are deeply rootedin
fundamental psychological needs [140].
Empowerment refers to having the agency to take responsibility and
act independently, stemming from individual needs for power and a
sense of control. Ideally, digitalization can empower employees by
allowing them to experience alternative work routines, prioritize tasks,
and personalize their workflows. However, empowerment should not be
assumed to be an automatic outcome of all digitalization initiatives.
While digitalization can empower employees to take on more re­
sponsibility by streamlining processes and increasing efficiency, it can
also limit their ability to act independently. For example, digitalization
can lead to increased decision augmentation and employee monitoring,
undermining employee autonomy [165].
Innovation is the freedom to explore, experiment and create through
independent action, driven by individual needs for creativity and a sense
of novelty. Digitalization can promote or hinder autonomous innova­
tion. The freedom to innovate liberates employees and helps them
achieve goals; consequently, this positively shapes employees’ attitudes
toward more strategic use of digital technologies [166,167]. While
digital tools can empower employees by offering new avenues for cre­
ative problem-solving, they can also disrupt traditional methods of
innovation and constrain employees’ ability to apply personal creativity
and judgment in crafting innovative solutions. These limitations can

Digitalization has the potential to satisfy employees’ fundamental
need for competence by providing more efficient avenues to achieve
their goals. Competence development through digitalization manifests
itself in two key forms: learning and adaptability. First, employees seek
to fulfill their need for competence by pursuing learning opportunities
such as acquiring knowledge and developing skills. These experiences
may arise directly from digitalization or indirectly as its by-products
(learning to use an AI assistant versus learning from an AI assistant).
Certain digitalization initiatives enhance employee mastery in the role
by enhancing their learning and decision-making [168,169]. This sense
of mastery encourages them to embrace the larger adoption of tech­
nology. For example, digitalization projects can equip employees with
the knowledge needed to effectively use analytical tools, thereby
improving their problem-solving abilities [170]. Nevertheless, digitali­
zation can also limit learning opportunities when augmentation and
automation take over, requiring employees to follow directives without
engaging in problem-solving, critical thinking, or skill development.
Second, the need for competence extends beyond mere learning to
include the ability to navigate and respond to dynamic work environ­
ments [171]. Previous research particularly highlighted that compe­
tence involves the ability to adapt to and manage change [172].
Leveraging digitalization, employees can develop adaptability as a core
aspect of competence, enabling them to effectively manage and respond
to change, navigate uncertainty, and continuously adjust to evolving
work demands [155]. For example, introducing a new customer moni­
toring system may increase employee preparedness by allowing them to
quickly access necessary information and respond efficiently.
When employees exhibit both learning and adaptability, they are
more likely to develop positive sentiment toward digitalization, largely
because of an enhanced sense of growth and achievement. As a result,
they develop positive cognitive predispositions by forming associations
between digital tools and professional growth. This cognitive appraisal,
grounded in prior learning and rationalization of self-efficacy, shapes
expectations that future technologies will likely contribute to skill
enhancement and task mastery. Concurrently, emotional predispositions
may arise from feelings of accomplishment and enhanced adaptability,
reinforcing the inclination to engage with digital transformation. This
realization helps employees assess the potential effects of DT initiatives,
leading them to embrace and capitalize on their benefits [107].
Conversely, when digitalization does not foster competence develop­
ment, employees may cognitively appraise these experiences as mis­
aligned with growth opportunities, leading to perceptions of stagnation
and diminished self-efficacy. These perceptions can also trigger frus­
tration and negative emotions such as self-doubt, which, in turn, in­
crease the likelihood of skepticism and resistance toward digital
transformation. Therefore, we expect the following:
H1b: Employee sentiment toward digitalization, in terms of com­
petence—learning and adaptability—affects employee predispositions
toward digital transformation.

The third aspect of employee sentiment, relatedness, stems from the
fundamental need for connection and meaningful relationships with
others. In this context, relatedness refers to employees’ reactions to
whether digitalization has improved their ability to connect with and
relate to their peers. This can be explored through two key dimensions:
communication and cooperation. Communication is the need for clear,
open, and effective information exchanges with others. Some digitali­
zation initiatives meet this need by fostering inter-departmental in­
teractions, encouraging knowledge exchange throughout the
organization [173]. For example, shared digital workspaces can help
employees identify common values and build meaningful relationships
with colleagues [109]. These interactions promote familiarity, trans­
parency, and networking, collectively fulfilling employees’ needs for
relatedness [174]. When employees experience enhanced communica­
tion, they are more likely to develop a positive sentiment toward the
continued use of digital technology [175]. On the other hand, when
open and direct communication is blocked by digitalization, employees
can experience feelings of isolation, frustration, and disengagement,
leading to a decrease in trust in digital tools and a reluctance to embrace
further DT initiatives.
Digitalization can also strengthen cooperation by facilitating the
formation of meaningful relationships and a sense of belonging within
communities of practice [176]. Employees may also use digital tools to
engage with customers and external stakeholders, co-creating solutions
that benefit all parties [177]. When employees build relationships
through technology, they are more likely to trust each other and the
digital tools that support these interactions [109]. Increased trust leads
to greater knowledge sharing, a key objective of digitalization [147,
178]. When digital technology fails to connect employees or isolates
them, it can create negative cognitive and emotional predispositions
toward using it systematically [134].
Cognitive predispositions are shaped as employees evaluate whether
digital tools effectively support collaboration and enable efficient
knowledge exchange. Affective predispositions, on the other hand, stem
from the emotional consequences of connection or disconnection, such
as feelings of inclusion, trust, or isolation. For example, positive relat­
edness sentiment can foster psychological safety [179,180], leading to
more favorable emotional predisposition toward DT initiatives.
Conversely, when digitalization obstructs connections and interactions,
employees are more likely to develop negative predispositions. Such
impediments can manifest cognitively as a burgeoning skepticism
regarding the instrumental value of digital technology and affectively as
heightened states of frustration or digital alienation, thereby eroding
favorable predispositions toward digital transformation. Therefore, we
hypothesize:
H1c: Employee sentiment toward digitalization in terms of rela­
tedness—communication and cooperation—affects employee pre­
dispositions toward digital transformation.
3.2. Employee willingness to participate in digital transformation and its
drivers
Based on Weiner’s [181] view of change readiness, we theorize that
employees’ willingness to participate in DT initiatives is influenced by
their predispositions toward digital transformation in general. This is
rooted in social and environmental psychology, where past experiences
influence future decision-making or attitudes [182] and is supported by
digital transformation literature [67]. According to the TEA model,
predispositions toward digital transformation act as an “organism” that
mediates the relationship between employee sentiment toward
digitalization and employee willingness to participate. The response,
here, is shaped by doxastic rationalization, where the appraisal of past
experiences functions as attitudinal activation, thereby guiding future
behaviors [183,184].
Doxastic rationalization typically involves both rational, objective
justifications and emotional, affective responses that underpin an in­
dividual’s belief or attitude [105,120,185]. This helps employees
rationalize their willingness to participate based on the practical and
tangible benefits they anticipate. This attitude encourages the openness
of employees to change; therefore, they can show a stronger willingness
to participate in change proactively [186,187] and in future technology
implementation [107]. Cognitive reasons often stem from utilitarian
values that emphasize maximizing utility, productivity and effectiveness
[188].
Conversely, doxastic rationalization can also be driven by emotional,
affective justifications that influence an employee’s willingness to
participate [189,190]. Employees can rationalize their participation
based on the positive emotions they associate with the changes [183]. If
they experience joy or excitement at the prospect of using new tech­
nologies or engaging in novel working methods, these emotions can
strongly influence their rationalization [188]. The importance of these
hedonic values in justifying technology use is also well-documented
[191]. Similarly, employees may rely on their emotions to rationalize
their unwillingness to participate in change [192]. Thus, we argue that
the result of doxastic rationalization, willingness to participate in our
case, involves both cognitive and emotional components. Employees
may justify their engagement in digital transformation not only based on
anticipated improvements in work processes but also due to the intrinsic
excitement and positive emotions the change evokes.
Accordingly, we argue that employees with positive predispositions
toward change are more likely to engage actively in transformation ef­
forts [193] and may even become advocates for the change process
[194]. In contrast, negative predispositions can discourage participa­
tion, as employees may perceive changes as threats rather than oppor­
tunities [120]. This adverse outlook can lead to resistance, decreased
engagement, and, in some cases, overt opposition to DT initiatives
[184]. Therefore, we hypothesize:
H2: Employee predispositions—cognitive and emotional—toward digital
transformation positively affect employee willingness to participate in
digital transformation initiatives.
3.3. Control variables
To ensure the robustness of our analysis, we account for situational
factors, including employee participation efficacy (how capable I am of
participating) and participation norms (how expected I am to partici­
pate). Employee participation efficacy refers to the employee’s self-
efficacy in participating in DT initiatives and contributing positively.
Complementing this, participation norms capture organizational con­
ventions supporting employee participation in DT initiatives. These two
variables are critical control factors frequently reported in the literature.
Our intention in including them is not to retest their importance but to
demonstrate the significance of predispositions even in the presence of
these well-established factors.
Participation Efficacy. Employee self-efficacy plays a crucial role in
determining employee participation [38] and performance [77] in
change initiatives. The literature on organizational change consistently
reports a positive correlation between employees’ personal and collec­
tive efficacy (e.g., [181]) and their participation in the change [195].
High self-efficacy encourages employees to initiate, commit to, and

which can potentially influence employee willingness to participate.
Similarly, controlling for individual factors such as gender, age, edu­

our model. For example, employees with higher levels of experience and
education may demonstrate a greater willingness to participate in digital
transformation due to increased confidence and familiarity.
4. Methods
We tested our model using a cross-sectional survey conducted in the
United States and Vietnam. We capture temporal elements—experience-
driven sentiments, current predispositions, and future willingness to
participate—within a single time frame by using proxy questions that
signal different temporal contexts rather than relying on longitudinal
methods to observe changes over time. This method also provided a
robust basis for comparing these variables across different groups and
contexts while also minimizing potential temporal confounding factors,
ensuring the comparability of data between the two national cohorts.
Our survey targeted employees and lower-level managers, such as team
leaders and supervisors, due to their critical role in the actualization of
digital transformation. Previous research underscores the importance of
involving this group, revealing that excluding operational employees
and lower management from planning phase can lead to increased
resistance [201] and, consequently, lower levels of accountability and
commitment [74,101].
4.1. Model specification and instrumentation
We operationalized employee sentiment toward digitalization as
three higher-order formative constructs, each with two reflective di­
mensions.9
We operationalized predispositions as a higher-order
formative construct and focused on two reflective dimensions: cogni­
tive predispositions and emotional predispositions. Two control vari­
ables, employee willingness toparticipate in DTinitiatives, participation
efficacy, and participation norms, were modeled as reflective constructs.
To determine whether these constructs should be modeled forma­
tively or reflectively, we carefully evaluated their causality, sensitivity,
interchangeability, inclusivity, and predictability [202–207]. In this
study, each lower-order construct can partially cause, predict, and
define its corresponding higher-order construct. Each dimension of
autonomy, competence, and relatedness addresses a specific need, and
only when combined do they provide a comprehensive understanding of
the overall construct. For example, employees’ sentiment toward au­
tonomy is shaped by how digitalization empowers them and facilitates
innovation rather than their sense of autonomy driving these outcomes.
Similarly, cognitive and emotional predispositions represent two
distinct aspects of employee appraisal, collectively forming the overall
predispositions rather than being caused by them. Moreover,
lower-order constructs are sensitive to the exclusion of others because of
their inclusive nature. For example, empowerment and innovation are
distinct expressions of autonomy, and neither alone fully captures the
sentiment of autonomy. Last, changes in one lower-order formative
construct do not necessarily lead to changes in others. For example,
learning and adaptability, while related to competence, are independent
and non-interchangeable; omitting one would compromise the defini­
tion and completeness of the higher-order construct. Therefore, these
constructs should be modeled formatively to preserve their integrity and
the accuracy of the overall construct.10
Measurement Items. The measurement items were adapted from
previous studies (e.g., [32,109,116]) and further refined and contextu­
alized through two rounds of pretesting, pilot testing, and expert con­
sultations [205]. For employee sentiment toward digitalization,
respondents were first provided with our clear definition of digitaliza­
tion. We defined digitalization for them as the use of digital technologies
in their unit or department to improve existing processes, enhance ef­
ficiency, and streamline workflows without fundamentally changing
business models or organizational structures. We provided examples,
such as adopting a new application like a CRM system, automating
routine tasks like scheduling, leveraging cloud-based tools for file
sharing, and implementing digital communication and collaboration
platforms to optimize day-to-day operations. Then, they were asked to
reflect on digitalization initiatives or projects in their immediate work
environments over the past three years. For the autonomy dimension,
items related to empowerment [208] and innovation [209] were
contextualized and included. These items assessed how digitalization
has enabled employees to take proactive actions, assume more re­
sponsibility, and participate in innovative work. The dimension of
competence was measured through adaptability [210] and learning
[211], focusing on the ability of employees to adapt to changes and
continuously improve their skills and knowledge. The relatedness
dimension included items on communication and cooperation [81],
which evaluated improvements in teamwork, cross-functional collabo­
ration, and knowledge-sharing.
Employee predispositions toward digital transformation were
assessed through cognitive predispositions [116] and emotional pre­
dispositions [109]. For these constructs, respondents were asked about
their opinions about “recently introduced” organization-wide DT ini­
tiatives directly impacting them. We reminded participants of the dif­
ferences between past digitalization efforts and digital transformation
and defined the latter as a strategic and fundamental shift in an orga­
nization’s operations, value propositions, and identity, driven by the
integration of digital technologies to transform major business

colleagues’ expectations regarding their participation.
The final set of items, detailed in Appendix C, was measured using a
Likert-type scale ranging from (totally disagree (1) to totally agree (7),
ensuring comparability across responses. Because our primary purpose
was to measure employees’ willingness—a proactive, future-focused
stance—to participate in DT initiatives, we deliberately employed
positively framed survey items to illuminate how individuals perceive
and respond to the potential benefits or their absence. However, this
approach still allowed respondents to express disagreement, neutrality,
or resistance through the 7-point Likert scale. The items were rigorously
refined based on pretest and pilot study, ensuring their theoretical
robustness and practical relevance. This process was necessary because
some constructs were not directly sourced from the digital trans­
formation literature, necessitating an iterative approach that involved
dropping, rewording, or replacing certain items. Furthermore, since the
survey was administered in English in Vietnam, special attention was
paid to ensuring readability for participants whose first language was
not English.
Pretests and Pilot Study. After developing the initial version of the
instrument, we conducted a pretest to identify any respondent concerns
and questionnaire-related issues. The survey was first shared with 10
researchers affiliated with our research lab to gather feedback on the
wording and presentation of the questions. Following initial adjust­
ments, we invited 10 business managers to evaluate the questions for
readability and general comprehension. After further refining the survey
instrument, we conducted two pilot studies to ensure its proper func­
tioning and readability. In the first pilot study, we collected 64 responses
to assess the reliability of the instrument. Subsequently, we adjusted 12
items and collected an additional 78 responses, which confirmed the
reliability and validity of all constructs. Based on the results of the
second pilot study, we made final adjustments, modifying five items and
removing three. As a result, all measurement items demonstrated
loadings on their latent variables exceeding 0.7, indicating acceptable
item reliability [214]. Cronbach’s alpha and composite reliability of all
constructs were greater than 0.7, indicating good internal consistency
between the items measuring each variable [214]. The results also
showed adequate convergent and discriminant validity for all first-order
constructs since a) all average variance extracted (AVE) were higher
than 0.50, b) the square root of the AVE of each variable was larger than
the correlations of the given variable with the other variables, and c) the
intervariable correlations were below 0.90 [214].
When finalizing our online survey, we implemented strategies to
ensure anonymity and carefully crafted survey questions with neutral
language that did not suggest desirable choices or biases, thus indirectly
controlling for social desirability bias. Additionally, these neutral and
strategically ordered questions were designed to reduce the risk of
Common Method Bias (CMB). Since our pilot study did not indicate a
significant impact of social desirability or CMB on responses, we chose
to omit direct controls for these factors in the final survey. This decision
allowed us to streamline the questionnaire, addressing one of the main
concerns from the pretest—its length—while maintaining a strong focus
on the key variables.
4.2. Data collection
The field study was conducted in two contexts: the United States and
Vietnam. Customized recruitment processes were used for each region to
collect data from professionals in these distinct cultural environments.
In Vietnam, the recruitment of 616 initial respondents was systemati­
cally facilitated through collaboration with an executive education
institution. This institution played a crucial role in identifying and
inviting professionals who were aligned with the study’s target de­
mographic. played a crucial role in identifying and inviting professionals
who were aligned with the study’s target demographic. Leveraging its
extensive networks within the business communities of Hanoi and Ho
Chi Minh City, the institution ensured the participation of relevant
candidates. However, theUnited Statescohortwas recruitedthrough the
LinkedIn platform. Through targeted outreach, including sponsored
content, approximately 1000 professionals who met predefined selec­
tion criteria were invited to participate in the study. This approach
enabled the participation of a diverse but qualified sample, ensuring a
broad range of perspectives essential to the research. To maintain con­
sistency in data collection across geographically and culturally diverse
cohorts, all respondents received the same standardized questionnaire.
This uniformity ensured that the data collected were directly compara­
ble between regions, providing a robust foundation to draw generaliz­
able conclusions.
Eligibility was reconfirmed through a series of screening questions to
ensure the inclusion of relevant respondents. After obtaining informed
consent, participants were first asked if their organizations had intro­
duced or implemented any DT initiatives within the past three years. A
clear definition of digital transformation was provided with examples.
Those who responded affirmatively were further asked about the spe­
cific types ofdigital transformationthey hadexperienced, choosing from
four categories plus an “other” option: a) process trans­
formation—reforming business processes, b) business model trans­
formation—redefining the company’s business model, c) domain
transformation—launching a new line of products or services, and d)

implemented screening questions to exclude digital transformation
leaders, senior management, or executives from the study. This decision

tion. By focusing on individuals who experienced these transformations
rather than those who directed them, we aimed to capture more
balanced and representative insights, ensuring a more accurate under­
standing of the impacts and perceptions of digital transformation within
organizations. Furthermore, at the end of the survey, respondents were

4.3. Data analysis
After collecting data, we used partial least squares structural equa­
tion modeling (PLS-SEM) and SmartPLS [215] to test the measurement
model and hypotheses. PLS-SEM is a reliable method when the research
goal is the identification of key drivers (exploration) based on an
extension of an existing structural theory rather than confirmation or
testing an existing theory [216,217]. Additionally, in exploratory
studies like ours, the goal is to maximize the variance explained in the
dependent variables rather than confirm the goodness of fit between the
model and data, as emphasized by covariance-based structural equation
modeling [206]. PLS also offers greater efficiency in parameter esti­
mation and prediction power �in the presence of inflated standard er­
rors [214]. Furthermore, PLS facilitates the modeling of formative
constructs, and it is recommended for the hierarchical model evaluation
used for this study [217].
To compare the differences between the United States and Vietnam
cohorts, we used PLS Multigroup Analysis (PLS-MGA). PLS-MGA was
selected as a favorable approach for group comparison because it en­
ables the detection of significant differences between groups without
requiring distributional assumptions, making it suitable for comparing
structural relationships in different subgroups [218]. This method
allowed us to assess whether the relationships between constructs
differed significantly between the two distinct cultural contexts, thus
adding depth and rigor to our analysis.
5. Results
We initially tested our measurement model and hypotheses using the
full dataset, which combined two distinct datasets: one from the United
States and one from Vietnam. In the United States, data were collected
through an online LinkedIn survey panel, yielding 260 completed sur­
veys. In Vietnam, we collected 296 completed surveys from the target
population. After screening the responses and eliminating incomplete
submissions, we obtained a final sample of 469 responses, with 245 from
the United States and 224 from Vietnam. Table 3 summarizes the de­
mographics of participants grouped by country. The respondents were
predominantly men, reflecting global trends in gender representation
within various industries, as reported by the World Economic Forum in
2023 [219]. Most participants were aged between 25 and 45 years, with
a notable portion holding a bachelor’s degree or higher. Additionally,
74% of the participants had over five years of experience, and about
70% of them were employed by medium to large companies. Both the
United States and Vietnam samples exhibited a broad distribution in
various industry sectors, with notable similarities in certain categories
and differences in others.
The Services sector dominated in both samples, accounting for
50.9% in Vietnam and 43.1% in the United States. Both countries had
similar representation in technology, with 18.2% in Vietnam and 14.8%
in the United States, as well as in the public sector & government,
around 10% in each. The retail & trade sector also showed comparable
representation in Vietnam (12.1%) and the United States (7.7%).
However, despite our expectations, the United States sample had a
significantly higher representation in manufacturing (24.4%) than
Vietnam (9.1%). Respondents in both groups reported that their orga­
nizations underwent digitalization in the last three years and are
currently planning or implementing one or more forms of digital
transformation that directly affect them. Given that some respondents
selected more than one form of digital transformation, the observed
differences between the transformation types were all below 13%,
indicating a high level of comparability between the samples. The
datasets were comparable in respondent profiles, enabling a meaningful
comparison between the two samples.

5.3. Assessment of the structural model
The structural model was tested following the two-step procedure
suggested by Wetzels et al. [217] for hierarchical models. In the first
step, the latent-variable scores for the second-order constructs were
obtained using PLS path modeling by specifying a latent variable that
represents all manifest variables of the underlying first-order latent
variables [217]. In the second step, the hypotheses were tested in the
presence of control variables. The results of thestructural modelanalysis
are illustrated in Fig. 3 and summarized in Tables 6 and 7.
As shown in Table 6, our model accounts for 67% and 45% of the
variance in employees’ predispositions toward digital transformation
and willingness to participate in DT initiatives, respectively. The results
suggested that predispositions toward digital transformation could be
predicated by employee sentiment toward digitalization in terms of
autonomy (β = 0.34, p < 0.001), competence (β = 0.28, p < 0.001), and
relatedness (β = 0.26, p < 0.001). Predispositions toward digital trans­
formation exerted a significant, positive impact on willingness to
participate in DT initiatives (β = 0.28, p < 0.001). Our data thus provide
support for all hypotheses. A comparison of path coefficients, using
Cohen’s f 2
effect size [228], reveals that autonomy (f 2
= 0.1, p < 0.05)
has greater predictive powerwhen compared to relatedness (f 2
=0.08, p
< 0.05) and competence (f 2
= 0.07, p < 0.05). Additionally, examining
their confidence level supported our decision to include all three vari­
ables in the proposed model. Furthermore, we observed a positive
impact of our two key control variables on the willingness to participate
in DT initiatives: perceived participation efficacy (β = 0.26, p < 0.001)
and perceived participation norms (β = 0.20, p < 0.01). We also
controlled and tested the effects of organizational factors (industry and
size) and individual demographics (gender, age, education, and expe­
rience). Among these, only education (β = 0.11, p < 0.001) and expe­
rience (β = 0.11, p < 0.05) showed positive effects, indicating that
higher levels of education and greater experience within the firm are
associated with an increased willingness to participate in DT initiatives.
Mediation analysis was conducted to explore the role of pre­
dispositions toward digital transformation in mediating the perception
of digitalization outcomes—autonomy, competence, and rela­
tedness—on willingness to participate in DT initiatives. In this study, we
adopted the bootstrapping method as a more rigorous, powerful
approach to assess the mediating role of perceived participation affor­
dances [229,230]. The bootstrapping method was preferable because
the indirect effect was measured directly in this case rather than merely
inferred to exist through a sequence of tests. Moreover, the boot­
strapping method imposes no assumption of the dataset’s normality;
thus, it is generally recommended for studies with smaller sample sizes.
In this study, the 95% confidence interval for indirect effects was ob­
tained with 5000 bootstrap resamples. The results of our analysis
(Table 7) revealed that the bootstrap confidence intervals did not
include zero. Therefore, we concluded that predispositions toward dig­
ital transformation significantly influenced autonomy, competence, and
relatedness on willingness to participate in DT initiatives.

5.4. Comparative analysis
The demographic profiles of the United States and Vietnam pop­
ulations were broadly similar in several key dimensions. When grouping
respondents by broader age categories, a significant majority of the re­
spondents from both countries fell into the 25–45 year age range, with
77% of participants in Vietnam and 78% in the United States belonging
to this category. Both populations also had comparable levels of pro­
fessional experience, with approximately 74% of the participants in both
samples having more than five years of professional experience.
Furthermore, educational attainment was high in both groups, with
more than90% ofparticipants inthe UnitedStates and Vietnamhaving a
bachelor’s degree or higher. Furthermore, when comparing company
sizes, around 57% of participants in both countries worked in organi­
zations with more than 250 employees, indicating similar employment
environments. The similarities in age, experience, education, and com­
pany size indicate that the United States and Vietnam samples had a
comparable demographic structure, supporting the validity of the
comparisons in this study. To make the comparison, we used PLS-MGA
with two groups: Vietnam (n = 224) and the United States (n = 245).
PLS-MGA was a favorable approach for group comparison, given its
robust nature and lack of restriction over normality [231]. We followed
the three-step MGA protocol [232]. After generating two groups in the
first step, we tested the measurement model to ensure the reliability of
the measures when tested under different conditions. The test of the
measurement model (for both samples) reconfirmed the reliability and
validity of the instrument. In the next step, the model parameters were
calculated for each group independently to examine the specific re­
lationships within each group before comparing them. In the last step,
we compared the differences between the two groups.
Interestingly, our comparison, which accounted for control vari­
ables, revealed no significant difference between the two groups
(Table 8). The ΔR² values for predispositions toward digital trans­
formation and willingness to participate in DT initiatives were 0.01 (p =
0.91) and 0.09 (p = 0.40), respectively, indicating non-significance. The
analysis also confirmed the mediating role of employee predispositions
toward digital transformation, without revealing any significant differ­
ences between Vietnam and the United States contexts
(ATN→PDT→WPD, p = 0.52; CMP→PDT→WPD, p = 0.36;
RLT→PDT→WPD, p = 0.67). The results thus suggest the transferability
of our findings and the external validity of the model.
We also examined the differential effects of control variables on
willingness to participate in DT initiatives. The analysis confirmed the
positive and significant impacts of participation efficacy and participa­
tion norms on willingness to participate in DT initiatives, though the
differences in their effects were not statistically significant (p = 0.43 and
p = 0.24, respectively). Organizational factors (size and industry)
remained non-significant, showing no meaningful differences in their
effects. When controlling for individual factors, we found that experi­
ence positively influenced willingness to participate in DT initiatives,
with no significant difference between the United States and Vietnam. In
contrast, education was significant only in the Vietnam context (Δß =
1.0, p < 0.001), indicating its contextual importance.
6. Discussion
Information systems scholarship increasingly emphasizes the critical
role of employees in the success of digital transformation. These studies
collectively suggest that digital transformation starts with neither stra­
tegic planning nor technology deployment but with employees, as DT
initiatives are less likely to succeed without the latter (e.g., [34,52,71]).
However, these studies often offer a reactive account, falling short of
explaining why some employees embrace and internalize digital trans­
formation while others resist it under similar conditions. Many either
focus narrowly on rational justifications for engineered participation,
overlooking emotional influences, or remain highly abstract, rarely ac­
counting for the role of past experiences in shaping employee responses
(e.g., [14,33,37]). They frequently fail to empirically capture the un­
derlying attitudinal mechanisms with the cross-contextual trans­
ferability and individual-level granularity required for broader
applicability. This persists despite the growing emphasis on micro­
foundations in the literature [10,12,13] and the need for findings with
broader generalizability [34]. As a result, current models are either
generalizable but not operationally practical or situationally applicable
but not transferable to different contexts. Hence, we argue that our field
can benefit from middle-range theory, which identifies the critical
contextual determinants of employee participation in digital trans­
formation (“concepts”) and provides observable mechanisms (“propo­
sitions”) to allow for generalizations (cf., [233]).
In response, we tap into microfoundation literature [9,156,234] and
propose a theoretical model that explains experience-driven participa­
tion in digital transformation and its underlying mechanisms. Grounded
in the S-O-R paradigm and its temporal extension (the TEA model), our
experience-driven participation model offers a novel lens for examining
the underlying mechanisms that explain employee participation in
digital transformation. Unlike previous studies that focus predominantly
on external mechanisms, such as incentives and support or conditions,
such as trust and uncertainty, our approach focuses on employee atti­
tudinal factors, offering deeper insights into employee participation in
DT initiatives.
We borrowed the notion of employee predispositions from the
organizational change literature, which signifies the attitudes of em­
ployees toward new change initiatives. Then, we theorized that
employee predispositions (attitudinal inclination) toward digital trans­
formation could result from need-based attitudinal appraisal from prior
(personal) experiences with digitalization. We also discussed that the
collective attitude from this appraisal would be learned and subjective
(cf., [235]), making it unique to each employee and not linked to any
specific technology or digitalization process; instead, it would refer to
accumulated experiences over time. We then explained that attitudinal
appraisal, regardless of subjectivity, is a judgment in nature and,
therefore, needs a reference point for comparison. By referencing
organizational psychology scholarship and self-determination theory,
we claimed that psychological needs could serve as common and uni­
versal reference points that allow employees to make judgments on
digitalization. This reference frame is relatively independent of the types
of digitalization experienced by employees since the need for autonomy,
competence, and relatedness is universal [148]. However, we contex­
tualized these needs based on the digitalization literature and concep­
tualized autonomy, competence, and relatedness in terms of a) giving
employees autonomy through empowerment and permission to inno­
vate, b) allowing employees to learn and adapt to face changes and
challenges, and c) connecting employees to their colleagues by facili­
tating cooperation and communication.

6.1. Theoretical contributions
By centering on employee participation as a microfoundational
element, our research sheds light on the attitudinal activation as an
underlying dynamic that either propels or impedes participation in
digital transformation. This addresses critical blind spots in existing
frameworks and problematization approaches that primarily attribute
employee participation to structural enablers, behavioral triggers, or
rational-choice calculations rooted in external motivators and engi­
neered interventions (e.g., [14,236]). While acknowledging the contri­
butions of previous research, we offer a complementary perspective in
three ways.
First, we reemphasize the significance of employee predispositions
toward digital transformation. Although individual factors have been
acknowledged in organizational change literature (e.g., [38,108,162])
and conceptual work on digital transformation (e.g., [82,30,127,2]),
empirical studies that closely examine employee participation with
meaningful granularity remain limited [9,23]. The closest line of inquiry
in existing studies is the examination of employee mindset or attitude
and its influence on behavior during transformation processes (e.g.,
[107,109,237,238]. To shed light on the complexity of employee atti­
tude, our study theorizes employee predispositions toward digital
transformation by introducing the dual concepts of cognitive pre­
dispositions and emotional predispositions. We posit that employee
participation in digital transformation is shaped by the interplay of these
two forces. By positioning employee predispositions as a central mech­
anism, our study not only underscores their critical role in reshaping an
organization’s sociotechnical dynamics during digital transformation
but also offers a framework for examining transformation at the
micro-level. This approach provides a fresh perspective for exploring the
root causes of employee resistance and identifying pathways to greater
participation. Such understanding is also essential for grasping how
digital transformations unfold over time, particularly considering the
recent emphasis on their dynamic and iterative nature (e.g., [239,240]).
Second, this study advances the literature by identifying employee
sentiment toward digitalization as the foundational source of pre­
dispositions toward digital transformation. This complements recent
research that highlights the importance of employee experiences [34] by
demonstrating how an employee’s previous interactions with digitali­
zation significantly influence their predispositions. Since employees
may not always have a clear understanding of what a new DT initiative
entails, they often refer to previous experiences with digitalization,
reflecting on how well their personal needs were met and the actual
gains achieved (attitudinal appraisal) to form their positions. We
demonstrate that participation in digital transformation, beyond a pro­
fessional obligation, is a personal endeavor deeply influenced by how
employees subjectively appraise digitalization. Furthermore, by theo­
rizing this attitudinal appraisal as an evaluation of need satisfaction (or
frustration), we contribute to the emerging need-based perspective on
employee behavior—a line of inquiry that has recently gained notable
attention in the field (e.g., [109,241,242]). This theorization also en­
ables the articulation of experience-driven participation through the
lens of the TEA model, which clarifies the temporal mechanisms un­
derlying employee participation (or its absence) in digital
transformation.
Last, our comparative analysis of data from Vietnam and the United
States reveals that experience-driven participation, while contextually
expressed, exhibits a high degree of universality. This finding challenges
the prevailing emphasis on hyper-contextualization in the micro­
foundations of change literature, particularly in relation to employee
readiness (cf., [66]). By aligning with and contextualizing Ding and
Kuvaas’s [148] claim, our study reinforces the notion that employees
evaluate work experiences through a common lens of need-based ex­
pectations, thereby affirming the cross-contextual relevance of motiva­
tional theories in the digital transformation domain. While situational
factors such as participation norms and perceived efficacy remain
important contextual factors, the underlying psychological mechanisms
appear consistent. Accordingly, understanding employee pre­
dispositions toward digital transformation offers insights that are likely
to transcend national and organizational boundaries.
6.2. Practical implications
Digital transformation requires a shift in employee mindsets and
behaviors—an undertaking that, as Bucy et al. ([70], p. 2) observe, “few
leaders know how to achieve.” Yet, in practice, the dominant emphasis
on aligning technologies and strategies often eclipses this need, over­
looking the pivotal role of the employee. Without employees’ genuine
willingness to change, even the most meticulously designed trans­
formation efforts risk stagnation and diminished impact. This oversight
is particularly consequential given the high failure rate of DT initiatives
[7], frequently attributed to insufficient employee engagement [53,
243]. In response, our study offers fresh practical insights across three
critical areas.
First, we posit that understanding the collective interest of em­
ployees in digital transformation is crucial to accurately assessing
organizational readiness for transformation. Without gauging this in­
terest, organizational leaders risk overestimating readiness, particularly
regarding key stakeholders’ buy-in. Therefore, evaluating the willing­
ness to participate in DT initiatives becomes an essential step in any
successful initiative. By incorporating this practice, organizations can
take human-centered digital transformation to the next level, inte­
grating planning tools and governance mechanisms that align more
closely with employees’ needs. For example, practitioners can use our
instrument to predict experience-driven participation in readiness as­
sessments, allowing for more informed decision-making and strategic
alignment in the transformation process.
Second, we posit that the success of digital transformation depends
not only on technically skilled and operationally agile employees, but
also on their psychosocial readiness. However, this readiness cannot be
assumed nor reduced to a generic notion of “mindset.” Rather, it should
be understood in terms of how employees think and feel about digital
transformation more broadly—what we define as predispositions. Our
empirical findings demonstrate that this construct reliably predicts
willingness to participate in DT initiatives across two distinct national
contexts. As such, investing in these psychosocial dimensions of
employee readiness is as critical as investing in incentives and support
systems. For instance, assessing employee predispositions prior to
launching a DT initiative can enable organizations to anticipate and plan
for participation, thereby increasing the likelihood of successful trans­
formation outcomes.
Last, our findings reveal that employees’ experiences with digitali­
zation indirectly influence their willingness to participate in DT initia­
tives. Organizations should, therefore, treat every digitalization project
as an opportunity to cultivate positive attitudes toward future DT ini­
tiatives, engaging employees well in advance. This aligns with the recent
emphasis on digital employee experience (DEX) strategies, which focus
on meeting the experiential needs of employees during digitalization
[244,245]. DEX should be seen not just as a tool to improve business
processes but as a strategic approach to foster positive employee expe­
riences and encourage proactive participation in future initiatives. For
example, empowering employees to make independent decisions and

7. Limitations and future work
This study opens new avenues for research and development in
human-centered digital transformation, though its limitations should be
acknowledged and addressed in future work. First, our research is
grounded in the assumption that employee participation in planning and
implementing DT initiatives is critical to their success. Although this
assumption aligns with the existing literature on organizational change
and digital transformation scholarship (e.g., [12,14,236]), it requires
further investigation. Future studies should aim to quantify and evaluate
the positive outcomes of employee participation or the negative conse­
quences of its absence.
Second, our study took a broad approach to conceptualizing digital
transformation, focusing on early-stage initiatives without narrowing
down to specific types or the nature of participation due to the explor­
atory nature of this research. While this approach was necessary for
transferability, future research should address this limitation by con­
ducting more focused studies that account for different digital trans­
formation attributes, such as processes, domains, and strategies.
Additionally, recognizing digital transformation as an evolving process
with distinct milestones underscores the need for longitudinal studies to
complement our understanding of employee participation dynamics
over time. This approach could also explore how employee participation
facilitates the routinization of new work practices and the normalization
of digital transformation.
Third, it is crucial to consider the limitations and boundaries of
employee participation in DT initiatives, which may be influenced by
factors such as role, function, and the specific nature and scale of the DT
initiative. Future research should identify and define these boundaries,
distinguish between formal and informal participation, and consider
organizational mandates that affect the scope, rate, and quality of
participation. Additionally, participation boundaries can be defined
more precisely based on digital transformation governance expecta­
tions, ranging from initial feedback and requirements assessment to
experimentation, pilot implementation, and subsequent evaluation and
improvement. We anticipate that the impact of employee pre­
dispositions may vary depending on these participatory governance
needs.
Fourth, given the cross-sectional nature of our study, more applied
research is necessary to develop practical recommendations by directly
measuring employees’ actual contributions to digital transformation.
For example, assessing willingness to participate at the outset of a DT
initiative and then comparing it to actual participation, as well as
quantifying the impact between groups with varying levels of initial
willingness, could provide a deeper understanding of participation nu­
ances. Future research could also draw on implementation science to
develop roadmaps for orchestrating employee participation and con­
tributions. Furthermore, conceptualizing employee participation as a
dynamic feedback loop can provide valuable insights for monitoring and
responding to operational challenges, ultimately enhancing organiza­
tional adaptability.
Fifth, future research should explore additional individual-level
factors that influence employee participation in digital transformation.
For instance, this study did not account for personal goals or other
psychological constructs such as identity, self-perception, and self-
concept. Investigating intrinsic motivations and their relative impor­
tance may also offer deeper insights into fostering participation—or, at
the very least, inform strategies to mitigate the effects of negative sen­
timents and predispositions. This line of inquiry could also examine
constructs like socio-professional responsibility and organizational
citizenship, particularly how organizations might cultivate a sense of
accountability toward DT initiatives, even among employees who do not
initially hold favorable predispositions.
Last, our research sheds light on how employees internalize the
values of digitalization through the lens of psychological need satisfac­
tion. However, the survey’s predominantly positive framing may unin­
tentionally narrow the range of captured experiences, potentially
underrepresenting negative or critical employee sentiments toward
digital transformation. Although the instrument permitted expressions
of disagreement, the limited use of negatively framed items constrains
its capacity to fully capture the complexity of psychological need
satisfaction. This methodological limitation warrants caution in gener­
alizing the findings, as the study may not fully reflect concerns some
employees hold, particularly those related to misalignment with orga­
nizational goals or unmet personal expectations. To address this, we
encourage future researchers to explore alternative theoretical per­
spectives, such as experience theory or regulatory focus theory, to
capture a broader array of psychological needs, including identity, and
further refine the experience-driven participation model. By integrating
these perspectives, future research can offer a more comprehensive
understanding of employee participation in digital transformation.
8. Conclusion
This work highlights the critical importance of understanding
employee participation in digital transformation. While strategy and
technology form the wings of transformation, it is employees who pro­
vide the lift and give it flight. To advance this perspective, we introduce
a theoretical framework that explains experience-driven participation in
digital transformation. It sheds light on the individual-level micro­
foundations of transformation, highlighting how predispositions toward
change are formed well before new initiatives take shape. This
perspective invites a shift from reactive engagement strategies to pro­
active efforts that cultivate positive predispositions long before change
is formally underway.

Appendix A: Three perspectives on employee participation in digital transformation
Three main analytical perspectives exist—technology-centric, strategy-centric, and process-centric—each offering unique ways to conceptualize,
study, and strategize employee participation in digital transformation.
The technology-centric perspective tends to reduce employee participation to a functional role. It confines their involvement in the instrumental
use of new tools, assuming proficiency and prioritizing efficiency while neglecting employees’ experiences [81]. As criticized, this view often treats
employees as mere components of anoperational system, valuing their adaptability and compliance over their autonomyand meaning-making [14,85,
156,246]. Studies within this framework typically characterize participation as how employees interact with and respond to these technologies,
exploring factors such as workplace design, adoption, resistance, training, and individual competencies such as literacy [14,33,109,247,248]. The
emphasis here is predominantly on the mechanics of participation, examining how these factors contribute to or hinder the change [1,239].
The strategy-centric perspective views employee participation as a strategic imperative, emphasizing the alignment of their behavior with
overarching business goals to fundamentally transform dynamic capabilities, organizational identity, and competitive positioning [6,48,60,79,249].
This approach excels at theorizing employee participation, its necessity, and the outcomes it aims to achieve. It also recognizes employees as drivers of
organizational change, yet often within a top-down framework—as a means to an end rather than as a dynamic, integral component of the trans­
formation process [24,34,75,76]. This perspective tends to treat employee participation as a strategic issue to be resolved through interventions such
as digital governance, change leadership, DT advocacy, and resource allocation. Thus, it often relegates the operational challenges of executing these
strategies to a secondary concern [25,76,239,250]. This view risks misaligning strategic objectives with employees’ daily experiences, as it does not
necessarily consider the practical challenges, they navigate during DT implementation [1,25,50].
The process-centric perspective on employee participation has recently become prominent, especially in addressing the operational challenges of
DT implementation [34,79,104]. This perspective frames DT as a continuous change process, integrating the operational, procedural, individual, and
temporal dimensions of transformation. Central to this view is the pivotal role of employees, especially digital leaders, and the broader organizational
culture in ensuring successful DT, as reflected in performance metrics during and after implementation [14,23,30,109]. This perspective acknowl­
edges employees as a central challenge in DT but tends to focus more on the “what” and “how” of participation, for example, resistance, or adoption
process. As a result, it often overlooks the deeper “why”—why employees choose to participate beyond organizational mandates, leadership-driven
promotional campaigns, and extrinsic rewards and incentives [6,74]. For example, this perspective often narrows its focus to specific roles, such as
Chief Information Officers (CIOs) or Chief Digital Officers, and concentrates on implementation outcomes linked to low employee performance, such
as missed operational targets or certain conditions such as employee trust and uncertainty [11,69,74,156,251].
