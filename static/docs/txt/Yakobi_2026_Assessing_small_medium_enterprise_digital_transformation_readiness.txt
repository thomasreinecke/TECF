ABSTRACT
Small and Medium Enterprises (SMEs) play a central role in South Africa’s economy but
face significant challenges in adapting to ongoing digital transformation. While much
of the global literature focuses on large firms or advanced economies, less is known
about how SMEs in resource-constrained settings navigate the structural, human, and
ethical demands of digitalisation. This study addresses that gap through a systematic
thematic synthesis of 22 interdisciplinary and Information Systems (IS) studies. Drawing
on grounded theory–inspired coding techniques, the analysis identified four interrelated
dimensions shaping SME digital readiness: digital integration, workforce capabilities,
policy alignment, and ethical governance. These dimensions were consolidated into the
Human-Technology Impact Matrix (HTIM), a conceptual framework that maps readiness
challenges along the axes of structural complexity and human-centricity. The HTIM
provides both an analytical tool for researchers and a diagnostic resource for
policymakers seeking to strengthen SME digital capacity in the Global South. By
extending existing models of digital transformation to include ethical and developmental
considerations, the study contributes to Information and Communication Technologies
for Development (ICT4D) scholarship and offers actionable insights for inclusive
innovation policy in South Africa and similar contexts.
1. Introduction
South African Small and Medium Enterprises (SMEs) currently face significant challenges in adapting to
the Fourth Industrial Revolution (4IR), even as the global dialogue progresses rapidly towards the Fifth
Industrial Revolution (5IR) (Sithole & Ruhode, 2021; Sutherland, 2020). This ongoing transition represents
more than a technological shift; it signifies a profound socio-economic transformation that emphasises
human-centricity, ethical Artificial Intelligence (AI), and sustainability (Mkansi & Landman, 2021). For
South Africa, a nation grappling with persistent socio-economic disparities and infrastructural limitations
(Mthombeni, 2024; Rampitsang, 2023), understanding the readiness of its SMEs for the 5IR is critical not
only for their continued survival but also for fostering inclusive economic growth.
The 5IR marks a deliberate shift from technology-centric automation towards human-centric innova-
tion, fostering a symbiotic relationship between humans and intelligent machines (Takawira & Pooe,
2025). In contrast to the 4IR’s primary focus on hyper-connectivity and datafication (Sutherland, 2020),
the 5IR places human well-being, sustainability, and resilience at its core (Samuel & Moagi, 2022). This
evolving paradigm necessitates that enterprises not only adopt advanced technologies but also funda-
mentally reconfigure their operational models to align with broader societal values and environmental
stewardship. For SMEs, this presents a complex array of challenges, including critical technological

2. Theoretical background
2.1. Digital transformation and SME readiness
Digital transformation has emerged as a central strategic imperative for SMEs, enabling them to reduce
expenditure, optimise operations, and focus on value-adding activities (Bhuiyan et al., 2024). In contrast
to large firms, SMEs often engage with digital transformation under conditions of constrained resources,
limited skills, and socioeconomic factors that shape both the pace and outcomes of transformation
efforts (Díaz-Arancibia et al., 2024). As a result, digital transformation for SMEs is not merely a techno-
logical process but an organisational and socio-technical one that involves rethinking digital infrastruc-
tures, implementing new organisational mechanisms, and senior management orchestrating change (Ben
Slimane et al., 2022).
Within this study, SME digital transformation readiness is understood as the extent to which firms
possess the technological, organisational, human, and institutional conditions required to meaningfully
engage with digital change, influenced by strategic leadership, organisational culture, information tech-
nology capabilities, and business model transformation (Motjolopane & Chanza, 2023). This definition
moves beyond narrow measures of technology adoption to account for organisational preparedness, as
companies often advance in adopting digital technologies without being ready from an organisational
perspective (Machado et al., 2021).
The concept of readiness is particularly salient in relation to the 5IR. Unlike the 4IR, which prioritises
automation and efficiency, the 5IR foregrounds advanced technologies such as artificial intelligence and
Internet of Things with human-centric approaches (Takawira & Pooe, 2025). Importantly, the 5IR is not
treated in this study as a deterministic technological stage or as an empirically realised reality among
SMEs. Rather, it is conceptualised as a framework for assessing digital readiness and technological adop-
tion (Kumar & Sharma, 2025).
In the South African context, many SMEs continue to face unresolved 4IR challenges such as inconsis-
tent internet access, electricity issues, lack of tailored training programs, and the need to address funda-
mental challenges before benefiting from advanced technologies (Sheik, 2023). These challenges do not
contradict the relevance of the 5IR; instead, they shape the conditions under which readiness for 5IR
principles can emerge. Accordingly, this study examines readiness for 5IR-aligned transformation through

2.2. Literature review and theoretical positioning in IS
Research on SME digital transformation has drawn on a range of IS and organisational theories to explain
adoption decisions, capability development, and contextual constraints. Each theoretical perspective
offers important but partial insights into SME readiness. This section reviews key theories commonly
applied in IS research and positions them in relation to the study’s focus on human-centric and
context-sensitive transformation.
2.2.1. Technology-organisation-environment (TOE)
The TOE framework conceptualises technology adoption as the outcome of interactions between tech-
nological characteristics, organisational readiness, and environmental conditions (Tornatzky & Fleischer,
1990). In SME research, TOE has been widely used to explain how factors such as perceived usefulness,
organisational size, managerial support, competitive pressure, and regulatory environments influence dig-
ital adoption (Baker, 2012). While TOE is effective in capturing contextual constraints, it tends to prioritise
adoption drivers over human agency and ethical considerations, which limits its ability to fully explain
readiness for 5IR-aligned transformation.
2.2.2. Resource-based view (RBV)
The RBV focuses on firm-specific resources and capabilities as sources of competitive advantage (Barney,
1991). Applied to SME digital transformation, RBV highlights the importance of digital skills, managerial
competencies, financial resources, and learning capabilities (Wade & Hulland, 2004). However, RBV
largely assumes relatively stable institutional environments and does not sufficiently account for exter-
nal governance constraints or socio-technical tensions that characterise SMEs operating in emerging
economies.
2.2.3. Socio-technical systems
Socio-Technical Systems Theory emphasises the interdependence between technological systems and
social structures, including work practices, organisational culture, and human agency (Bostrom & Heinen,
1977). This perspective is particularly relevant to the 5IR, as it foregrounds the need for alignment
between digital technologies and human values. In SME contexts, socio-technical theory helps explain
why digital transformation initiatives often fail when technological adoption is not accompanied by
workforce readiness, participatory design, and ethical consideration.
2.2.4. Institutional theory
Institutional Theory examines how organisational behaviour is shaped by formal regulations, norms, and
cognitive structures (Scott, 2008). For SMEs, especially in emerging economies, institutional theory pro-
vides insight into how fragmented policies, weak enforcement mechanisms, and misaligned support pro-
grammes constrain coordinated digital transformation (DiMaggio & Powell, 1983). This perspective
underscores that SME readiness is not solely an internal organisational matter but is deeply embedded
in broader governance ecosystems.
2.2.5. Information and communication technology for development (ICT4D)
The ICT4D perspective situates digital transformation within broader development goals, including inclu-
sion, equity, and socio-economic upliftment (Heeks, 2009; Walsham, 2017). ICT4D is particularly relevant
for analysing SME readiness in Global South contexts, as it foregrounds uneven access to digital infra-
structure, skills, and opportunities (Avgerou, 2008). Unlike mainstream adoption models, ICT4D explicitly
interrogates who benefits from digital transformation and under what conditions.

2.3. Integrating IS theories to explain SME readiness
Digital transformation within SMEs has long been examined through established IS frameworks such as
the TOE framework (Baker, 2012; Tornatzky & Fleischer, 1990) and the RBV (Barney, 1991; Wade & Hulland,
2004), which explain how technological conditions, organisational capabilities, and environmental pres-
sures shape adoption and readiness. However, when applied in isolation, these models provide only
partial explanations, particularly in resource-constrained and institutionally fragmented contexts.
Socio-Technical Systems Theory (Bostrom & Heinen, 1977) extends this understanding by foregrounding
the interaction between human agency and technological systems, a perspective that is central to the
5IR emphasis on human-centric, ethical, and sustainable innovation. Institutional Theory (DiMaggio &
Powell, 1983; Scott, 2008) further contextualises SME readiness by highlighting how governance frag-
mentation, policy incoherence, and normative pressures constrain coordinated digital transformation,
especially in the Global South.
More recent IS scholarship has called for integrative and ethically informed approaches to digital
transformation. Vial (2019) emphasises transformation as a strategic and organisational process rather
than a technological endpoint, while ICT4D perspectives (Heeks, 2009; Walsham, 2017) foreground inclu-
sion, contextual sensitivity, and development outcomes. Ethical governance and responsible innovation
have also gained prominence, with IS scholars arguing that concerns such as data justice, algorithmic
accountability, and sustainability must be treated as integral rather than peripheral to digital transforma-
tion. Despite these advances, there remains a lack of theory-informed frameworks that synthesise tech-
nological, human, institutional, and ethical dimensions to assess SME readiness for the 5IR. This study
addresses this gap by integrating these complementary IS perspectives and operationalising them
through the HTIM. This theoretical integration provides the analytical foundation for the HTIM introduced
in the following section.
2.4. Conceptualising the HTIM
Building on this integrated theoretical perspective above, the study introduces the HTIM as a diagnostic
framework for assessing SME digital transformation readiness. The HTIM operationalises key 5IR principles
by positioning human-centricity and structural complexity as its two core analytical dimensions. Structural
complexity captures the institutional, organisational, and infrastructural conditions shaping transforma-
tion capacity, while human-centricity reflects the extent to which digital initiatives align with human
agency, ethical governance, and inclusive development (Walsham, 2017).
2.4.1. Positioning HTIM relative to existing models
Unlike traditional SME digital readiness models and 4IR maturity frameworks, which typically emphasise
levels of technological adoption or automation, the HTIM foregrounds human-centricity and structural
complexity as primary diagnostic dimensions. Many existing models implicitly assume stable institutional
environments and linear progression toward technological maturity. In contrast, the HTIM explicitly
accounts for governance fragmentation, ethical considerations, and developmental constraints that char-
acterise SMEs operating in Global South contexts (Heeks, 2009). As such, the HTIM functions not as a
linear maturity model, but as a context-sensitive diagnostic tool for assessing readiness under conditions
of institutional and resource variability. The four readiness dimensions identified through this synthesis
formed the empirical and theoretical basis for the construction of the HTIM, presented in Section 5.
3. Materials and methods
This study synthesise existing empirical and conceptual literature to assess the digital transformation
readiness of SMEs in South Africa and identify key technological, organisational, environmental, policy,
and ethical factors influencing this readiness. By conducting a thematic synthesis, the study aims to
develop an integrative understanding of readiness dimensions and provide policy-relevant insights for
SMEs and stakeholders. This study followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and
Meta-Analyses (PRISMA 2020) guidelines to ensure transparency and rigor in the literature identification,

3.1. Research design
This study adopted a systematic thematic synthesis informed by grounded theory–inspired coding tech-
niques to examine the readiness of South African SMEs for digital transformation in the context of the
5IR. This study does not apply classical grounded theory as originally developed by Glaser and Straus
(1967), which relies on primary data collection and iterative field sampling. Instead, it employs thematic
analysis informed by grounded theory coding principles, adapting open, axial, and selective coding tech-
niques to secondary literature. This approach allows for systematic theory development while remaining
methodologically appropriate for a literature-based synthesis. This approach was selected because empir-
ical research on SME engagement with the 5IR remains limited, particularly in Global South contexts, and
existing insights are fragmented across IS, ICT4D, policy, and management literature. A qualitative syn-
thesis therefore provides a suitable method for theory development and conceptual integration rather
than statistical generalisation.
3.2. Data source and search strategy
A structured literature search was conducted across four major academic databases commonly used in
IS and management research (Scopus, Web of Science, AIS eLibrary, and EBSCOhost). These databases
were selected to ensure coverage of both core IS scholarship and interdisciplinary research relevant to
SME digital transformation. Search strings combined terms related to SMEs, digital transformation, and
emerging industrial paradigms. Examples of search strings included:
• “SMEs” AND “digital transformation” AND “South Africa”,
• “Industry 5.0” OR “Fifth Industrial Revolution” AND “SMEs”,
• “ICT4D” AND “small enterprises”,
• “digital readiness” AND “Global South”.
3.3. Inclusion and exclusion criteria
Included studies were assessed for methodological quality and relevance using adapted qualitative
appraisal criteria. Studies were evaluated based on clarity of research design, data collection and analysis
rigor, and relevance to SME digital transformation. Studies with insufficient methodological detail or lim-
ited relevance were excluded to enhance the credibility of the synthesis. Studies were included and
excluded based on the following criteria that are presented in Table 1.

3.4.1. Data extraction and assessment
The flow diagram in Figure 1, summarises the identification, screening, eligibility, and inclusion stages of
the literature review, consistent with recent qualitative and mixed-methods synthesis studies.
Following database searching, 367 records were identified. After removing duplicates, 312 records
remained for title and abstract screening, resulting in the exclusion of 248 records that did not meet
the inclusion criteria. Sixty-four full-text articles were assessed for eligibility, of which 42 were excluded
due to limited relevance to SME contexts, insufficient engagement with digital transformation dimen-
sions, or lack of applicability to Global South settings. A three-step screening process was employed.
First, articles were screened on the online platform through title and abstract review. Second, full-text
screening was conducted independently by two scholarly experts in the field. Finally, ATLAS.ti (version
24) was used to support an iterative coding and thematic analysis process, enhancing analytical trans-
parency, traceability, and methodological rigour. The final corpus comprised 22 studies included in the
thematic synthesis.
3.4.2. Study characteristics
This search yielded 18 studies, mostly from interdisciplinary domains such as innovation policy, public
management, education, and ICT4D. While many of these were not published in IS journals, they pro-
vided context-specific insights into SME readiness within the South African context, aligning with the
constructivist and interdisciplinary nature of grounded theory synthesis.
To enhance the disciplinary alignment with IS and strengthen the theoretical grounding of the emer-
gent categories, the study incorporated the following additional four highly cited IS studies:
• Vial (2019) on digital transformation,
• Walsham (2017) on ICT4D and contextual IS design,
• Heeks (2009) on development-oriented IS innovation, and
• Stahl et al. (2016) on responsible research and ethical IS governance.
These additions were purposefully selected to serve both corrective and complementary roles.
Correctively, they address earlier gaps in IS disciplinary engagement. Complementarily, they enrich the
coding process by bringing in critical IS debates around ethics, governance, and socio-technical bal-
ance, themes that were underdeveloped in the initial set. These studies were integrated into the cod-
ing and memoing stages without modifying the grounded theory methodology. The selection of the
final 22 studies followed theoretical sampling principles, with the objective of achieving conceptual
sufficiency rather than statistical saturation. As the study is based on secondary literature, classical
grounded theory notions of saturation were not applicable. Instead, sufficiency was determined when
continued analysis of additional sources no longer yielded substantively new conceptual categories
beyond digital integration, workforce readiness, policy alignment, and ethical governance. This approach
aligns with prior qualitative syntheses that prioritise theory building and explanatory completeness
over sample size. Following screening and eligibility assessment, 22 studies presented in Table 2 were
retained for analysis.

4. Results and thematic synthesis
The grounded theory–informed thematic synthesis yielded four interrelated dimensions central to under-
standing SME readiness for the 5IR in South Africa: digital integration, workforce readiness, policy align-
ment, and ethical considerations. These dimensions do not exist in isolation; rather, they intersect to
form a complex landscape of opportunities and constraints that collectively shape how SMEs can engage
with 5IR imperatives. While much of the reviewed literature addresses 4IR challenges such as digital
integration, connectivity, and basic automation, these issues are interpreted in this study as structural
preconditions shaping readiness for 5IR principles. Persistent 4IR deficits constrain SMEs’ ability to engage
meaningfully with human-centric, ethical, and sustainable transformation agendas. In this sense, unre-
solved 4IR challenges do not contradict the 5IR framing but rather explain its uneven and aspirational
character in resource-constrained contexts. The synthesis not only maps what challenges exist, but also
explores how and why they persist, revealing systemic inertia, uneven capability distribution, and
policy-practice disconnects that hinder inclusive transformation.
4.1. Digital integration
Digital integration is the foundational enabler of 5IR transformation, encompassing the adoption of tech-
nologies such as AI, IoT, cloud computing, and data analytics (Chander et al., 2022). Across the literature,
a consistent pattern emerges: while larger urban enterprises are advancing rapidly, SMEs, particularly in
rural or underserved areas, struggle to adopt even foundational technologies (Philip & Williams, 2019).
This divide is not merely technical but structural, reflecting deeper inequities in funding access, broad-
band infrastructure, and digital leadership capacity. For example, studies by Abrahams (2024) and Sithole
and Ruhode (2021) demonstrate how poor connectivity and energy instability constrain SME competi-
tiveness. These are not isolated issues but part of a broader governance challenge. Although initiatives
like “South Africa Connect” aim to redress digital inequity, their fragmented execution underscores a
broader trend: policy ambition frequently outpaces implementation capability. Hence, the issue is not
just the absence of digital tools, but the failure to create an enabling ecosystem for their sustained use
among SMEs.
4.2. Workforce readiness
Workforce readiness refers to the availability of digital, emotional, and cognitive competencies required
to thrive in human-machine collaborative environments. The literature foregrounds a critical insight:
upskilling cannot be treated as a one-off intervention but must be embedded within continuous

The analysis suggests that the skills gap in SMEs is not only a symptom of underinvestment but also a
product of policy and educational misalignment. Moreover, this readiness gap risks compounding
socio-economic inequality, as SMEs, especially those employing marginalised groups, are left behind in
the race toward digital inclusion. Therefore, bridging the skills divide must be approached as a strategic
priority requiring coordinated action across education, industry, and policy domains (Arora et al., 2024).
4.3. Policy alignment
Policy alignment emerges as a decisive factor in shaping the trajectory of SME readiness. National frame-
works such as the NDP 2030 and the White Paper on Science, Technology, Innovation (STI) present
visionary goals, yet lack the operational granularity needed to support SMEs in navigating complex dig-
ital transitions (Masarira & Msweli, 2013). The analysis reveals a striking policy-practice disconnect:
although multiple policy instruments exist, their fragmented governance, across ministries, provinces,
and local governments, creates confusion and inefficiency. Literature (Buthelezi & van Eck, 2024; Josopu,
2023) indicates that SME support is often incidental rather than intentional within broader innovation
policies. This suggests that policy failure is not just a matter of oversight but of systemic design. SMEs
require integrated, multi-scalar support systems that align digital strategy with inclusion, ethics, and
capacity-building objectives (Henderson, 2020). Without such alignment, policies risk entrenching existing
inequalities by serving well-resourced firms while marginalising under-resourced SMEs.
4.4. Ethical considerations
Ethical readiness is arguably the least developed dimension across the literature but is essential to the
ethos of 5IR, which prioritises human-centricity, sustainability, and fairness. While some legal frameworks
like Protection of Personal Information Act (POPIA) offer minimum protections, most SMEs operate with-
out clear ethical guidance on AI deployment, data governance, or algorithmic transparency. Studies
(Kumar et al., 2024; Mokofe, 2023) point to widespread gaps in ethical awareness and institutional capa-
bility. More importantly, ethical lapses are not just technical vulnerabilities—they are development risks
that could exacerbate exclusion, surveillance, or discrimination, especially in marginalised communities.
The analysis underscores the need to move beyond compliance toward proactive ethical innovation. The
absence of Corporate Digital Responsibility (CDR) frameworks, digital ethics training, and inclusive tech-
nology assessment tools limits SMEs’ ability to embed ethical foresight into business processes (Elliott &
Copilah-Ali, 2024). Without deliberate action, ethical failure could become the defining constraint of dig-
ital transformation.
4.5. Synthesis across dimensions
Collectively, these four dimensions reveal that SME readiness is not a linear function of technology adop-
tion but a systemic capacity built through the interplay of infrastructure, human capital, institutional align-
ment, and ethical stewardship. Challenges in one domain, such as digital integration, often cascade into
others, such as workforce development or ethical risk management. Importantly, the literature synthesis
shows that addressing these challenges in isolation is insufficient. Although much of the reviewed literature
addresses challenges traditionally associated with the 4IR, such as digital infrastructure, connectivity, and
skills gaps, these challenges are analytically relevant to the 5IR context. In Global South contexts, progress
toward the human-centric goals of the 5IR is often constrained by unresolved 4IR challenges. For many
SMEs, basic digital integration, connectivity, and skills development remain incomplete. These structural

deficits create path dependencies that limit SMEs’ ability to engage with advanced 5IR principles such as
ethical AI, sustainability, and human–machine collaboration. Accordingly, this study treats 4IR challenges
not as conceptually separate from the 5IR, but as structural conditions that shape the pace and depth of
5IR readiness. The synthesis demonstrates that without resolving these foundational constraints, SMEs
struggle to engage meaningfully with 5IR principles such as human–machine collaboration, ethical AI, and
inclusive innovation. The HTIM captures this tension by explicitly mapping how low levels of digital inte-

An integrated approach is required, one that recognises the interdependence of digital tools, inclusive gov-

interlinked challenges in a structured, human-centric manner.
5. Human-technology impact matrix
The HTIM (see Figure 2) is introduced as a conceptual typology derived from grounded theory–informed
thematic synthesis of 22 studies, including both interdisciplinary and core IS literature. Developed during
the axial and selective coding phases, the matrix provides a two-dimensional classification of SME digital
transformation challenges, mapping them along the axes of structural complexity (simple to complex)
and degree of human-centricity (low to high). This framework is intended to guide policymakers, IS
researchers, and SME leaders in diagnosing systemic readiness and designing tailored interventions in
the context of the 5IR.
5.1. Constructs definition and theoretical anchoring
The two primary constructs of the matrix are defined as follows:
• Human-Centricity refers to the extent to which digital transformation initiatives within SMEs empha-
sise ethical foresight, stakeholder inclusion, human-machine collaboration, and workforce empower-
ment. This dimension draws from Socio-Technical Systems Theory (Bostrom & Heinen, 1977),
Responsible Innovation (Stahl et al., 2016), and the ICT4D paradigm (Heeks, 2009; Walsham, 2017),

which collectively advocate for the prioritisation of human values, equity, and developmental out-
comes in IS design and adoption.
• Structural Complexity captures the degree of systemic intricacy involved in addressing a particular
challenge. It reflects the extent of institutional misalignment, multi-stakeholder dependency, and
regulatory or infrastructural fragmentation. This construct is informed by Institutional Theory (Avgerou,
2008; Scott, 2008) and the TOE framework (Tornatzky & Fleischer, 1990), particularly the environmen-
tal dimension encompassing policy, governance, and infrastructure.
Together, these axes form four conceptual quadrants, each representing a unique configuration of
readiness barriers and enabling conditions.
5.2. Quadrant descriptions and examples
5.2.1. Quadrant I: Human-centric & simple
This quadrant includes challenges that are human-centered but relatively low in structural complexity.
These may involve basic skills gaps, digital literacy deficits among youth and women, or limited aware-
ness of ethical AI use. Solutions are often low-cost, such as short-term public-private training partner-
ships, SETA-funded workshops, or SME-specific digital awareness campaigns. These challenges are highly
addressable through targeted, bottom-up initiatives without requiring systemic reform. This quadrant
may include SMEs operating with minimal digital infrastructure and limited human capacity, such as
informal retail or township-based manufacturing SMEs relying on manual processes and basic mobile
communication. Digital tools, where present, are used primarily for transactional purposes (e.g. WhatsApp
orders or mobile payments), with little strategic integration or consideration of employee skills, data
ethics, or long-term capability development.
5.2.2. Quadrant II: Human-centric & complex
Challenges in this quadrant require deep alignment between educational institutions, labor markets,
and technological innovation systems. Examples include misalignment between national curricula and
5IR labor demands, the absence of AI ethics boards, or the marginalisation of SMEs in policy design.
These are systemic and interdependent, necessitating long-term reforms, e.g. overhauling
education-to-work pipelines or embedding CDR frameworks into national innovation strategies. This
quadrant reflects principles from RRI and calls for collaborative governance models. SMEs in this quad-
rant may have invested in advanced digital systems such as Enterprise Resource Planning (ERP) plat-
forms, cloud-based supply chain tools, or data analytics, often driven by industry or client requirements.
However, these technologies are introduced without adequate employee training, participatory design,
or ethical governance. For example, a manufacturing SME may adopt automation technologies to
improve efficiency while simultaneously experiencing worker resistance, skills mismatches, or concerns
about job displacement.
5.2.3. Quadrant III: Tech-centric & simple
These are primarily infrastructural or technological barriers with low institutional complexity. Examples
include broadband access deficits, unreliable electricity, or lack of access to productivity platforms. While
such challenges are severe, their solutions, such as targeted subsidies, infrastructure rollouts, or SME
cloud adoption support, are well understood and operationally manageable. This quadrant corresponds
with the technological context of the TOE framework and highlights the need for executional efficiency
more than systemic change. This quadrant includes SMEs that prioritise employee inclusion, skills devel-
opment, and community engagement but operate within significant infrastructural and financial con-
straints. For instance, a small agri-processing SME may invest in digital literacy training and participatory
decision-making while relying on basic digital tools due to unreliable connectivity or limited access to
finance. Such firms demonstrate strong alignment with 5IR principles but face systemic barriers to scaling
their digital capabilities.

5.2.4. Quadrant IV: Tech-centric & complex
This quadrant comprises deeply embedded systemic barriers that are both technologically intensive and
institutionally misaligned. Examples include fragmented ICT policy regimes, intergovernmental coordina-
tion failures, or regulatory lag in AI and data governance. Addressing these challenges requires multi-scalar,
cross-departmental reforms and innovation ecosystems. These issues reflect the environmental dimension
of TOE and align with broader IS governance challenges in emerging economies. SMEs positioned in this
quadrant exhibit both advanced digital capabilities and a strong commitment to human-centric and eth-
ical practices. An example may include a medium-sized professional services SME that integrates
AI-supported decision tools alongside continuous staff upskilling, transparent data governance policies,
and inclusive innovation practices. These firms are better positioned to pursue sustainable digital trans-
formation aligned with 5IR ideals, although such cases remain relatively rare in the South African SME
context.
5.3. Analytical and practical implications
The matrix provides both analytical and applied value. Analytically, it extends IS theorisation by introduc-
ing a socio-technical typology that is sensitive to both micro-level human dynamics and macro-level
structural enablers, a feature often missing in traditional digital readiness models (DeLone & McLean,
2003; Vial, 2019). Practically, it supports diagnostic assessments of SME ecosystems, helping policymakers
prioritise resource allocation, sequencing of interventions, and cross-sector collaboration. Future studies
can adapt this matrix by developing empirical instruments to measure structural complexity and
human-centricity as latent constructs, allowing for comparative SME readiness profiling across sectors or
regions. This operationalisation would facilitate quantitative validation and longitudinal tracking, trans-
forming the matrix into a robust IS planning tool for digital inclusion and innovation governance.
Furthermore, by incorporating examples from the literature (Josopu, 2023) on skills policy misalignment,
(Shibambu, 2025) on public sector integration, the matrix translates complex, cross-sectoral insights into
an actionable typology. This tool allows policymakers, researchers, and enterprise leaders to prioritise
interventions based on both the human-centred relevance and the complexity of transformation.
As a diagnostic tool, the HTIM can be applied in future empirical studies to assess SME ecosystems
or specific sectors. For example, human-centricity may be explored through indicators such as workforce
participation in digital decision-making, awareness of ethical data practices, investment in upskilling, or
the presence of inclusive innovation strategies. Structural complexity may be examined through indica-
tors such as policy fragmentation, reliance on public infrastructure, inter-organisational dependencies,
or regulatory uncertainty. These indicators are illustrative and can be adapted to different empirical
contexts, allowing the HTIM to support both qualitative case studies and mixed-methods research
designs.
6. Theoretical contribution
This study makes four substantive contributions to the IS discipline, particularly in the areas of digital
transformation, ICT4D, and socio-technical systems thinking. While TOE and RBV have traditionally guided
digital readiness research, the HTIM introduces ethical governance and human-centricity as primary diag-
nostic axes, thus responding to recent IS calls for context-sensitive, justice-oriented frameworks (Stahl
et al., 2016; Walsham, 2017).
6.1. Extending the TOE framework for 5IR contexts
First, the study extends the TOE framework (Baker, 2012; Tornatzky & Fleischer, 1990) by introducing a
5IR-specific interpretation that explicitly incorporates ethical governance, human-centric innovation,
and policy fragmentation as critical environmental factors. Whereas traditional TOE applications have
primarily emphasised regulatory or market forces, this study foregrounds normative and institutional
dimensions, such as AI ethics and inclusive governance, that shape SME readiness in developing

contexts. This contribution is especially relevant given the increasing complexity of digital transforma-
tion under 5IR conditions (Vial, 2019).
6.2. Operationalising socio-technical systems theory
Second, the study contributes to Socio-Technical Systems Theory (Bostrom & Heinen, 1977) by offering
an operational matrix, the HTIM, that captures the intersection of human-centricity and structural com-
plexity in SME digital readiness. This matrix serves as a conceptual tool for IS scholars and practitioners
to classify digital transformation challenges across four domains: Human-Centric & Simple, Human-Centric
& Complex, Tech-Centric & Simple, and Tech-Centric & Complex. It offers an empirically grounded
response to calls in the IS field for context-sensitive and actionable socio-technical models (Stahl et al.,
2016; Walsham, 2017).
6.3. Bridging ICT4D with ethical IS innovation
Third, the study contributes to the ICT4D literature by synthesising empirical and theoretical insights
from South Africa into a multidimensional framework that considers digital inclusion, capacity constraints,
and institutional misalignments. Drawing on Heeks (2009) ICT4D 2.0 agenda and Avgerou’s (2008) call for
critical IS research in developing countries, the matrix foregrounds the role of systemic inequality, power
asymmetries, and policy incoherence in shaping IS outcomes. By embedding ethical foresight and CDR
into the framework, the study addresses a significant gap in the literature where normative consider-
ations are often peripheral to adoption models.
6.4. Advancing is theory through multi-scalar integration
Finally, this study demonstrates the utility of multi-scalar theorisation in IS. While prior research has
treated digital readiness as either an organisational or policy-level issue, this study integrates insights
across levels, from micro-level workforce skills to macro-level policy design. In doing so, it aligns with
Vial’s (2019) call for holistic IS models that move beyond adoption to consider strategic transformation,
and with Stahl et al. (2016) who advocate for IS research that engages with ethical, political, and social
implications of emerging technologies. The HTIM offers a transferable analytical scaffold that can be
applied to other emerging economy contexts and future studies of IS capability building, ethics, and
governance.
In summary, this study contributes to the IS discipline by bridging empirical insights with established
theoretical models, extending their relevance to resource-constrained and ethically complex digital envi-
ronments. It responds to emerging imperatives in IS research to move beyond deterministic or
one-size-fits-all frameworks, instead proposing an integrative, ethically grounded, and developmentally
sensitive approach to SME digital transformation in the age of 5IR.
7. Discussion and conclusion
This study examined the readiness of South African SMEs for digital transformation by conducting a
systematic thematic synthesis of 22 interdisciplinary and IS studies. The analysis revealed that SME read-
iness is shaped not only by technological access but also by human capacity, institutional alignment, and
ethical governance. Rather than extending any single theoretical tradition, the study synthesises comple-
mentary IS perspectives, TOE, RBV, Socio-Technical Systems Theory, Institutional Theory, and ICT4D, to
explain how technological, organisational, human, and institutional dynamics interact in
resource-constrained environments. In doing so, the study responds to calls for context-sensitive and
ethically grounded IS theorising in the Global South. The proposed HTIM synthesises these findings into
a diagnostic framework that maps readiness challenges along two dimensions: structural complexity and
human-centricity. The findings suggest that while SMEs in South Africa are making gradual progress in
adopting digital technologies, structural barriers such as fragmented policy implementation and weak

skills pipelines continue to limit their ability to fully benefit from digital transformation. Moreover, ethical
considerations remain underdeveloped, raising concerns about inclusion, equity, and sustainability in
SME digital futures. By highlighting these multidimensional challenges, the study contributes to ICT4D

importantly, it responds to ongoing debates in digital development by emphasising that technology
adoption alone is insufficient; inclusive, ethical, and policy-coherent strategies are required for meaning-

8. Implications, limitations and future research
8.1. Practical and policy implications
This study yields several implications for policymakers, practitioners, and researchers concerned with
advancing SME digital transformation in developing contexts.
National digital strategies should move beyond rhetorical commitments to the “4IR” or “5IR” and
provide concrete, implementable pathways that address SME-specific constraints. Greater cross-sector
coordination is required to minimise policy fragmentation and ensure that SMEs are deliberately
incorporated into digital development agendas. Workforce development initiatives must be contex-
tualised to SME realities, linking digital skills acquisition directly to productivity, innovation, and
sustainability outcomes. Collaborative partnerships among government, higher education, and indus-
try are essential to align training content with the practical needs of SME sectors. SME digitalisation
efforts should be guided by adapted ethical frameworks, including CDR and responsible AI guide-
lines. Embedding ethical foresight in SME operations will help prevent digital transformation from
deepening social and economic inequalities. The proposed HTIM offers a diagnostic tool for policy-
makers and development agencies to assess readiness gaps, prioritise interventions, and monitor
progress across sectors. Future empirical studies may operationalise the matrix into measurable indi-
cators of human-centricity and structural complexity to facilitate comparative analyses and policy
benchmarking.
8.2. Theoretical implications for is research
From a theoretical standpoint, the HTIM contributes to IS scholarship by introducing a diagnostic frame-
work that bridges individual, organisational, and institutional layers of digital transformation. The matrix
extends the TOE framework by incorporating ethical and governance dimensions under its environmen-
tal pillar. It also operationalises Socio-Technical Systems Theory by mapping the dynamic interplay
between human capacity and systemic complexity, an interplay that is underexplored in traditional IS
adoption models. Furthermore, the matrix addresses a significant gap in ICT4D and IS-for-development
literature, where ethical foresight and policy coherence are often treated as separate research concerns.
By integrating these themes, the study offers a unified theoretical scaffold for understanding 5IR readi-
ness in resource-constrained environments, thus contributing to IS theory-building in Global South
contexts.
8.3. Limitations and future research
The study is limited by its reliance on secondary literature rather than primary empirical data. While the
thematic synthesis provides a conceptually rich framework, further validation through SME case studies,
surveys, or longitudinal designs is needed. Future research should also test the transferability of the HTIM
across different sectors and regions of the Global South. Despite these limitations, the study demon-
strates that a human-centric, systemic approach to SME digital transformation is both necessary and
achievable. By recognising the interplay of infrastructure, human capital, policy, and ethics, the HTIM
framework offers a foundation for more inclusive and sustainable digital futures in South Africa
and beyond.
