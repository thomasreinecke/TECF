ABSTRACT Governments seek to implement digital transformation initiatives to improve the effectiveness
of their public services. To achieve this, governments need to assess their IT environment and evaluate their
digitalmaturity.Theconventionaldigitalmaturityassessmentframeworks,mainlyconstructedbyconsulting
firms,oftenfailwhenappliedtothepublicsectorduetothedifferencesbetweenthebusinessandgovernment
sectorsintermsoftheiroperationalmissionsandactivities.Therefore,amaturityassessmentframeworkthat
accommodates the unique requirements of governments and their operating environments is necessary. The
novelty of this research lies in its contribution to digital transformation in the public sector by presenting a
framework specifically designed to assess the digital transformation maturity of government organizations.
This research is based on analyzing the factors of maturity assessment models developed by industry leaders
from three leading global consulting firms. Insights from the academic literature on these factors were
extracted, and a comprehensive survey was conducted with 88 IT experts from government institutes across
six countries to ensure their relevance and applicability in real-world government settings. In this research,
we tested our hypothesis on whether there is a significant relationship between the importance of digital
transformation factors and their implementation. The analysis indicated that there is no strong statistical
relationship between the importance of digital transformation maturity factors and their implementation.
The framework helps assess digital maturity and supports the formulation of effective strategies to drive
successful digital transformation initiatives in government.

IV. BACKGROUND OF DIGITAL MATURITY
Digital transformation plays a key role in enhancing an
organization’s overall success by supporting many if not all,
elements within the 7-S framework. Originally developed
in 1980 by consultants at McKinsey & Company at the
time, the 7-S framework posits multiple factors—Structure,
Strategy, Skills, Staff, Style, Systems, and higher Goals—
that shape an organization’s effectiveness. They argued that
the alignment of these elements is essential to achieving
optimal effectiveness within an organization [20], and that
the relationship between these factors constitutes is a critical
successfactorforchange[21].AneffectiveITstrategyshould
be integrated with the overall strategy of the organization
without causing any disruption. Rather, it should be an
extension or enhancement by providing the technological
means of developing and improving the organization’s per-
formance [22] and measure progress towards the anticipated
digital maturity. As a key dimension, the strategy should
integrate components from the business and technology
aspects of the organization, define a clear vision of the
desired outcome, assess the current level of digital maturity,
and identify any gaps between the current and target digital
maturity levels.
Furthermore,organizationsstrivingtoenhancetheirdigital
maturity implement a gradual digital transformation, inte-
grating technology into their operations, staff, and resources.
Achieving the desired level of digital maturity requires
a clearly defined digital strategy, supported by effective
governance. This strategy should also ensure the necessary
infrastructure and structures while considering the human
element, particularly the readiness of management and
employees for digitalization, as well as the necessary skills
and competencies. Enhancing these attributes is essential for
achieving digital maturity [22].
Digital maturity assessment enables organizations to
measure their progress in digital transformation and supports
them in developing strategies to reach advanced levels
of digital evolution in the future [23]. Research in [24]
shows that digitally mature organizations have an outstand-
ing financial performance compared to their less mature
competitors. An organization’s maturity level reflects a
stage in its development efforts and is an indicator of
its performance across various areas. The terms ‘‘maturity
stage’’ and ‘‘maturity level’’ are used interchangeably and
theyareusedsynonymouslyintheacademicliteraturetorefer
to this concept [24].
The organization can measure its digital maturity using
maturity models, which provide a structured framework
with distinct levels or stages. the organization can iden-
tify its specific maturity levels, by assessing its digital
transformation efforts. According to Haryanti et al., and
Schumacher et al. [37], a maturity model aims to assess,
and compare the organization’s or process’s current maturity
level with its desired level. This is done by identifying and
measuring their gaps [23]. In recent years, various maturity
models have been developed to assess digital maturity in
organizations, to support effective leadership and direction
for digital transformation [24]. A maturity model serves
as a valuable tool that helps organizations guide their

The scientific literature also presents several maturity
models that organizations can leverage to assess their
maturity level. The model presented by Mittal et al. [38]
utilized a five-level maturity framework (novice, beginner,
learner, intermediate, expert) and considered five dimen-
sions: finance, people, strategy, process, and product [31].
Bittighofer et al. [39] conducted a study on the maturity level
of Industry 4.0 in German companies, specifically examining
six dimensions: product development process, steering and
control,manufacturingandoperation,smartservices,process
organization, and big data [31]. Hamidi et al. [40] assessed
Industry 4.0 readiness through six dimensions: strategy
and organization, smart factory, smart products, data-driven
services,smartoperations,andemployees. Thesedimensions
were evaluated on a Likert scale ranging from 0 to 5
[31]. Sheen and Yang [41] presented a self-assessment of
readiness specifically designed for handicraft enterprises.
The assessment focused on three dimensions: produc-
tion/operations, digitalization, and ecosystem. Within these
dimensions, a total of 23 items were assessed, including
factors such as data security, perception of digitalization,
and quality of internet connection. The assessment used a
Likert scale ranging from 1 to 5 [31]. Hizam-Hanafiah et al.
considered the dimensions of Technology, People, Strategy,
Leadership, Process, and Innovation to be critical for
organizations of all sizes and industries and universally
important to have a significant impact on an organization’s
success, regardless of its specific context [32]. Chinar et al.
conducted a literature review to identify existing maturity
models that assess an organization’s readiness to adopt of
Industry 4.0 technologies, determine whether these models
are comprehensive enough, and identify their strengths and
weaknesses. This resulted in a total of 16 distinct MMs in the
literature [33]. However, none of the maturity models found
in the literature discusses the digital maturity of government
organizations.
Effectively managing the full scope of digital transfor-
mation requires a coordinated and comprehensive approach.
Thisbroadperspectiveenablestheintegrationandmonitoring

the organization [34]. Achieving digital maturity requires the
gradual integration of organizational processes, talent, and
resources into digital workflow. To reach the highest levels
of digital maturity, a well-defined digital strategy is essential,
as its absence can lead to misguided decisions. Additionally,
the presence of the necessary infrastructure, structures, and
specialized units is crucial for progress. The human element,
including the appropriate skills and expertise, is a key factor
inenhancingdigitalmaturity.Ultimately,thejourneytowards

essential components [22].
V. METHODOLOGY
This paper outlines an approach to creating a framework
for assessing digital transformation maturity systemically
and comprehensively. The framework was developed to
meet the specific needs of government organizations.
The methodology included several critical stages, namely:
1) analyzing existing maturity assessment models from
industry consultancy firms to identify relevant assessment
factors, 2) reviewing the scientific literature to ensure its
scientific validity of these factors, and 3) collaborating
with 88 Subject Matter Experts (SMEs) with technol-
ogy expertise from various government entities across six
Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC) countries. These experts
provided insights to refine the assessment factors and
contribute to the development of the framework. Fig. 2
illustrates the research stages and steps followed in each
stage.
The process began with an in-depth review of existing
Maturity Assessment models to identify an initial set of
assessmentfactors.Theemphasiswasplacedonthreemodels
developed by reputable firms [35], to analyze these factors
and select the most appropriate ones for the government
context. The initial factors identified were 90 factors. After
factors analysis and consolidation of the factors, the final list
was reduced to 34 factors. Moving into the next phase of
the research, a systematic literature review was conducted,
searching academic journals and publications related to
digital transformation. This was done to examine research
conducted in the government sector and determine the
scientific basis for each previously identified assessment
factor. The review ensured the scientific basis for each
assessment factor and established its validity and relevance
in existing research. It also contributed to prioritizing the
assessment factors by assigning them appropriate weights
based on their frequency and relevance in the literature.
To enhance the validity and reliability of the assessment

FACTORS LIST
In developing a model specifically tailored to government
entities, existing digital transformation maturity models were
analyzed to compile a comprehensive list of factors, selected
specifically to align with the government context. These
factors were organized into eight categories, reflecting the
common themes and dimensions identified across the mod-

landscape,itsdevelopmentgoals,andhowthisalignswiththe

Thiscategoryconsolidatesfactorsrelatedtoanorganization’s
assets, and capabilities from a digital perspective and how
it adopts emerging technologies. Classifying the factors into
these criteria or domains provides an organization with the
opportunity to assess its digital maturity systematically and
helps target the improvements in specific areas of its digital
transformation efforts. In [35] illustrates the list of factors for
assessing the digital maturity of government organizations.
It also indicates the distribution of factors in the models
created by the consulting firms and the frequency of each
factor in these models. To extend this, the factors were
researched in the literature to obtain scholarly input on the
relevance and inform the selection process for inclusion in
the new hybrid model.
B. FACTORS ELIGIBILITY & LITERATURE VALIDATION
After preparing the initial list of factors, a thorough review
of selected references from published research in scientific
journals was conducted. This process aimed to validate
selected factors through available academic research. The
review process included a careful examination of relevant
scientific articles to ensure that the selected factors were
consistent with the findings presented in Table 1 of the
references. This validation step adds credibility to the
expected government-specific maturity assessment model.

For the preliminary search using keywords, Scopus served as
the primary data source. We selected the Scopus database for
its extensive coverage, as it is one of the largest repositories
of research papers related to digital transformation factors.
Its relevance was confirmed through a comparative analysis
with the Big Four digital transformation consultancy firms,
aligning with industry standards. Scopus provides access to
abstracts, citations, and scholarly documents across various
disciplines. Moreover, it is widely used for literature reviews
and research assessments. The search process imposed
a time constraint to include all final English-language

limited to computer science, engineering, and business
administration.

VII. RESEARCH FINDINGS AND REMARKS
Looking at the results, we find that the factors in most of
the criteria scored very high according to the survey analysis.
Both the literature and experts have recognized these criteria,
including strategy, culture, talent, infrastructure, finance, and
innovation, underscoring their importance in driving digital
transformation.
We observed significant variation in scores on the Strategy
and Vision, scores scale across job titles. The analysis
revealed that participants in academic and consulting roles
scored significantly lower than those in leadership and
management. This may indicate that the perceptions of these
areas depend on the participant’s professional technological
background, as well as their history and current roles and
responsibilities. Leadership and managerial roles are at the
top of the application areas and should implement the
mission and vision of the government institution and receive
instructions directly from the top management, typically the
minister. Infrastructure also scored significantly lower than
technology experts, who may have a more critical view of
current infrastructure than managers.
On the other hand, the distribution of scores on the
Responsive Operations and Supply Chain, varied across
gender categories, with Females rating it as significantly
important, highlighting potential differences in how they per-
ceiveoperationalefficiencyand supply chainresponsiveness.
Funding and Resource Allocation also received significantly
higher scores from females than males, suggesting that
women may view funding and resource allocation practices
more positively or place greater importance on these factors.
The third criterion that differed across genders was Culture,
Talent, and Skills, which received higher average scores from
female participants, suggesting that workplace culture, talent
development, and skill acquisition are more highly valued
or experienced by this group. Gender-based behavior and its
characteristics are beyond the scope of this research, but it
was worth highlighting these findings to open future research
in this area.
Implementing each of these factors contributes to govern-
ment organizations’ AI readiness by creating a supportive
environment for its adoption. For example, government
organizations need to develop AI strategies that align with
their broader organizational objectives. They should foster
an environment that supports creative thinking and the
exploration of AI solutions. They should also foster a
culture of continuous learning, openness to change, and
ethical AI practices to embrace AI, which is critical for its
successful implementation. Each of these digital transforma-
tion factors has its unique role to play in establishing AI
readiness.
Identifying and effectively implementing high-value fac-
tors enables governments to replicate successful practices
while addressing implementation barriers in other areas.
While government IT leaders recognize the critical impor-
tance of digital transformation factors, the extent to which
they are implemented varies. This discrepancy reveals
an opportunity to enhance alignment between perceived
importance and actual implementation, ultimately improving
the maturity and success of digital transformation within
government organizations.
Some more untapped opportunities and areas need further
development to achieve a more mature digital transformation
landscape in the public sector. An example of this could
be developing mechanisms and measuring progress over
time for each factor in each area or providing guide-
lines and recommendations for each maturity factor to
help government organizations improve their application
and consequently their maturity level. Areas that require
enhancement in application extend to cover, for example,
Operational flexibility and resilience. Those are factors that
received very high ranks in importance yet struggled in
application, suggesting the presence of challenges preventing
their full implementation in practice. This dictates further
investigation to identify the underlying reasons and address
potential obstacles to effective application.
VIII. CONCLUSION
Other unused opportunities and areas need further develop-
ment to achieve a more mature digital transformation envi-
ronment in the public sector. Examples include developing
mechanismsandmeasuringprogressovertimeforeachfactor
in each area, or providing guidelines and recommendations
for each maturity factor to help government entities improve
their implementation and, consequently, their maturity level.
Findings suggest that government organizations place value
on key areas such as security and leadership. Areas requiring
improvement in implementation include, for example, oper-
ational flexibility and resilience. These factors were highly
rated for importance but faced difficulties in implementation,
indicating challenges that prevent their full implementation
in practice. This requires further research to identify the
underlying causes and address potential obstacles that may
hinder their effective implementation.
The study’s findings open up opportunities for further
researchandanalysis.Theseapproachesextendcollaboration
with SMEs to conduct a qualitative study exploring the
challenges and obstacles to implementing the recognized
factors. Another recommendation is to conduct case studies
within government institutions to examine their specific
circumstances, capabilities, and constraints, helping to tailor
approaches and strategies for implementing digital transfor-
mation maturity factors.
