Abstract
This study examines how digital transformation in higher education institutions (HEIs)
contributes to public value creation, moving beyond efficiency-oriented narratives toward
broader societal outcomes. Using a PRISMA-ScR approach, the study systematically re-
views 47 peer-reviewed articles published between 2013 and 2025 across major academic
databases. The review maps the evolution of scholarship and identifies the key mechanisms
through which digital transformation influences public value. The findings reveal three
interrelated dimensions shaping outcomes: digital competencies, institutional readiness,
and governance alignment. Digital competencies enable the effective adoption and use of
technologies, while institutional readiness—comprising digital infrastructure, leadership
capacity, and organizational culture—acts as a mediating condition influencing imple-
mentation success. Governance alignment, including regulatory coherence, accountability
mechanisms, and stakeholder engagement, plays a moderating role in determining whether
digital transformation initiatives generate inclusive and socially beneficial outcomes. In
addition to positive outcomes such as improved access, service quality, and transparency,
the review identifies critical risks—including digital inequality, data governance challenges,
and algorithmic bias—that may constrain public value creation, particularly in resource-
constrained and Global South contexts. Building on these findings, the study develops
the Global Digital Transformation—Public Value Creation (G-DTPVC) framework as an
evidence-synthesized model derived from the reviewed literature. The framework specifies
key constructs, causal relationships, and indicative measures to support future empirical
research and policy application. By linking digital transformation processes in HEIs to
broader public value outcomes and Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs 4, 9, and 16),
this study advances theoretical understanding and provides actionable, context-sensitive
guidance for policymakers and institutional leaders seeking to foster inclusive, accountable,
and resilient higher education systems.

1. Introduction
Digital transformation has become a defining feature of contemporary higher edu-
cation systems, fundamentally reshaping how institutions design, deliver, and govern
teaching, research, and administrative functions. Higher education institutions (HEIs)
increasingly deploy digital technologies—including learning management systems, arti-
ficial intelligence, big data analytics, cloud computing, and e-governance platforms—to
enhance operational efficiency, expand access, and improve service delivery [1–4]. While
these developments have generated measurable improvements in institutional performance
and responsiveness, the dominant discourse remains largely techno-centric, emphasizing
efficiency gains and innovation outputs rather than broader societal impacts.
In contrast, the concept of public value shifts attention from internal organizational
performance to outcomes that benefit society at large. In higher education, public value
encompasses equitable access to education, transparency and accountability in governance,
responsiveness to stakeholder needs, and contributions to social mobility and democratic
development [5–7]. As public and quasi-public institutions, universities play a critical
role in advancing inclusive development and knowledge-based economies. Consequently,
digital transformation represents not only a technological shift but also a key mechanism
through which public value can be created—or undermined.
Despite this relevance, the relationship between digital transformation and public
value in HEIs remains conceptually fragmented and empirically underdeveloped. A
significant body of literature adopts information systems and innovation perspectives,
focusing on technology adoption, digital maturity, and institutional readiness [3,6,8,9].
While these approaches provide valuable insights into organizational capabilities and
performance, they rarely connect digital transformation processes to explicit public value
outcomes such as equity, legitimacy, transparency, or societal impact. Conversely, research
on public value and digital governance has developed robust theoretical insights into
value co-creation, accountability, and citizen-centric service delivery, yet these perspectives
are only sporadically applied within higher education contexts [7,10,11]. This disconnect
constrains a holistic understanding of how digital transformation can be aligned with the
broader societal mission of universities.
The urgency of addressing this gap was further underscored by the COVID-19 pan-
demic, which accelerated the adoption of digital technologies across HEIs worldwide.
While this rapid transition enabled continuity in teaching, learning, and administration, it
also exposed structural inequalities in access to digital infrastructure, institutional capacity,
and digital competencies—particularly in Global South contexts [12,13]. These disparities
highlight that digital transformation is not merely a technical process but a complex socio-
institutional phenomenon shaped by organizational readiness, governance arrangements,
and contextual constraints.
Emerging scholarship increasingly conceptualizes digital transformation in higher
education as an integrated process involving technological, organizational, and governance
dimensions. Institutional readiness—comprising digital infrastructure, leadership capacity,
and organizational culture—has been identified as a critical condition for successful imple-
mentation [3,9,14]. Similarly, digital competencies among academic staff, administrators,
and students play a central role in shaping the effective use and sustainability of digital

comparative synthesis of how digital competencies, institutional readiness, and governance

Addressing this gap requires a comprehensive and methodologically rigorous synthe-
sis of existing scholarship. Scoping reviews guided by the PRISMA-ScR framework are
particularly suited to mapping heterogeneous bodies of literature, identifying thematic
patterns, and clarifying conceptual relationships in emerging fields. Accordingly, this study
undertakes a PRISMA-ScR review of peer-reviewed literature published between 2013
and 2025 to examine how digital transformation in higher education contributes to public
value creation.
This study advances the literature by developing an integrated, empirically grounded
conceptual framework that systematically links digital competencies, institutional readi-
ness, and governance alignment to public value creation in higher education. While
extant research has examined these dimensions independently, a critical gap remains in
understanding their interdependencies and cumulative effects on digital transformation
outcomes. Addressing this limitation, the framework proposed in this study is inductively
derived from a PRISMA-ScR synthesis of 47 peer-reviewed studies, thereby ensuring strong
empirical traceability and analytical robustness. Rather than functioning as a purely ab-
stract construct, the framework serves as a multi-level analytical lens that captures dynamic
interactions between individual capabilities, organizational conditions, and institutional
governance structures. The study further contributes to theory by integrating insights from
public value theory, institutional theory, and the technology–organization–environment
(TOE) framework into a coherent explanatory model. In doing so, it provides a structured
basis for understanding how digital transformation processes can generate inclusive, effi-
cient, and sustainable public value outcomes—particularly within resource-constrained
and policy-fragmented higher education systems.
Specifically, the study aims to: (1) map the evolution of research on digital transforma-
tion in HEIs; (2) identify the key drivers and conditions shaping transformation outcomes,
with particular attention to digital competencies, institutional readiness, and governance
alignment; and (3) synthesize these insights into an evidence-based analytical framework
that explains the pathways through which digital transformation generates public value.
By integrating insights across technological, organizational, and governance dimensions,

2. Literature Review
The literature on digital transformation in higher education has expanded significantly
over the past decade, reflecting growing scholarly and policy interest in how digital tech-
nologies reshape institutional processes and outcomes. Existing research can be broadly
organized into four interrelated dimensions: (i) technology-driven transformation, (ii) insti-
tutional readiness, (iii) digital competencies and human capabilities, and (iv) governance
and public value outcomes. Despite this expansion, the literature remains fragmented,
with limited integration across these dimensions and insufficient attention to how gover-
nance mechanisms mediate the relationship between digital transformation and public
value creation.
2.1. Technology-Driven Transformation in Higher Education
The first strand of research focuses on technology-driven change, emphasizing the
adoption and integration of digital tools such as learning management systems, artifi-
cial intelligence, cloud computing, and data analytics [1,3,10,20]. These studies highlight
improvements in teaching delivery, administrative efficiency, and data-driven decision-
making. Digital platforms have enabled flexible and personalized learning models, ex-
panded online and blended education, and supported institutional responsiveness to
changing demands [3,10,18].
However, this body of work often adopts a functionalist and linear perspective, treat-
ing digital transformation primarily as a process of technology adoption and implemen-
tation. As a result, it tends to underemphasize the broader organizational, governance,
and societal implications of digitalization. In particular, the focus on tools and systems
frequently obscures how digital transformation interacts with institutional contexts and
public value objectives.
2.2. Institutional Readiness and Organizational Capacity
A second strand of literature emphasizes institutional readiness as a critical condition
for successful digital transformation. Institutional readiness encompasses digital infrastruc-
ture, leadership commitment, governance capacity, organizational culture, and strategic
alignment [1,3,6,9,10].
Empirical studies and digital maturity models suggest that many HEIs remain at
early or intermediate stages of transformation, characterized by fragmented initiatives and
limited strategic coordination [3,9]. Robust infrastructure—comprising hardware, software,
and network systems—is essential for enabling digital teaching, learning, and administra-
tive functions, yet access to such infrastructure varies significantly across institutions and
regions [1,10,20].
Leadership and governance capacity are equally important. Research consistently
identifies strategic vision, institutional leadership, and clear accountability structures as
key enablers of sustained digital transformation [6,9,10]. Organizational culture further
shapes transformation outcomes: institutions characterized by openness to innovation,
collaboration, and adaptability are more likely to embed digital initiatives effectively, while
rigid structures and resistance to change can limit impact [1,6,7].

2.3. Digital Competencies and Human Capabilities
Alongside institutional conditions, a growing body of research highlights the role of
digital competencies and human capabilities in shaping digital transformation outcomes.
Access to digital resources—including connectivity, devices, and platforms—remains un-
even, reinforcing socio-economic and geographic inequalities [1,10,12,18,20]. Evidence
from the COVID-19 period shows that students from disadvantaged backgrounds faced
significant barriers to participation despite rapid digitalization [10,18,21,22].
Digital competencies extend beyond technical skills to include information literacy,
pedagogical application, and collaborative capabilities in digital environments [10,20,23].
Studiesconsistentlyfindthatgapsincompetenciesamongstaffandstudentscanundermine
the effectiveness of digital technologies and limit their contribution to learning outcomes
and institutional performance [1,20,24].
Importantly, these capabilities are not solely individual attributes but are shaped by
institutional support structures. Training programs, professional development, technical
support systems, and organizational incentives play a crucial role in enabling sustained
engagement with digital tools [10,24]. This highlights the interdependence between human
capabilities and institutional environments in digital transformation processes.
2.4. Digital Governance and Transformation Processes
A critical but underdeveloped dimension in the literature is digital governance, which
mediates how digital transformation unfolds within higher education institutions. Dig-
ital transformation processes span academic, administrative, and governance domains,
including teaching and learning systems, administrative platforms, and decision-support
tools [1,3,19].
Administrative digitalization—covering admissions, records, finance, and communica-
tion systems—has been shown to enhance efficiency and enable data-driven management.
However, many institutions experience fragmentation across units, limiting interoperability
and strategic use of data [3,7,9,25].
Governance digitalization involves the use of digital tools to enhance transparency,
accountability, and stakeholder participation. Emerging studies highlight the role of dash-
boards, business intelligence systems, and online engagement platforms in reshaping
institutional governance and public engagement [1,9,10]. At the same time, concerns re-
garding data ethics, surveillance, and algorithmic bias underscore the need for robust
governance frameworks to guide digital transformation [20,26,27]. Despite these insights,
governance is often treated as a secondary consideration rather than a central analytical
dimension. This limits understanding of how regulatory alignment, accountability mecha-
nisms, and stakeholder participation influence whether digital transformation generates
inclusive and sustainable outcomes.
2.5. Public Value Outcomes of Digital Transformation
A growing strand of literature explicitly examines how digital transformation con-
tributes to public value in higher education. Studies suggest that digitalization can expand
access to education, enhance service quality, and support social innovation, thereby con-
tributing to inclusive development and sustainability goals [10,12,21].
However, public value outcomes are uneven and context-dependent. While digital
technologies enabled continuity of learning during the COVID-19 pandemic, they also
exposed and, in some cases, intensified inequalities in access and participation [18,21,22].
Without targeted interventions—such as inclusive design, subsidized access, and context-
sensitive policies—digital transformation may disproportionately benefit already advan-
taged groups [10,12].

Transparency, accountability, and trust are also central dimensions of public value.
Digital governance tools can enhance openness and stakeholder engagement, but they also

Institutions that align digital strategies with public missions and ethical standards are
more likely to strengthen legitimacy and trust, whereas poorly governed digitalization may
erode confidence among stakeholders.
2.6. Contextual Moderators and Integrative Linkages
Across the literature, digital transformation in higher education is consistently shaped
by a range of contextual moderators, including socio-economic conditions, regulatory
environments, and cultural norms. These factors influence not only the feasibility of
digital initiatives but also the extent to which they generate meaningful public value
outcomes. EmpiricalstudiesfromLatinAmerica, Asia, Africa, andEasternEuropehighlight
how funding constraints, uneven digital infrastructure, and policy instability can limit
institutional readiness and exacerbate digital inequalities [1,6,10,19,21].
Regulatory frameworks play a particularly important role in shaping digital trans-
formation trajectories. Policies related to data protection, quality assurance, accreditation,
and digital governance determine whether technological innovations are effectively in-
stitutionalized or remain fragmented and peripheral [10]. At the same time, cultural
norms—such as attitudes toward technology adoption, authority structures, and equity—
affect organizational behavior, leadership approaches, and stakeholder engagement pro-
cesses [6,10,18,20,24].
Beyond these contextual influences, the literature increasingly points to the need for
integrative perspectives that connect technological, organizational, and societal dimensions
of digital transformation. Existing studies suggest that institutional readiness and digital
competencies interact to shape implementation processes, while governance mechanisms
influence whether these processes translate into inclusive and sustainable public value
outcomes [3,9,10,28]. In this regard, institutional readiness may function as a mediating
condition between digital capabilities and outcomes, while governance alignment and
contextual factors act as moderating influences.
Importantly, evidence also suggests the presence of feedback dynamics. Positive
public value outcomes—such as improved access, transparency, and stakeholder trust—can
reinforce institutional commitment, attract resources, and sustain digital transformation
efforts. Conversely, negative outcomes, including digital exclusion, governance failures, or
ethical concerns, may generate resistance, reduce trust, and trigger policy or organizational
backlash [6,7,9,10].
Despite these insights, the literature lacks a coherent analytical framework that sys-
tematically integrates digital competencies, institutional readiness, governance alignment,
and contextual moderators into a unified explanatory model. This gap limits the ability to
understand how digital transformation processes translate into public value across diverse
higher education systems. Accordingly, this study builds on the identified gaps to develop
an evidence-based analytical framework that captures the interdependencies among these
dimensions and clarifies the pathways through which digital transformation contributes to
public value creation in higher education.

3. Theoretical Framework
This study develops an integrated theoretical framework to explain how digital trans-
formation in higher education institutions (HEIs) translates into public value outcomes. In
line with the scoping review design, the framework is derived from the synthesis of the
reviewed literature and integrates complementary perspectives from public administration,
information systems, and organizational theory. Rather than advancing a standalone con-
ceptual model, the framework provides a structured lens for interpreting the relationships
identified across the 47 included studies.
At its core, the framework combines three primary theoretical anchors: the Technology–
Organization–Environment (TOE) framework, public value theory, and institutional theory.
These are further supported by complementary perspectives—including technology adoption
models (TAM/UTAUT), diffusion of innovation, social cognitive theory, and digital capital
theory—to capture micro-, meso-, and macro-level dynamics of digital transformation.
3.1. Public Value Theory as Normative Foundation
Public value theory provides the normative foundation of the framework by concep-
tualizing universities as public-serving institutions whose performance must be assessed
not only in terms of efficiency but also in relation to equity, transparency, accountability,
and societal impact [1,6,29,30]. In the context of digital transformation, value is created
when technology-enabled changes enhance inclusive access to education, improve quality,
strengthen governance accountability, and contribute to long-term human capital develop-
ment [3,31].
Recent scholarship emphasizes that digital transformation in public institutions must
move beyond service optimization toward broader value constellations that include citizen
participation, data rights, and fair distribution of digital benefits [29,30,32]. This orientation
aligns digital transformation in higher education with Sustainable Development Goals,
particularly SDG 4 (quality education), SDG 9 (innovation and infrastructure), and SDG 16
(inclusive and accountable institutions) [1,6].
3.2. TOE Framework as Structural Backbone
The Technology–Organization–Environment (TOE) framework provides the struc-
tural backbone for analyzing digital transformation processes. TOE posits that innovation
adoption and implementation are shaped by technological characteristics, organizational
conditions, and environmental contexts [1–3,10]. In this study, these dimensions are opera-
tionalized as follows:
Technological dimension → digital competencies and capabilities (skills, tools, and
digital resources)
Organizational dimension → institutional readiness (infrastructure, leadership, cul-
ture, and strategy)
Environmental dimension → governance alignment and contextual conditions (regu-
lation, policy, and socio-economic context)

3.3. Institutional Theory and Governance Alignment
Institutional theory complements the TOE framework by explaining how formal
regulations, normative expectations, and cultural-cognitive structures shape organizational
behavior. It is particularly relevant for understanding governance alignment, including
policy coherence, accountability mechanisms, and stakeholder engagement processes that
influence digital transformation outcomes [6,10].
Through this lens, governance is not treated as a passive environmental factor but as
an active mechanism that conditions how digital initiatives are interpreted, implemented,
and sustained. Institutional pressures—such as accreditation requirements, national digital
policies, and societal expectations—shape both the direction and legitimacy of digital
transformation in HEIs.
3.4. Micro- and Meso-Level Complementary Perspectives
To capture behavioral and capability-level dynamics, the framework incorporates
insights from technology adoption and learning theories. Technology Acceptance Mod-
els (TAM/UTAUT) explain how perceived usefulness, ease of use, and social influence
shape individual adoption of digital systems [33–36]. Social Cognitive Theory further
emphasizes the role of self-efficacy, training, and social reinforcement in sustaining digital
engagement [23,27].
Diffusion of Innovation theory extends this perspective by explaining how digital
initiatives spread within and across institutions, highlighting the importance of innovation
characteristics and institutional networks [1,3,37]. These perspectives collectively inform
the analysis of digital competencies, user engagement, and the institutionalization of
digital practices.
In addition, digital capital theory provides a critical lens on inequality, emphasizing
disparities in access to devices, connectivity, and digital skills [6,10,27]. This perspective is
particularly important for understanding why similar digital initiatives produce divergent
outcomes across socio-economic and geographic contexts.
3.5. Integrated Framework and Causal Pathways
The integration of these theoretical perspectives enables a multi-level explanation of
digital transformation and public value creation in higher education. Based on the synthesis
of the reviewed literature, the framework specifies the following relationships:
Digital competencies (technological dimension) enable the adoption and effective use
of digital systems
Institutional readiness (organizational dimension) acts as a mediating condition, shap-
ing implementation effectiveness
Governance alignment (environmental/institutional dimension) functions as a moder-
ating factor, influencing whether transformation processes generate inclusive and account-
able public value outcomes
Contextual conditions (e.g., socio-economic context, regulatory environment) further
moderate these relationships.
The framework also recognizes feedback dynamics, whereby positive public value
outcomes (e.g., improved access, transparency, and trust) reinforce institutional commit-
ment and resource mobilization, while negative outcomes (e.g., digital exclusion, ethical
concerns) may undermine sustainability and legitimacy.

3.7. Integrative Theoretical Foundations
Research on digital transformation in higher education draws on multiple theoretical
perspectives to explain how technologies are adopted, implemented, and institutionalized.
At the micro level, technology adoption models such as the Technology Acceptance Model
(TAM) and the Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT) highlight
the importance of perceived usefulness, ease of use, social influence, and facilitating
conditionsinshaping individualengagementwith digital systems[35,38]. These modelsare
particularly relevant for understanding faculty and student adoption of learning platforms
and digital tools.
At the organizational level, Diffusion of Innovation (DOI) theory explains how
digital initiatives spread within institutions, emphasizing the role of communication
channels, organizational culture, and institutional support in scaling innovations across
departments [27,35]. Complementing this perspective, the Technology–Organization–
Environment (TOE) framework underscores the importance of organizational capabilities,
leadership, and external environmental conditions in shaping institutional readiness for
digital transformation [10,39].
Despite their analytical value, these models are often applied in isolation and primarily
focus on adoption and implementation processes, with limited attention to broader societal
outcomes. Public value theory addresses this limitation by providing a normative lens that

This integrative foundation enables the study to move beyond descriptive accounts
of digital transformation toward a structured and comparative understanding of how,
and under what conditions, digital transformation in higher education contributes to
sustainable and public-oriented outcomes.
Conceptual Framework Development
4. Evidence-Synthesized Conceptual Framework: G-DTPVC
The conceptual framework proposed in this study is grounded in the systematic syn-
thesis of the reviewed literature and reflects recurrent patterns identified across empirical
studies; it is not introduced as an abstract or purely theoretical construct, but as an evidence-
based model that integrates key determinants of digital transformation in higher education.
Specifically, the framework captures dynamic and interdependent relationships between
digital competencies, institutional readiness, and governance alignment, positioning these
elements as mutually reinforcing components that collectively shape public value outcomes.
By consolidating insights from public value theory, institutional theory, and the technology–
organization–environment framework, the model provides a structured and theoretically
robust lens for analyzing complex transformation processes. Importantly, it addresses a
critical gap in the literature by moving beyond fragmented analyses toward a holistic and
relational perspective, thereby enhancing both explanatory power and practical relevance
for policymakers and institutional leaders. Building on the thematic synthesis of the 47 in-
cluded studies, this study further proposes the Governance–Digital Transformation–Public
Value Creation (G-DTPVC) framework as an analytically grounded model explaining how
digital transformation in higher education institutions (HEIs) generates public value under
varying institutional and governance conditions; unlike purely conceptual models, the
G-DTPVC framework is inductively derived from patterns identified across the reviewed
literature, directly addressing the fragmentation between technology adoption, organi-
zational capacity, and governance-oriented explanations of digital transformation, and
therefore functions both as an evidence-based synthesis and a theory-building contribution
that responds to gaps identified in prior scholarship.

The framework integrates complementary theoretical perspectives to explain the

emphasize factors such as perceived usefulness, organizational support, and environmental
conditions as critical to digital uptake [27,41,42]. However, these models primarily focus
on adoption processes and do not sufficiently explain how digital transformation translates
into broader societal outcomes.
To address this limitation, the framework incorporates public value theory, as devel-
oped by Mark H. Moore and further elaborated by Colin Talbot, which emphasizes the
role of public institutions in generating societal benefits such as equity, transparency, and

4.2. Core Constructs of the G-DTPVC Framework
The G-DTPVC framework is structured around three interrelated enabling constructs
digital competencies, institutional readiness, and governance alignment which collectively
shape public value outcomes.
Digital Competencies:
Digital competencies refer to the technical, cognitive, and adaptive capabilities re-
quired to effectively adopt and utilize digital technologies within HEIs. These include
digital literacy among staff and students, data analytics capabilities, and the ability to inte-
grate emerging technologies into teaching, research, and administrative processes [38,43].
Indicative measures include:
• ICT proficiency levels
• Staff training participation rates
• Extent of digital tool integration in academic and governance functions
Institutional Readiness:
Institutional readiness captures the organizational conditions that enable or constrain
digital transformation. This includes digital infrastructure, leadership commitment, finan-
cial capacity, governance structures, and organizational culture [42,44].
Indicative measures include:
• ICT infrastructure investment
• Existence of institutional digital strategies

5. Methodology
5.1. Research Design and Rationale
This study employed a systematic scoping review to examine how digital transforma-
tion in higher education institutions (HEIs) contributes to public value creation. The review
followed the PRISMA-ScR (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Scoping
Reviews) framework to ensure a transparent, replicable, and methodologically rigorous
protocol for identifying, screening, and synthesizing evidence.
Scoping reviews are well suited to emerging, interdisciplinary fields where conceptual
boundaries are fluid and the evidence base is heterogeneous. Research on digital transfor-
mation in higher education spans multiple domains—including educational technology,
public administration, information systems, and governance—which makes a scoping
approach appropriate for capturing disciplinary diversity while enabling conceptual in-
tegration. Unlike prior reviews that address isolated dimensions (e.g., digital literacy or
technology adoption), this study systematically maps evidence across digital competencies,
institutional readiness, governance alignment, and public-value outcomes to inform the
development of the G-DTPVC framework.
5.2. Research Questions
Consistent with scoping review best practices, the review began with exploratory
questions that were iteratively refined during the review process. The final set comprised
one central question and six sub-questions:
Central question: How do digital competencies and institutional readiness shape
public-value outcomes in higher education across different global contexts?
Sub-questions:
1. How are digital transformation, digital competencies, and institutional readiness
conceptualized in the higher education literature?
2. Which public-value dimensions (e.g., equity, transparency, accountability, trust, legiti-
macy) are associated with digital transformation in HEIs?
3. How does digital capital influence human capabilities and institutional readiness?
4. What processes underpin digital transformation in HEIs?
5. How do public-value outcomes vary across regional and income-level contexts?
6. What patterns emerge across governance contexts (for example, Global North versus
Global South)?

7.1. Theoretical Contributions and Advancement of the G-DTPVC Framework
A central contribution of this study lies in the development and empirical ground-
ing of the Global Digital Transformation–Public Value Creation (G-DTPVC) framework,
which integrates technological, organizational, and governance perspectives into a unified
analytical model. While prior studies often treat digital transformation as a technical
or managerial process, this study conceptualizes it as a multi-dimensional and context-
dependent phenomenon, shaped by the interaction of capabilities, institutional conditions,
and governance structures.
The framework builds on and extends established theoretical traditions. The
Technology–Organization–Environment (TOE) framework highlights how organizational
and environmental conditions shape technology adoption [53], while public value theory
emphasizes the alignment of institutional activities with societal outcomes [54,55]. Institu-
tional perspectives further explain how governance norms, regulatory environments, and
cultural contexts influence transformation trajectories.
Importantly, the findings provide empirical validation of relational mechanisms within
the framework:
• Institutional readiness mediates the relationship between digital competencies and
transformation outcomes
• Governance alignment moderates how these outcomes translate into public value
This contributes to the literature by moving beyond linear models of technology
adoption toward a configurational and interaction-based explanation of digital transfor-
mation. It also responds directly to the fragmentation identified in prior research, where
competencies, institutions, and governance are often examined in isolation [3,7].
7.2. Digital Competencies and Organizational Transformation
The findings reinforce that digital competencies are foundational but insufficient
drivers of transformation. Consistent with prior research, competencies extend beyond
technical skills to include pedagogical innovation, data literacy, and managerial capabilities
necessary for integrating digital technologies into teaching and administration [12,14,56,57].
This aligns with broader scholarship emphasizing human capital and organizational
learning as key enablers of technological innovation [58,59]. Continuous professional
development and capacity-building initiatives are therefore critical for fostering a culture
of digital innovation. However, the findings also highlight persistent gaps in critical and
advanced competencies, particularly in areas such as AI governance and digital ethics.
Moreover, resistance to digital transformation is not simply attitudinal but often
reflects structural and institutional constraints, including inadequate training, increased
workload, and tensions with professional identity. This supports emerging arguments that
digital transformation should be understood as an organizational change process, rather
than a purely technological upgrade [2].

Institutional readiness emerges as the most influential determinant of digital transfor-

with the TOE framework, organizational factors—such as infrastructure, leadership, gover-
nance structures, and financial resources—shape the extent to which digital competencies

8. Conclusions
This study set out to systematically examine how digital transformation in higher
education institutions (HEIs) contributes to public value creation, addressing a critical gap
in the literature where technological adoption is often treated independently of broader
societal outcomes. Using a PRISMA-ScR approach, the study synthesized evidence from
47 peer-reviewed studies published between 2013 and 2025, providing a comprehensive
mapping of key drivers, processes, and contextual dynamics shaping digital transformation
and its implications for public value.
The findings demonstrate that digital transformation in HEIs is not merely a techno-
logical or managerial shift, but a complex socio-institutional and governance-embedded
process. Central to this process is the interaction of three interdependent dimensions: digi-
tal competencies, institutional readiness, and governance alignment. Digital competencies
constitute the foundational capabilities required for engaging with digital technologies;
however, their effectiveness depends on institutional readiness, which mediates the transla-
tion of capacity into implementation outcomes. Governance alignment, in turn, determines
whether these outcomes generate meaningful, inclusive, and sustainable public value. This
multi-dimensional and relational perspective advances existing scholarship by moving
beyond fragmented approaches that treat these elements in isolation [1,3,14].
A major contribution of this study is the development and refinement of the Global
Digital Transformation–Public Value Creation (G-DTPVC) framework, which offers an
integrated and empirically grounded model for understanding digital transformation in

higher education. By specifying key constructs, relational pathways, and context-sensitive
dynamics, the framework bridges technological, organizational, and governance perspec-
tives within a unified analytical structure. It also provides a foundation for future empirical
testing, comparative research, and policy-oriented analysis across diverse institutional and
regional contexts.
From a theoretical standpoint, the study contributes to ongoing debates by demon-
strating that digital transformation must be understood as a configurational and context-
dependent phenomenon, shaped by institutional capacities, governance systems, and
socio-political environments. It also extends public value theory by incorporating digital
transformation dynamics, highlighting how value creation is contingent on alignment
between technological innovation and societal objectives [7,61].
From a policy perspective, the findings underscore that effective digital transformation
requires holistic and multi-level strategies that go beyond infrastructure investments.
While digital transformation can enhance access to education, improve transparency, and
strengthen institutional responsiveness, it also introduces significant risks, including digital
inequality, data governance challenges, algorithmic bias, and emerging forms of data
dependency. Addressing these challenges requires coordinated efforts across institutional,
national, and global levels, with particular emphasis on:
• Capacity building and digital skills development
• Strengthening institutional readiness and leadership
• Establishing robust governance and regulatory frameworks
• Promoting inclusive and equitable access to digital education
Such integrated approaches are essential for ensuring that digital transformation
contributes to sustainable and socially responsive higher education systems [2,4,13].
The study also situates digital transformation within broader global development
agendas, particularly its alignment with Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) 4 (quality
education), 9 (industry, innovation, and infrastructure), and 16 (peace, justice, and strong
institutions). By emphasizing governance, inclusivity, and institutional capacity, the study
contributes to ongoing discussions on the role of higher education in advancing equitable
and sustainable development pathways [12,60].
Importantly, the findings highlight persistent global inequalities and contextual dis-
parities, with the literature heavily skewed toward high-income settings. This underscores
the need for more context-sensitive research, particularly in the Global South, where infras-
tructural constraints, governance challenges, and resource limitations significantly shape
digital transformation trajectories.
Finally, the study identifies key directions for future research. There is a need for:
• Empirical validation of the G-DTPVC framework using quantitative and compara-
tive methods
• Longitudinal studies to capture the dynamic evolution of digital transformation and
public value outcomes
• Greater focus on Global South contexts and underrepresented regions
• Deeper investigation into governance, ethics, and power dynamics, particularly in
relation to AI and data-driven education systems.
A limitation of this review is the inclusion of a small number of studies outside the
higher education sector. Although these were selected based on their conceptual relevance
to digital governance and public value, their contextual differences may influence the
transferability of findings. However, their inclusion strengthens the analytical depth of
the review by capturing cross-sectoral insights that are increasingly relevant in digitally
transforming higher education systems.

provides a robust foundation for guiding both future research and practical interventions
