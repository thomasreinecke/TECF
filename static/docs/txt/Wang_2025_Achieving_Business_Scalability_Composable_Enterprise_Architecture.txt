II. ENTERPRISE ARCHITECTURE IN HISTORICAL
CONTEXT: TRACING THE SHIFT FROM MONOLITHIC
TO COMPOSABLE FRAMEWORKS
A. MONOLITHIC VS. MICROSERVICES ARCHITECTURE:
TRANSITIONING TO AGILE DECENTRALIZATION
In a traditional Monolithic Architecture, the entire sys-
tem’s functionalities are centralized within a single codebase,
making development and testing more straightforward for
smaller-scale systems. However, as the system grows and
becomes more complex, this centralized design reveals sig-
nificant limitations. Each update requires redeploying the
entire application, which increases the risk of deployment
issues and reduces flexibility, thereby complicating efforts to
meet the demands for rapid iteration and high availability.
MicroservicesArchitectureovercomesthesechallengesby
decomposing a monolithic application into smaller, indepen-
dent services, each focused on a specific business function.
These services are organized around distinct business capa-
bilitiesandcommunicatethroughlightweightprotocols,such
as HTTP APIs, to minimize dependencies. This approach
allowsdevelopmentteamstoindependentlydevelop,test,and
deploy each service, improving the system’s adaptability and
enabling faster responses to changing business requirements.
While Microservices Architecture offers significant bene-
fits in terms of maintainability and scalability, the transition
from a Monolithic Architecture presents several challenges.
These challenges include restructuring databases, deter-
mining the appropriate level of service granularity, and
ensuring stable communication between services. To achieve
a successful migration, it is crucial to adopt an effective
decomposition strategy, such as segmenting services based
on business functions or utilizing automated tools to support
the transition [4], [5], [6].
Even after migrating to a Microservices Architecture,
organizations may still face challenges related to flexibility
and data management. Although the decentralized nature
of microservices improves system modularity and facilitates
independent deployments, it also introduces complexities in
areas such as data isolation and service coordination, mak-
ing it difficult to maintain data consistency and manage
maintenance costs. Additionally, excessive fragmentation of
services can lead to increased management overhead and
communication expenses, potentially affecting development
efficiency. To address these issues, newer approaches like
Miniservices and Composable Enterprise Architecture have
emerged,aimingtoovercomethelimitationsofmicroservices
while further enhancing system modularity and business
agility.

III. LITERATURE REVIEW
A. BUSINESS AGILITY AND DIGITAL TRANSFORMATION
Business agility is essential for enterprise success, compris-
ing both operational agility and strategic agility. Operational
agility focuses on intensifying the emphasis on customer
value within existing operations, while strategic agility
involves fostering the creation of novel services to attract
new clientele. However, achieving business agility requires
coordination across diverse departments, with information
technology playing a crucial role in empowering enterprises
to adapt to changes with greater flexibility [9], [10].
Digital transformation provides enterprises with flexible
and innovative solutions to address market changes and
challenges, thereby enhancing their competitiveness. This
transformation drives enterprises to adopt a more modular
approach to system construction internally, supporting agile
development of various business components. By reassem-
bling these components, enterprises can quickly respond to
market opportunities and adapt to potential threats. This
approach eliminates barriers between business and IT depart-
ments, enabling enterprises to meet customer needs more
effectively [11].
B. COMPOSABLE ENTERPRISE ARCHITECTURE
The concept of the ‘‘Composable Enterprise,’’ introduced by
Gartner, refers to an organizational model designed to facili-
tate innovation and agility in response to evolving business
requirements. In simpler terms, a Composable Enterprise
swiftly adjusts its operations and offerings to meet dynamic
market demands by integrating and leveraging pre-packaged
business functionalities. This approach fosters resilience
and adaptability, allowing companies to remain competitive
amidst changing landscapes [12].

VI. METHODOLOGY
To evaluate the effectiveness and applicability of the pro-
posed CEA framework, this study employed a qualitative
casestudydesignsupplementedbysystem-levelperformance
metrics.Themethodologywasdevelopedtoexplorehowcore
architectural features—PBC modularization, cloud-native
orchestration, and micro-frontend encapsulation—translate
into tangible benefits and challenges in a real-world enter-
prise context.
A. INTERVIEW DESIGN AND SAMPLING STRATEGY
This study conducted semi-structured interviews with 12 key
stakeholders who participated in the planning, develop-
ment, and deployment of CEA-based systems within a large
telecommunications enterprise. Participants were selected
using purposive sampling to ensure relevant domain exper-
tise, and included system architects, DevOps engineers,
software developers, and service managers. The interview
protocol focused on participants’ experiences with modular
development, deployment workflows, integration challenges,
and perceived benefits of CEA adoption. Each interview
lasted approximately 45 to 60 minutes and was conducted in
a hybrid format (in-person and virtual), with consented audio
recordings for analysis.
B. QUALITATIVE CODING AND NVIVO ANALYSIS
The interview data, originally collected in Traditional Chi-
nese, were transcribed and analyzed using NVivo. A two-
phase coding process was employed. First, open coding
was conducted to identify recurring concepts and obser-
vations. This was followed by axial coding to organize
related codes into higher-level categories aligned with the
research objectives. Emergent themes included improved
deployment independence, modular reuse, integration com-
plexity, and team coordination. To enhance coding reliability,
the research team performed cross-validation through inde-
pendent coding and resolved discrepancies via collaborative
discussions.

VII. RESULTS
This study conducted semi-structured interviews with 12 key
stakeholders and utilized NVivo for comprehensive analysis.
The findings indicate that the development team recognizes
several advantages of CEA, including reusability, inde-
pendence, and modular design. Specifically, the design of
PBCs facilitates the modularization of business functions,
which reduces coupling between components. This enhance-
ment improves system flexibility and reusability, allowing
individual business teams to develop and deploy solutions
independently. Furthermore, the assembly characteristics of
CEA enable a quicker response to evolving business needs.
However, the development team also identified several chal-
lenges, including a steep learning curve that necessitates
developers to possess a diverse skill set and substantial
experience to address these demands. Additionally, shared
functionalities among PBCs may lead to increased mainte-
nance costs and associated risks.
Quantitative data obtained from system operation records
indicate that the transition from a monolithic architecture
to CEA has resulted in significant improvements in key
performance indicators. The average system downtime has
decreased from 10 hours per month to 4.5 hours, representing
a reduction of approximately 55%. This reduction signifies a
substantial enhancement in both system stability and avail-
ability. Moreover, the frequency of service deployments has
increased from quarterly to monthly. The time required to
develop and launch new features has been reduced from an
average of 3 months to 1.2 months, indicating a decrease of
about 60%. These advancements underscore the flexibility of
CEA, enabling organizations to implement system updates
and introduce new features more frequently. This capability
allows for rapid adaptation to market demands and signifi-
cantly enhances overall business agility.
VIII. RESEARCH INSIGHTS AND FUTURE DIRECTIONS
A. ADVANTAGES OF CEA
Traditional monolithic architecture struggles to respond
swiftly to changing demands due to its high coupling, which
complicates updates and deployments. While microservices
and miniservices architectures enhance modularity, they still
face challenges related to module collaboration and data
consistency management [4], [5], [6], [7], [8]. In contrast, the
application of IVA-PBC highlights the significant advantages
of CEA. By employing loosely coupled, highly cohesive
module designs and refined module partitioning, IVA-PBC
effectively reduces system coupling, allowing business com-
ponents to be developed, tested, and deployed independently,
therebyfacilitatingrapidresponsestoevolvingdemands[14].
Furthermore,thedesignofCEApromotestheflexibleassem-
bly of business components, which is essential for adapting
to rapidly changing market trends. Standardized interfaces
enable organizations to quickly integrate different PBCs,
thereby enhancing system scalability, improving collabo-
ration efficiency among modules, and increasing overall
organizational adaptability and competitiveness [13].
B. APPLICABILITY ACROSS ENTERPRISE SCALES
The adoption and effectiveness of CEA vary significantly
with organizational scale. In large enterprises, CEA is typ-
ically implemented by assigning PBCs to cross-functional
teams, thereby enabling modular governance across busi-
ness domains. Each team oversees the full lifecycle of its
designated PBC—including design, development, deploy-
ment, and maintenance—facilitating component reuse and
promoting horizontal innovation. This organizational model
enhances collaboration across departments and supports
operational efficiency. Given the complexity of legacy sys-
tems and multi-layered structures, large enterprises often
adopt a phased integration strategy to reduce disruption and
ensure alignment with existing governance frameworks and
IT roadmaps [18].
In addition, Ross and Beath [27] highlight that effec-
tive enterprise architecture entails decomposing key business
outcomes—such as products, services, or processes—into
discrete components. Each component is managed by a
dedicated team that holds responsibility for the underlying
people, processes, and technologies. These principles are
well aligned with the modular and team-centric nature of
CEA, empowering organizations to enhance adaptability and
scalability while maintaining operational resilience.
In contrast, small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs)
tend to possess greater organizational flexibility but face
constraints in technical capacity and workforce availability.
For such organizations, CEA offers a practical pathway to
transformation through incremental modularization. Rather
thanoverhaulingentiresystems,SMEscanbeginbyapplying
CEA to specific business functions or product lines, then
scale gradually based on growth and resource availability.
This progressive approach allows SMEs to balance cost effi-
ciency with faster time-to-market [28].
In summary, the successful implementation of CEA
depends on aligning the adoption strategy with the spe-
cific characteristics of the organization—such as its scale,
technical capabilities, and resource availability. By carefully
adapting the modular design and deployment scope to these
factors, both large enterprises and SMEs can fully leverage
the flexibility, agility, and scalability that CEA offers.

CEA
In the implementation of CEA, organizations should priori-
tizetheintegrationoftechnologicalinnovation[14],andalign
it with their strategic planning processes to support digital
transformation [30]. Specifically, they should explore emerg-
ing technologies such as AI, cloud computing, blockchain,
and IoT to enhance their capabilities in digital transfor-
mation and business innovation. Additionally, fostering a
culture of open innovation is essential, as it encourages
cross-departmental collaboration and ensures that techno-
logical advancements are quickly translated into practical
applications, thereby improving the quality of products and
services [31]. By adopting these strategies, organizations can
maintain a competitive edge in a rapidly evolving market and
promote sustainable business growth and development.
E. LIMITATIONS
While CEA provides numerous benefits, this study also iden-
tifies several limitations that may affect its adoption and
long-term effectiveness.
First, the modular design of CEA requires that devel-
opers and architects possess advanced system thinking and
integration skills, potentially resulting in a steeper learning
curve. This may pose challenges for organizations with lim-
ited technical resources or experience in distributed design.
As emphasized in the HI-TOP model [30], successful adop-
tion of advanced architectural paradigms demands not only
technical readiness, but also organizational alignment and
change management support to facilitate smooth transition
and sustainable operation.
Second, our empirical findings reveal concrete organiza-
tional and coordination challenges during practical deploy-
ment. For instance, one participant described how the lack
of a centralized API registry led to redundant logic being
independently implemented by multiple teams, resulting in
conflicting behaviors and integration delays. Other intervie-
wees cited difficulties in aligning module release schedules
and interface expectations. As more PBCs are introduced and
managed across distributed teams, maintaining architectural
coherence and operational alignment becomes increasingly
complex [27]. Addressing these issues requires the imple-
mentation of structured governance frameworks, shared
designstandards,andongoingintegrationcheckpointstosus-
tain system consistency under evolving conditions.
Third, while modularity enhances operational flexibil-
ity, it also broadens the system’s attack surface, potentially
introducing new security vulnerabilities. Securing compos-
able architectures requires a combination of architectural
and runtime controls. For instance, service meshes enforce
mutual TLS and apply policy-based routing across modules,
whereas API gateways support centralized authentication,
access control, and request validation. System-wide logging
andanomalydetectionfurtherimproveobservabilityandhelp
identify misconfigurations or malicious behaviors. These
practices are essential for maintaining trust, particularly in
dynamic, multi-domain environments.
Lastly,thisstudyisboundedbyitsscopeandthecharacter-
istics of the organizations examined. Although the proposed
architecture demonstrated practical value in the studied
context, further validation is needed in larger and more het-
erogeneous environments. Future work should also explore
long-termmaintainability,costimplications,andcross-sector
applicability of CEA frameworks.
F. FUTURE WORK
Future research will leverage AI to support organizational
decision-making and enhance adaptability through predictive
data analysis, as emphasized in prior research on adaptive
business architecture [24]. In addition, we aim to optimize
the assembly of PBCs using AI and develop automated inte-
gration methods. This aligns with emerging perspectives on
how algorithmic coordination and intelligent systems can
reshapeworkprocessesandenhanceagilitywithindistributed
organizations [29]. We will also explore the deployment of
CEA across various industries to broaden the applicability
and impact of our findings.
Specifically, future work will examine how machine learn-
ing can identify workload patterns and predict component
scaling needs based on real-time operational data. Clus-
tering techniques may assist in classifying usage contexts,
facilitating more effective reuse or replacement of PBCs.
Additionally, AI-assisted interface matching and automated
schema validation may accelerate the integration of newly
introduced modules with existing systems. These directions
aim to further realize the vision of intelligent, self-adaptive
enterprise architectures that evolve in response to both
internal organizational goals and external environmental
changes.
IX. CONCLUSION
This study underscores the transformative impact of CEA in
enhancing agility within enterprise systems. By integrating
PBCs into a cloud-native, modular framework, CEA enables
scalableandflexibleoperationsthataddressthelimitationsof
traditional architectures. The results demonstrate that CEA
improves operational efficiency, minimizes downtime, and
accelerates deployment cycles, allowing organizations to
respond swiftly to dynamic market demands.
CEA’s modular design strategically aligns IT capabilities
with business objectives, creating an adaptable architec-
ture that supports scalable solutions and fosters continuous
innovation with minimal disruption. Although implement-
ing CEA requires a learning curve and skill development,
it offers a resilient foundation for digital transformation.
