ABSTRACT This study presents the development of an adaptive enterprise architecture (EA) model
designed to enhance strategic alignment in large, complex organizations. Developed through design science
research in the finance industry, the model integrates business capability maps (BCMs), value streams, and
IT applications into a unified business–IT view that evolves through continuous use. The accompanying
developmentmethodprovidesapragmatic,stepwiseapproachforconstructingandrefiningthemodelinreal
organizational settings. Building on the proposed model and its development method, the study contributes
by: 1) demystifying the creation of a practical, value-adding, and detailed BCM; 2) extending the capability-
centric view of EA with value streams to enable a holistic understanding of enterprise value delivery; and 3)
integratingbusinessartifactswiththeITsystemsthatrealizethem.Together,thesecontributionsdemonstrate
how an adaptive EA model can evolve through practical application by continuously delivering value while
fostering ongoing business–IT alignment and organizational sensemaking in complex environments.

nizational sensemaking, EA models.

B. BUSINESS CAPABILITY MAPS
Business capabilities are the abilities or capacities embed-
ded within people, processes, technology, and information
that an enterprise leverages to achieve its goals [23], [24].
According to Ulrich and Rosen [25], business capabilities
are technology-agnostic and describe what an enterprise
does, without detailing how, where, or why. Grounded in
the ‘‘theory of the firm’’ [26], an organization can be
understood as a coherent system of interrelated capabilities.
A BCM expresses this system as a structured, hierarchi-
cal representation of enterprise business capabilities [9],
[27]. It provides a common language and reference model
for understanding what the enterprise does, facilitating
communication and alignment between business and IT
stakeholders. Business-capability mapping is the systematic
process of identifying, organizing, and representing these
capabilities in a BCM to create a stable, technology-agnostic
view of the enterprise, serving as a foundation for SA and
informeddecision-making[9],[10],[17],[25]).Leonard[28]
classifies capabilities within a BCM into three categories.
Core capabilities differentiate a company in the market
and constitute its primary source of competitive advantage;
they are difficult to imitate and require long-term develop-
ment. Supplemental capabilities support and enhance core
capabilities—such as sales or marketing—while being more
easily replicable. Enabling capabilities are essential for the
company’s daily operations (e.g., accounting or legal) but do
not directly contribute to competitive differentiation.
Despite their potential value for managing complexity and
fostering SA [29], BCMs remain inconsistently addressed
across academic and professional literature. Van Riel and
Poels [9] identified a significant methodological gap in
business-capability mapping, noting that many organizations
rely on proprietary consultancy methods—limiting trans-
parency and internal capability-building. Similarly, Kotusev
andAlwadain[8]observethatpractitionerliteratureislargely

C. VALUE STREAMS
While the BCM defines what an enterprise must be able to
do, it does not show how value is delivered to customers. The
concept of value streams was first introduced in the context
of lean production by Womack and Jones [37] as the flow of
value-addingandnon-value-addingstepsinvolvedincreating
and delivering products or services. In business architecture,
the notion stems from Martin [38], who described value
streams as E2E activities delivering value to internal or exter-
nal customers. Over time, the notion of ‘‘value’’ has evolved
beyond mere product or service delivery to encompass a
broader sense of utility, desirability, and significance, reflect-
ing both monetary and non-monetary dimensions [39], [40].
Ultimately, however, the delivery of value to customers and
the generation of sustainable financial returns for investors
remain the fundamental purpose of any enterprise [41].
Value streams capture the high-level activities through which
this value is created and delivered, allowing organizations
to define and optimize these E2E flows [10], [25], [39].
In business architecture, Value-stream mapping (VSM) is the
systematic process of identifying, structuring, and represent-
ing value streams by linking value-creating activities to the
capabilities that enable them and analyzing how value flows
acrosstheenterprise.AsWhelanandMeaden[42]note,value
streams offer a strong foundation for validating capabilities
and ensuring they fit together coherently. Importantly, value
streams differ from business processes in that they depict
how value is delivered at a high conceptual level, whereas
business processes describe in detail how work is executed at
the operational level.
Business-architecture value streams should not be con-
fused with related concepts such as value chains, value
networks, or lean value streams. Porter’s [43] value chain
focuses on economic value creation, Allee´s [44] value
networks emphasize actor relationships, and lean value
streams [37] target waste reduction. In contrast, business-
architecturevaluestreamscombinewithbusinesscapabilities
to provide a holistic, strategic view of enterprise E2E value
delivery [10], [39].
Despite the conceptual maturity of value streams, empiri-
cal and methodological research within business architecture
remains limited. A focused literature review was conducted
using the same methodology as in the previous section,
extending the approach of Van Riel and Poels [9]. The term
‘‘value stream’’ was used as the primary search keyword
across Web of Science, Google Scholar, and grey-literature
sources. The search was iterative and complemented by
backward and forward snowballing to identify additional
studies addressing related concepts. The collected sources
were analyzed to assess the extent to which they provide
practical guidance for business-architecture value-stream
mapping and whether they address integration with busi-
ness capabilities or decomposition into IT systems. The
search concluded in June 2025. Most of the identified stud-
ies focused on lean value-stream applications or operational
efficiency rather than on business-architecture value streams.
The remaining literature offered very limited formal guid-
ance, found mainly in frameworks such as TOGAF [10], the
BIZBOK®Guide [33], and Ulrich & Rosen [25]. Notably,
value streams were incorporated for the first time in the
TOGAF® Standard, in its 10th Edition, which largely repro-
duced content from [39]—illustrating the scarcity of original,
detailed guidance on the topic. More recent sources, such as
Ardoq [45] and Pratap and Pant [46], emphasize the need
to align value streams with BCMs, yet no academically or
practitioner-endorsed methodology exists for value-stream
mapping within a business–IT EA model. Table 2 sum-
marizes the most relevant literature addressing the creation

vide a formalized development framework integrating value
streams, BCMs, and IT applications as part of a business–IT
EA model supporting SA in complex organizations. Build-

D. EA MODELS

modelling that supports enterprise-wide planning rather than
the design of individual systems. Because it bridges busi-

comprehensible, and communicable to business stakehold-
ers [8]. To maintain business–IT alignment in dynamic
environments,EAmodelsshouldevolvefromstaticrepresen-

to traditional model-driven engineering, which assumes that
models remain valid after implementation, Hinkelmann et al.
[53] introduced two complementary paradigms—continuous
adaptation and continuous business–IT alignment—wherein

EA model, represented by the EKG, containing concrete,
instance-level data about the enterprise and serving as a
model of the enterprise at Level 0; and at Level 0 lies the
enterprise itself, i.e., the enterprise architecture. By linking
enterprise data to the enterprise ontology, the EKG enables
machine interpretation and analysis [55], serving as an intel-
ligent, integrated repository of EA knowledge accessible to
both architects and broader stakeholders. Although not the
primary focus of this study, the EKG represents an essential
foundation for enabling adaptive and value-driven EA in
complex organizations.
III. METHODOLOGY
The research described in the paper follows the design sci-
ence research methodology [56]. In design-based research,
knowledge on how to build artifacts (such as conceptual
frameworks, models, and methods) emerges through an
iterative process of applying design theories to develop
solutions for problems that were previously unsolved. The
accumulated prescriptive knowledge is the outcome of this
iterativecycleofinvestigation,design,testing,reflection,and
learning—identifying what worked, what didn’t, and how the
solution can be generalized to address a broader class of
similar problems.
The study was carried out in a company which functions
as an acquirer—a financial institution authorized to pro-
cess credit and debit card transactions for merchants. The
company is an example of a large, complex organization,
employing over 10,000 people, with a decades-long history
of M&A that has significantly contributed to its current
business–IT complexity.
The study was conducted in two distinct phases. The first
phase involved developing the EA model, with the business
layer defined by the BCM and the IT layer integrating it
with relevant IT applications. The second phase extended
the model by incorporating value streams, enabling an E2E
representation of value delivery and its alignment with busi-
ness capabilities and supporting IT systems. To support the
creation and representation of model artifacts, the ArchiMate
modelling language [52] was used. ArchiMate offers a stan-
dardizedandwidelyadoptednotationfordescribingbusiness,
application, and technology architectures. The open-source
tool Archi served as the modelling environment and as the
repository for EA artifacts developed in this study.
To guide the development of a detailed BCM capa-
ble of supporting SA in complex organizations, this study
aimed to demystify the modelling process by establish-
ing a set of proven, practically applicable principles for
other practitioners. The process began by examining guid-
ing principles identified in the literature and refining them
through practical application to ensure their validity in real
organizational contexts. Following Keller’s [17] recommen-
dations, existing BCM materials within the company—such
as high-level consultancy models and internal business plan-
ning documents—were used as a starting point. To structure
the model, Leonard’s [28] capability grouping was applied

A. MODEL DEVELOPMENT GUIDANCE
1) BCM DEVELOPMENT GUIDANCE
Developing a detailed BCM that could serve as a shared ref-
erence across business and IT functions proved challenging
giventheenterprise’sscaleandcomplexity.Apragmaticgood
enough approach was adopted, enabling the model to evolve
through practical use rather than pursuing early complete-
ness. This adaptive strategy, grounded in Keller’s [17] and
Weick’s[2]principles,provedmoreeffectivethanformalized
mapping methods, such as the one proposed by Van Riel
and Poels [9], which assume rapid convergence. From this
iterative development, a set of guiding principles for business
capability mapping emerged, combining insights from liter-
ature with lessons learned in practice. They were validated
throughstakeholderengagementandreflect thepracticesthat
proved most effective in real-world settings:
1. BCM is business-focused. As the BCM provides enter-
prise insights and a shared business–IT reference,
it should be developed collaboratively with business
experts to ensure alignment with business think-
ing [30], [32]. IT reference models are not BCMs [9].
2. Start with a BCM skeleton. Use the generic template
(Figure 1) and adapt it to reflect organizational rele-
vance and language.
3. Leverage existing versions. Build on existing BCMs,
internal categorizations, or industry frameworks
instead of starting from scratch [17].
4. Apply a pragmatic, value-focused approach. Model
first the core 20% of capabilities delivering most busi-
ness value and evolve iteratively—good enough and
fast, not exhaustive [17].
5. Test and refine through use. Continuously validate and
enrich the BCM through real initiatives with domain
experts [18], [31].
6. Use established terminology. Apply company or
industry-standard terms to ensure the BCM serves as
a shared organizational language [9], [25].
7. Prioritize pragmatism over taxonomy. Emphasize
strategically important capabilities even if this breaks
strict hierarchy [17]. For example, 41.Indirect Sales
appears as a separate capability next to 9.Sales
(Figure 6) because it plays a strategically significant
role in the company.
8. Learn as you go. Treat BCM building as an exploratory
process that reveals ‘‘tribal’’ organizational knowl-
edge [30]
9. BCM is hierarchical but also an ontology. Maintain
hierarchical clarity but use ontological links (e.g.,
serves, associates to) when capabilities have multiple
relationships [52].
10. Keep it organization-agnostic. Design capabilities
to remain stable despite structural or organizational
changes [31].
11. Ensure uniqueness. Each capability must represent a
distinct building block, even if used across multiple
parents [25], [29].
12. Name capabilities as nouns. Express what the business
does (e.g., Partner Management), not how [9], [10],
[52].
13. Use numbering for structure. Hierarchical numbering
clarifies relationships and aids navigation in capability
repositories [8].
14. Assign capability identifiers. Use stable capability
codes to support reuse and synchronization across
teams and initiatives.
15. Validatewithvaluestreams.Confirmthateachcapabil-
ity supports at least one value-stream step; otherwise,
exclude it [42].

V. CONCLUSION
This paper demonstrates why large, complex organizations
should embrace their inherent complexity and pursue a
holistic understanding of their business as a foundation for
effective management. To support this, it presents a practical,
holistic business–IT model and its accompanying develop-
ment method, designed to enable SA in large, complex
enterprises. Developed through an adaptive probe–sense–
respond approach, the model is continuously refined and
validated through real business initiatives to ensure ongoing
relevance.
Together, the model and its method constitute the paper’s
scientific contribution by:
1. Demystifying the development of a practical, value-
adding BCM that extends beyond conceptual abstrac-
tions.
2. Extending the capability-centric view of EA with value
streams to enable holistic enterprise understanding.
3. Integrating business architecture artifacts with the IT
applications realizing them.
The study illustrates how EA can evolve along-
side the enterprise, supporting holistic sensemaking
and tangible stakeholder value rather than enforcing
rigid processes detached from reality. It also demon-
strates how EA—traditionally associated with IT—can
take a leading role in developing and maintaining the
BCM, aligning more closely with business architecture
and acting as a strategic bridge between business and
IT.
Aswithanyresearch,thisstudyhaslimitations.Conducted
as a long-term design science study within a single finan-
cial enterprise, it provides deep, practice-grounded insight
with unique access to the internal realities of a large,
complex organization, yet remains inherently bounded by
its context. This limitation was mitigated by abstracting
the approach, metamodel, templates, and guiding principles
to enhance their transferability. Future research will focus
on further developing the BASE framework by extending
the model’s application—for instance, to portfolio manage-
ment and through continued exploration of the Composable
Enterprise approach.
