ABSTRACT
This study investigates how organizational resources and capabilities interact during digital
transformation (DT) across diverse industries. We conceptualize DT as a form of organization-
wide change in which digital technologies act as catalysts for reconfiguring value creation and
reshaping organizational identity, in contrast to IT-enabled improvements that largely sustain
existing value propositions. Drawing on qualitative analysis of 30 interviews spanning 13 industries
in Singapore, we propose the REACT framework, consisting of Resources, Enabling Factors,
Adaptive Capabilities, Capability Cultivation, and Transformation Outcomes. Our findings indicate
that enabling factors, including leadership commitment, cultural readiness, and external shocks
like COVID-19, function as triggers, shifting organizations from resource exploitation toward
capability reconfiguration amid heightened uncertainty. This transition produces enduring ten­
sions that require constant balancing of stability with change and efficiency with adaptability,
demanding surge capacity to manage unpredictable disruptions.

Introduction
Digital transformation (DT) has evolved from episo­
dic strategic initiatives to a persistent organizational
condition that reshapes how firms compete and
engage with stakeholders across industries.1–3
Since
the mid-2010s, scholarly interest in DT has expanded
rapidly, accompanied by sustained debate over its
definition and boundaries.2,4
Rather than viewing
this ambiguity as a theoretical weakness, our study
treats it as a productive space for clarifying the con­
ceptual foundations of transformation, thereby posi­
tioning DT as a distinctive organizational
phenomenon.
We conceptualize DT as organization-wide change in
which digital technologies act as catalysts for reconfi­
guring value creation and capture, ultimately reshaping
organizational identity over time. This framing distin­
guishes DT from implementation as a result of IT-
enabled organizational transformation (ITOT), which
typically enhances existing processes while preserving
core value propositions and identities.5
Although the
boundary between DT and ITOT is not ontological, it
serves as an important analytical distinction. Many
initiatives begin as incremental digitization effort and
evolve into broader transformation as value architec­
tures shift. Maintaining this distinction helps avoid con­
flating routine system upgrades with transformational
change.
Empirical research supports this differentiation.
Wessel et al.5
show that DT is marked by the redefinition
of value propositions and the emergence of new organi­
zational identities. This view aligns with the integrative
reviews by Hanelt et al.,1
whereby DT is conceptualized
as triggered and shaped by pervasive digital technologies.
Their synthesis identifies four analytical perspectives,
ranging from localized technological impacts to holistic
co-evolution within digital ecosystems. Transformation
often unfolds through adaptive, ecosystem-embedded
processes rather than discrete restructuring events,
explaining why the boundary between DT and ITOT
often appears blurred in practice.
Complementary insights emerge from consumer and
marketing research. Paul et al.4
organize DT around
interconnected elements, including value propositions,
value networks, digital channels, and organizational
agility. Their framework traces progression from digiti­
zation to digitalization and, in fewer cases, to full trans­
formation. This perspective highlights customer-facing
reconfigurations and network realignments as key indi­
cators of transformation beyond ITOT. At the same
time, the review identifies persistent barriers related to
access, skills, trust, and governance. This suggests that
DT outcomes remain uneven across sectors and
populations.

of DT. The COVID-19 pandemic accelerated digital

Beyond the pan­
demic, firms now operate amid disruptions driven by
geopolitical tensions, supply chain instability, and reg­
ulatory change, further constraining transformation
timelines and resources.7
This context reveals a core
paradox of DT: environmental volatility necessitates
rapid capability reconfiguration, yet the same turbu­
lence limits organizations’ capacity to respond effec­
tively. The experience of low-cost carriers illustrates
this tension. Their ability to sustain sub-30-minute
turnaround time reflects cultivated surge capacity rather
than operational efficiency alone. Such capacity is built
during periods of relative stability and enables organiza­
tions to absorb shocks without compromising service
delivery.6,8
Despite growing recognition of DT’s contextual nat­
ure, many initiatives remain guided by assumptions of
uniform applicability.9,10
Sectoral characteristics, regu­
latory regimes, and legacy infrastructures shape distinct
transformation pathways, complicating the transfer of
best practices across contexts.11,12
These differences
suggest that DT cannot be fully understood through
standardized models alone.
Theoretical explanations of DT have largely drawn
on the Resource-Based View (RBV) and Dynamic
Capabilities Theory (DCT). RBV explains competitive
advantage through resources that are valuable, rare,
difficult to imitate, and non-substitutable.13,14
While
foundational, RBV’s relatively static orientation limits
its explanatory power in digital contexts, where
resources depreciate quickly and possession alone rarely
translates into sustained advantage without continual
reconfiguration.15,16
DCT addresses this limitation by emphasizing firms’
abilities to sense opportunities, seize them, and trans­
form existing resource bases.15
DCT, however, often
underplays the resource foundations that make such
capability development possible. As a result, organiza­
tions tussle with contradictions between stability and
change, efficiency and adaptability, and standardization
and flexibility.12,17
Disruptions such as the COVID-19
pandemic highlight the role of surge capacity—the abil­
ity to swiftly mobilize, redeploy, and scale resources
under severe time and operational constraints. In these
contexts, dynamic capabilities extend beyond gradual
adaptation to encompass compressed decision cycles,
rapid resource reconfiguration, and operational resili­
ence in response to abrupt demand and supply shocks.
These tensions help explain why firms with similar
resource endowments experience markedly different
transformational trajectories. Against this backdrop,
our study integrates RBV and DCT to examine how
organizations manage these contradictions in practice.
We henceforth pose the following research question:
How do organizational resources and capabilities inter­
act to facilitate digital transformation across different
industries?
To address this question, we conducted 30 semi-
structured interviews across 13 industries in Singapore
and analyzedthem using interpretive phenomenological
analysis. The analysis yielded 15 themes that informed
the REACT (i.e., Resources, Enabling Factors, Adaptive
Capabilities, Capability Cultivation, and
Transformation Outcomes) framework. REACT does
not propose a linear causal model. Instead, it illustrates
how enabling conditions such as leadership commit­
ment, cultural readiness, and external pressures shape
how organizations balance resource exploitation with
capability reconfiguration. By bridging RBV and DCT
within sector-specific realities, REACT offers
a grounded lens for understanding DT as an ongoing
process of tension management rather than goal attain­
ment. It advances theory by clarifying how resources
and capabilities co-evolve and offers practical guidance
for leaders navigating the persistent contradictions that
define DT.
The following section reviews the literature under­
pinning this integration of RBV and DCT and situates
our contribution within recent DT scholarship. We then
outline our methodological approach and present our
findings, followed by a discussion of theoretical and
practical implications as well as directions for future
research.

Resource-based view and digital transformation
When applied to DT, the RBV provides a robust theo­
retical framework to help understand how firms achieve
and maintain competitive advantage through their
VRIN (i.e., valuable, rare, inimitable, and non-
substitutable) resources.13,14,36
The role of human capi­
tal in DT has also emerged as a critical factor in the RBV
framework. Nadkarni and Prügl37
identified several
challenges managers may face in balancing the explora­
tion and exploitation of resources during DT. Recent
research has highlighted the importance of resource
orchestration in DT, demonstrating that insufficient
attention to environmental uncertainty and inefficient
resource orchestration can lead to suboptimal digital
maturity.38
However, the traditional RBV perspective
assumes that valuable resources alone can drive trans­
formation success, potentially overlooking the complex
dynamics in rapidly evolving digital landscapes.2,13,15,39
Resource-based view and dynamic capabilities as
frameworks for digital transformation
In digital contexts, data generated through production
and markets has emerged as a crucial strategic resource,
while human capital, particularly digital competencies,
also enables effective technological deployment.37,40
Resource possession alone rarely guarantees transfor­
mation success; effective resource orchestration deter­
mines whether digital initiatives yield competitive
advantage.38
Traditional RBV assumptions about stable
resource value can be problematic when technological
change rapidly shifts definitions of valuable resources
and when environmental uncertainty demands contin­
uous resource reconfiguration.2,15
This gap motivates
integrating RBV with DCT to explain how organiza­
tions transition from resource possession to capability-
based transformation.
Data and methodology
Rationale for a cross-sector approach
Cross-sector research design was chosen to align with
our theoretical positioning rather than to enable
detailed industry-level comparisons. Our conceptualiza­
tion of DT as organization-wide change, in which digital
technologies catalyze shifts in value creation and orga­
nizational identity, requires examining whether such
transformation processes extend beyond sector-specific
conditions. A cross-sector approach allows us to assess
whether these core dynamics may recur across diverse
organizational contexts.
This design serves three purposes. First, it enables
identification of core patterns in the shift from resource
exploitation to capability reconfiguration. Second, it
helps distinguish foundational mechanisms from sec­
tor-specific adaptations. Third, it allows us to examine
whether the RBV—DCT tension is managed through
similar enabling mechanisms across regulatory and
institutional settings. Rather than producing sector-
specific models, the aim is to develop a foundational
framework that captures the essential dynamics of DT
across contexts.
The theoretical distinction between DT and ITOT
rests on how digital technologies are mobilized. DT
involves redefining value propositions and reshaping
organizational identity, whereas ITOT focuses on opti­
mizing existing processes while preserving established
value structures.4,5,41
This distinction implies that fun­
damental transformation mechanisms should appear
consistently across sectors, even though their imple­
mentation varies. Our sample includes organizations
of different sizes, from small practices to large enter­
prises. Although organizational size was not systemati­
cally controlled, the recurrence of key themes across
firms of varying scale suggests that the tension between
resource stability and capability adaptation operates in
similar ways, despite differences in resources and
execution.
Data
Our study sample consisted of 30 professionals from 13
industries. The finance sector represents the largest
group (five participants). Participants demonstrated
a wide range of professional work experience, ranging
from 3 to 36years (median, 5years; mean, 8.15 years).
The majority (60%) had 3–6 years of experience.
Midcareer professionals with 7–14years of experience
constituted 23.3% of the sample, while senior profes­
sionals with over 15 years of experience represented
16.7%. The participants held diverse positions, from
frontline (e.g., teachers and medical officers), middle
management (e.g., assistant managers and team lea­
ders), to senior leadership roles (e.g., managing direc­
tors and professors). Technical specialists were well
represented, including cloud architects, research engi­
neers, and data analysts, along with functional roles in
marketing, finance, and human resources. This distri­
bution provides broad perspectives from emerging pro­
fessionals to established practitioners, offering insights
into understanding and implementing DT across career
stages. Participants with more than five years of experi­
ence (about 50% of the sample) predominantly held
managerial positions, suggesting management’s role in

Methodology
Our study adopts a qualitative approach to examine
how organizational resources and capabilities facilitate
DT across sectors. Interview data were analyzed using
interpretive phenomenological analysis, which provides
a structured method for examining how individuals
interpret and make sense of organizational change
within their lived contexts.42
The analysis followed
a multi-stageprocess designedto ensure methodological
rigor and theoretical grounding.
Initial coding was conducted independently by two
research assistants under the supervision of the first
author. This procedure enhanced analytical reliability
and helped mitigate individual interpretive bias. The
first-stage analysis focused on identifying interview
segments in which participants described DT-related
experiences, particularly those concerning resources,
capabilities, and the transformation’s outcomes. This
stage generated 60 first-order codes grounded in par­
ticipants’ accounts, following established qualitative
practices.43
RBV and DCT served as sensitizing fra­
meworks, guiding interpretation while allowing novel
insights to emerge from the data.44
A qualitative
approach is particularly suited to examining DT
across diverse industries because the central research
question concerns how resources and capabilities
interact, a process-level inquiry that resists operatio­
nalization through fixed-variable surveys.
Interpretative phenomenological analysis is appropri­
ate here because it privileges participants’ interpretive
accounts of transformation experiences rather than
imposing predetermined categories, thereby allowing
cross-sector patterns to emerge from the data rather
than from the measurement instrument. The cross-
industry sample strengthens rather than dilutes the
analysis: our interest is in identifying mechanisms
that may recur across institutional contexts, not in
generating sector-specific prescriptions. Variation
across industries thus serves as an analytical resource
for distinguishing generalizable dynamics from con­
text-specific adaptations.
In the second stage, first-order codes were system­
atically compared and consolidated into 15 second-
order themes capturing key dimensions of DT,
including digital infrastructure, human capital, orga­
nizational resources, leadership support, cultural
readiness, external catalysts, learning agility, innova­
tion capability, and strategic alignment. Interrater
reliability for second-order coding reached 0.7, indi­
cating substantial agreement between coders.45
Across these themes, uncertainty emerged as a cross-
cutting condition rather than a discrete category.
Participants consistently framed transformation chal­
lenges in terms of resource (in)sufficiency, timing of
capability development, outcome unpredictability,
and environmental volatility. This insight informed
our theoretical emphasis on managing persistent
contradictions rather than pursuing stable transfor­
mation endpoints.
The final analytical stage aggregated second-order
themes into higher-level theoretical dimensions that
align with RBV and DCT. This step enabled the identi­
fication of recurring patterns in how organizations
mobilize resources and cultivate capabilities during
DT, while remaining attentive to sector-specific varia­
tion. Table 1 summarizes the qualitative coding scheme
underpinning this analysis.
Findings and analyses
Our findings are organized into five interconnected
dimensions that show how DT unfolds as a process of
persistent tension management. Each dimension is
grounded in recurring patterns across participant
accounts: the framework components did not precede
the analysis but emerged from the convergence of first-
and second-order codes (see Table 1). Rather than pre­
senting a linear progression, the data show how organi­
zations navigate contradictions between competing
demands and paradoxical outcomes, and how success
in one dimension can generate new tensions in another.

Resources
DT resources emerge in practice as a tightly coupled
system of IT infrastructure, human capital, and resource
management capabilities. Rather than simply enabling
transformation, resource adequacy often introduces
new organizational tensions. Resources designed to
resolve inefficiencies frequently generate new depen­
dencies, risks, and coordination challenges, creating
a paradox in which organizations become simulta­
neously more capable and more fragile.
IT infrastructure forms the technical backbone of DT
and is often associated with clear efficiency gains.
Participants described substantial improvements from
replacing manual tools with scalable technologies:
“Previously, we used Excel for data processing, which
was a manual and time-consuming method. Now, we
leverage Python and other modern coding software,
enabling more efficient and scalable data operations”
[INV-1].a
Enterprise systems further automate and
standardize workflows, as reflected in one organiza­
tion’s ERP overhaul: “The introduction of SAP and the
revamping of our ERP system have automated processes
that were previously manual, ensuring greater efficiency
and consistency” [INV-9]. Yet these advances also
expose organizations to new vulnerabilities. As systems
grow more complex, failures become harder to diagnose
and more disruptive: “The system has several shortcom­
ings, with numerous loopholes and bugs hampering its
efficiency.Despite these flaws, weoften have no choice but
to push through and meet deadlines through patchwork
solutions” [INV-17]. Digital infrastructure thus ampli­
fies both operational capacity and systemic risk.
Human capital represents the most contested
resource in DT. Organizations face persistent tension
between the demand for digital skills and uneven cap­
ability distribution across the workforce. Skill-building
initiatives increase technical capacity, but they also cre­
ate generational and cultural divides: “Previously, only
50–60% of employees possessed coding skills, but the
organization has since focused on improving technical
expertise” [INV-1], while “older employees, particularly
those in their 50’s and 60’s, often struggle with adopting
digital tools due to limited prior exposure” [INV-24].
Training programs partially address these gaps but
introduce new inequalities. As one participant noted,
“Employees attended basic training sessions to learn new
software, but the limited depth of these programs left gaps
in understanding. Many had to rely on self-learning”
[INV-1]. Continuous learning demands often clash
with operational pressures and individual capacity for
change.

tradictions. Financial constraints frequently pit

Enabling factors
DT depends on the alignment of leadership, organiza­
tional culture, external pressures, and institutional fra­
meworks. Yet our findings show that these factors rarely
reinforce one another in practice. Instead, they generate
persistent tensions that organizations must navigate.
Success emerges not from perfect alignment, but from
the ability to live with contradiction.
Leadership commitment illustrates this paradox
clearly. Transparent communication can help create
shared direction. One participant described how “our
director has adopted a more transparent approach by
hosting monthly online meetings to discuss organiza­
tional priorities, helping employees stay aligned with the
vision and goals” [INV-5]. However, clarity at the top
often breaks down during execution. Another intervie­
wee noted that managers are expected to push adoption,
but “sometimes they will give unclear instructions or steps
which would result in mistakes or end up with needing
even more manual work, ultimately wasting time and
defeating the purpose of digital transformation” [INV-1].
Middle managers are caught in between. While they are
expected to act as change agents, staying updated on
agile practices to “bridge the gap between strategic goals
and day-to-day operations” [INV-29], this role fre­
quently creates tension between loyalty to strategy and
responsibility for operational stability.
Organizational culture reveals a similar gap between
intention and behavior. Many organizations emphasize
innovation as a core value. One participant explained
that “our organizational culture is built around being
industry leaders, which requires constant innovation,
pushing the adoption of new systems to stay ahead”
[INV-9]. Yet resistance may persist even in these set­
tings. As another interviewee put it, “employees must
embrace a mindset of continuous learning, but resistance
to change continues to limit growth” [INV-30].
Collaborative approaches are intended to ease this tran­
sition. Organizations conduct internal testing so that
“feedback is gathered and functionality ensured before
launch, helping to build confidence” [INV-21]. But these
collaborative processes can slow decisions, replacing
momentum with hesitation.
External forces amplify these internal strains. The
COVID-19 pandemic was repeatedly described as
a turning point. One participant noted that “COVID-
19 acted as a catalyst for DT, pushing organizations to
adopt new technologies at an unprecedented pace” [INV-
16]. Yet speed often came at the expense of coordination
and sustainability. Uncertainty became a constant con­
dition. As one respondent explained, “we can’t plan
transformations like traditional projects anymore,
because each quarter brings new disruptions that force
continual reconfiguration” [INV-12]. During the pan­
demic, organizations were pushed to “experiment in
production, learning while delivering, creating stress
alongside rapid learning” [INV-24]. Those able to rede­
ploy resources quickly proved more resilient, while
others struggled as “competition from agile digital
entrants intensified” [INV-3].
Institutional and regulatory environments further
complicate readiness. Regulators play contradictory
roles. As one participant observed, authorities aim to
foster “a very digital friendly, very innovative kind of
environment, while simultaneously acting as supervisors
that slow transformation” [INV-2]. In healthcare, com­
pliance demands are even more acute. System changes
driven by data security regulations require “patient
records to be fragmented and reported centrally, even
when this conflicts with efficiency goals” [INV-18].
Across these conditions, DT readiness depends less on
alignment than on the capacity to endure and manage
persistent tension.
Adaptive capabilities
Adaptive capabilities are often framed as an organiza­
tion’s strength in responding to DT, yet our findings
show that these capabilities frequently generate new
problems even as they solve old ones. Rather than sta­
bilizing change, adaptation itself can become a source of
instability that organizations must continuously
manage.
This paradox is evident in change management.
Despite sustained efforts, resistance remains persistent,
especially among employees accustomed to manual rou­
tines. As one interviewee explained, many staff “resist
adopting new systems, citing familiarity and comfort with

nizations often rely on incremental strategies. One par­

case that serves as an anchor to build momentum for
wider adoption” [INV-9]. While such pilots create opti­

works in a limited setting often fails to scale, leaving
organizations frustrated when broader implementation
stalls. Phased rollouts follow a similar pattern. Although
pilot projects are intended to “test feasibility before
expansion, running multiple systems in parallel fre­

attention across teams” [INV-24].
Learning and development initiatives reveal similar
tensions. Mandatory training is commonly used to sig­

promote professional growth” [INV-29]. In practice,
however, compulsory learning often leads to compli­
ance rather than engagement, generating resentment
instead of capability development. Generational divides
further complicate these efforts. New platforms can
“intimidate older advisors, requiring targeted programs
to build confidence” [INV-5], yet such targeting may
unintentionally reinforce group boundaries.
Knowledge-sharing mechanisms face similar coordina­
tion problems. Centralized hubs are designed to “facil­
itate the exchange of tools and practices” [INV-3], but
they can easily become bottlenecks that fail to reach
those who need support most.
Innovation capabilities add another layer of tension.
Organizations actively “encourage creativity and provide
platforms to turn ideas into action” [INV-14], yet many

or inertia. “Hands-on experimentation helps employees
learn by doing” [INV-1], but when left unstructured it
produces inconsistent practices and reduced efficiency.
Together, these findings suggest that adaptive capabilities
are not straightforward solutions, but ongoing balancing
acts that require constant negotiation.
Capability cultivation
Capability cultivation exposes the persistent gap
between transformation ambitions and implementa­
tion realities. Organizations are not simply building
new skills or systems; they are constantly juggling
competing demands for efficiency, integration, and
customer service, while also absorbing the disruptions
created by capability development itself. In this sense,
cultivation acts as a bridge between adaptive intent
and actual transformation outcomes, translating
potential into practice through structured but tension-
laden processes.
Process enhancement illustrates this clearly. Moving
beyond basic digitization, organizations reconfigure
workflows to create value and improve efficiency. As
one participant noted, “digitizing workflows has elimi­
nated manual errors and improved the speed of our
operations, with tasks that were once manual now fully
automated” [INV-21]. Yet these gains introduce
a familiar trade-off. Standardization improves control
and speed, but it also constrains flexibility. Integration
intensifies this tension. Bringing new systems into
legacy environments raise issues of compatibility and
data migration, requiring “careful planning and testing
to avoid disruptions” [INV-29]. While such preparation
is necessary, it often clashes with organizational pres­
sure to realize transformation benefits quickly. Even
when integration succeeds, it remains fragile. One inter­
viewee described combining advanced systems with
existing infrastructure as “complex but rewarding, allow­
ing the organization to leverage both old and new tech­
nologies” [INV-18]. At the same time, these hybrid
arrangements are harder to maintain as compared to
legacy or fully modern setups, creating new manage­
ment burdens.
Customer-centric development adds another
layer of complexity. Digital interaction capabilities
clearly create value, as “customers increasingly prefer
online engagement for convenience and immediacy”
[INV-24]. However, preferences differ across seg­
ments, forcing organizations to sustain multiple
service channels rather than simplifying operations.
Service improvements also raise expectations. One
participant observed that “processes that once took
days can now be completed within hours” [INV-18],
but faster service quickly becomes the new baseline,
driving even more resource-intensive demands.
Feedback mechanisms further complicate matters.
While “customer feedback plays a pivotal role in
shaping digital services” [INV-20], such input can
be contradictory, requiring organizations to manage
competing expectations rather than simply respond.
Strategic alignment is meant to stabilize these
dynamics. “Digital strategies provide direction and
clarity” [INV-24], but they age quickly in volatile envir­
onments. Aligning initiatives with business goals helps
avoid waste, yet overly rigid alignment can crowd out
unexpected opportunities. “Performance metrics are
essential for tracking progress” [INV-18], but isolating
the effects of digital initiatives is difficult, forcing

Transformation outcomes
Historically, major technological advances have been
accompanied by paradoxical tensions, where gains in
efficiency and scale generate new vulnerabilities. DT
continues this pattern: improvements in speed, integra­
tion, and flexibility often coexist with increased fragility
and coordination challenges. These enduring paradoxes
show that DT does not yield linear performance
improvements but instead requires ongoing managerial
effort to translate short-term success into sustainable
performance.
DT outcomes reveal a set of fundamental para­
doxes in which progress in one area often generates
strain in another. Rather than delivering unambigu­
ous gains, DT produces improvements that must be
actively managed to avoid new forms of vulnerabil­
ity. Organizations therefore face the challenge of
converting measurable success into sustainable
performance.
Operational improvement is a clear example. Many
organizations report that “digitalized workflows have
streamlined processes, reduced errors, and freed employ­
ees from repetitive tasks so they can focus on higher-
value work” [INV-18]. These efficiency gains, however,
can make operations more brittle. Highly optimized
processes leave little room for disruption, meaning
that small system failures can cascade quickly. Cost
reductions follow a similar pattern. For instance, “digi­
tizing document management has reduced spending on
paper and printing” [INV-7], yet these savings are often
offset by new expenses related to system maintenance,
software updates, and cybersecurity. Quality improve­
ments also carry hidden risks. “Automated checks
enhance consistency and reliability” [INV-18], but they
can entrench biases and may struggle in situations
where human judgments are more appropriate.
Competitive positioning further illustrates the dou­
ble-edged nature of DT. “Early technology adoption can
provide a crucial market edge, allowing organizations to
seize opportunities before competitors” [INV-29]. Early
adopters absorb higher costs and risks tied to immature
technologies. “Building strong digital capabilities sup­
ports operational efficiency and strategic decision-
making” [INV-24], but rapid technological change may
quickly erode advantages. Even innovation-oriented
cultures are not without cost. “Encouraging experimen­
tation and creativity energizes transformation” [INV-
24], yet sustained innovation can tire employees out
and introduce instability that undermines day-to-day
performance.
Business performance outcomes reflect similar ten­
sions. “Improved market positioning and customer
experiences often deliver immediate gains” [INV-24],
but maintaining these advantages demands continuous
investment that strains resources. “Digital services raise
customer satisfaction by making interactions more seam­
less and personalized” [INV-18], yet satisfaction quickly
translates into higher expectations that are increasingly
costly to meet. Competitive advantages gained through
DTare rarelyenduring. “Staying aheadrequiresconstant
vigilance and ongoing innovation” [INV-24]. Overall,
DT outcomes show that success is not an endpoint but
a new condition to manage. Organizationsmust develop
capabilities to not only achieve improvement, but to
cope with the accompanying pressures.
Discussion
Our findings show that DT is a multidimensional pro­
cess in which digital technologies act as catalysts for
reconfiguring how organizations create and capture
value, ultimately reshaping organizational identity.5
This distinguishes DT from ITOT, the latter optimizes
existing processes while preserving established value
propositions. DT does not unfold linearly. Instead, its
components interact in dynamic and often contradic­
tory ways, producing ongoing tensions as organizations
shift from resource exploitation toward capability
reconfiguration.
Transformation occurs when enabling factors
prompt organizations to move beyond static resource
use toward dynamic capability deployment. This cataly­
ticshift is rarelysmooth. Foundationalresources suchas
IT infrastructure, digital platforms, and human capital
remain essential, yet their effectiveness depends on how
organizations manage competing demands and internal
conflicts.46
The move from resource stability to capabil­
ity-based change exposes structural paradoxes with
uneven adaptation outcomes.
Leadership commitment, supportive culture, and
external pressures (e.g., COVID-19) function as cata­
lysts that accelerate this shift.2,9
At the same time, these
factors generate new constraints. Leadership vision can
enable transformation, but weak execution may erode
trustandstall progress.32
Externalshockscreate urgency
while compressing timelines, intensifying trade-offs
rather than simplifying implementation.
Adaptive capabilities, such as innovation, agility, and
continuous learning, are central responses to
uncertainty.16,47
Yet they also introduce new strains, as
experimentation and iteration can overwhelm

Theoretical implications
Through the development of the REACT framework, our
study advances the theoretical understanding of DT,
explaining how organizations can navigate the persistent
tension between resource-based stability and capability-
based change. Rather than treating transformation as
a linear progression from resource possession to capabil­
ity deployment, the framework conceptualizes DT as an
ongoing process in which enabling factors determine
when and how organizations shift from using existing
resources to reconfiguring capabilities.49
Figure 2 illus­
trates the REACT framework.
To illustrate: a retail bank in our sample held sub­
stantial digital infrastructure (Dimension 1) but
struggled to advance transformation until COVID-19
(an external catalyst in Dimension 2) prompted leader­
ship to authorize rapid resource redeployment. This
catalytic shift activated learning agility and change man­
agement capabilities (Dimension 3), which were then
cultivated into customer-facing digital services
(Dimension 4), though with the side effect of raising
customer expectations faster than what the back-end
systems could support (Dimension 5). The REACT fra­
mework thus maps this recursive tension rather than
resolves it.
A central contribution of REACT lies in its re-
specification of enabling factors as transformation
catalysts rather than passive facilitators. Leadership
commitment, cultural readiness, and external pres­
sures such as COVID-19 trigger decisive shifts in
organizational orientation, forcing firms to question
existing value creation logic and identity claims.2,9
Our findings help explain why organizations with
similar resource endowments experience divergent
transformation trajectories. Transformation success
depends less on the presence of valuable resources
or advanced capabilities, and more on whether
enabling conditions activate a willingness and
capacity to reconfigure them. Importantly, these cat­
alytic processes do not resolve organizational ten­
sions. Leadership vision can align action while
exposing execution gaps, illustrating how the same
enabling factor can simultaneously generate momen­
tum and fragility.32
REACT extends the RBV by showing that digital
competitive advantages arise from orchestrated changes
in how resources are deployed, not from resource pos­
session alone.46
While RBV emphasizes VRIN
characteristics,13,14
our findings demonstrate that such
attributes only become consequential when organiza­
tions are catalyzed to shift from efficiency-oriented
exploitation toward exploration-oriented capability
development. This transition introduces enduring ten­
sions between operational stability and adaptability,
particularly as digital tools expose skill gaps and resis­
tance that threaten established routines.
At the same time, REACT advances DCT by concep­
tualizing adaptive capabilities as mechanisms for mana­
ging tension rather than as solutions. Change
management capability, learning agility, and innovation
capability enable organizations to navigate uncertainty
through ongoing recalibration, not through one-off cap­
ability building.15,16
These capabilities operate through
feedback loops that can amplify both positive outcomes
and organizational strain, reinforcing the need for con­
tinuous adjustment.
A further theoretical contribution lies in introducing
capability cultivation as the bridge between adaptive
potential and transformation outcomes. Through pro­
cess enhancement, customer-oriented development,
and strategic alignment, organizations translate adap­
tive capacity into concrete action. These mechanisms
are interdependent and recursive rather than sequential.
Improvements in processes cause new customer expec­
tations, customer insights reshape strategic priorities,
and strategic choices redirect capability investments.
Success in one domain often generates tension in others,
underscoring that transformation outcomes are provi­
sional rather than final.39,40,48
Taken together, REACT reframes DT as a complex,
recursive system in which outcomes feed back into
resources and enabling conditions, shaping subsequent
transformation cycles.49
By positioning contradiction
management as central to DT, the framework offers
a more realistic foundation for understanding transfor­
mation across sectors and organizational contexts,
where persistent tensions are inherent to change rather
than mere implementation failures.9
REACT’s theoreti­
cal novelty lies in three specific moves that differentiate
it from prior work. First, unlike RBV-only accounts that
treat resources as static endowments, REACT

The REACT framework is most applicable to organi­
zations undergoing substantive DT, where digital tech­
nologies are actively reconfiguring value creation and
organizational identity, rather than to incremental IT-
enabled improvements. Its generalizability is strongest
in contexts characterized by significant environmental
uncertainty and multi-stakeholder complexity, such as
those represented in this study. Caution is warranted
when applying REACT to highly regulated or capital-
constrained settings, where enabling conditions (parti­
cularly cultural readiness and leadership discretion) are
severely restricted, as the catalytic mechanisms central
to the framework may operate differently. Similarly, the
framework is not designed to predict transformation
outcomes or prescribe sequencing; it is a conceptual
lens for diagnosing and navigating tensions.
Conclusion
Our study introduces the REACT framework as
a theory-building conceptual model that explains DT
as an ongoing organizational process rather than
a technological outcome. The framework is not
intended as an implementation guide or predictive
tool. Instead, it advances theory by clarifying how orga­
nizations navigate enduring tensions between resource-
based stability and capability-based change, with
enabling factors acting as catalysts rather than resolu­
tions. Our findings underscore that successful DT
requires moving beyond technology upgrades to recog­
nize transformation as a recursive process shaped by
persistent contradictions.
The REACT framework contributes by positioning
enabling factors such as leadership commitment, cul­
tural readiness, and external pressures as triggers that
shift organizations from resource exploitation to cap­
ability reconfiguration. Importantly, this shift does not
eliminate tensions but reproduces them in new forms.
While the framework offers a generalizable structure, its
components manifest differently across organizational
and institutional contexts. Cross-sector analysis sug­
gests that core transformation mechanisms are shared,
even as strategies for managing tensions vary.
Several limitations suggest directions for future
research. Uneven sectoral representation constrains
claims about industry-specific dynamics. The cross-
sectional design captures transformation at particular
moments, potentially overlooking how tension manage­
ment evolves over time. Qualitative insights could be
complemented by mixed-methods research to test rela­
tionships among framework elements and to examine
recursive feedback loops. Data collection during the
COVID-19 period may also reflect crisis-driven
acceleration rather than steady-state transformation.
Despite these limitations, REACT advances DT theory
by framing persistent contradictions as inherent fea­
tures of transformation, providing a foundation for
future research on how organizations manage compet­
ing demands over time.

Appendix B
Interview questions
(1) Please introduce yourself, your professional background,
your current role, and your experience level.
(2) As we dive into digital transformation, please share
information about your organization, your specific area
of expertise, and how it impacts your perspective on this
issue.
(3) What external factors (e.g., market trends, customer
needs, and technological advances) influence your orga­
nization’s digital transformation journey?.
(4) Can you describe the significant challenges your organi­
zation faces in its digital transformation journey and
how do you overcome them?.
(5) In the past few decades, disruptive events such as the
dot-com bubble in the late 1990’s, the financial crisis of
1998 and 2008, and, most recently, the Covid-19 pan­
demic have drastically affected industries worldwide.
Can you share how these events have impacted your
industry and your organization’s digital transformation
journey (e.g., the pace of transformation, effort in push­
ing forward the use of technologies, etc.)?
(6) Can you provide specific examples of how these events
change your pace of digital transformation? Did any
technologies or digital strategies that emerged turn out
to be especially important in response to these events in
your industry?
(7) What factors significantly influence the pace of digital
transformation within your organization?
(8) From your perspective, why do you think different indus­
tries adapt to digital transformation at varying speeds?
(9) Can you share an instance in which an unexpected event
significantly delays your digital transformation effort?
How did your company react and adapt to this setback?
(10) How do you think digital transformation may influence
middle managers’ roles and responsibilities? Can you
provide specific examples of managers’ reaction to such
changes?.
(11) Which division(s) in your company are involved in the
development and transformation of skills (e.g., HR, senior
leadership, IT, and/or other functional teams)? How will
this affect the digital transformation outcomes?.
(12) How do different divisions within your organization
(e.g., HR, senior leadership, IT, and/or other functional
teams) collaborate in the skill development process dur­
ing digital transformation? Can you share any successful
practices or challenges encountered in fostering interdi­
visional collaboration for skill development?.
(13) How has your organization’s culture changed or evolved
during the digital transformation?.
(14) How has your organization instilled a culture of innova­
tion to support and accelerate digital transformation
initiatives? Can you share an example of how this culture
of innovation has been instrumental in your transforma­
tion process?.
(15) Has your organization initiated any collaboration with
startups, tech companies, universities, or research insti­
tutes to accelerate digital transformation? Please share
any experience regarding employee training, exchange
programs, or similar initiatives to enhance a team’s digi­
tal skills.
(16) How do you ensure continuous learning and adaptation
to sustain the momentum of the digital transformation?
Can you share any practices or strategies that are parti­
cularly effective?.
(17) As digital transformation continues, how do you address
the evolving skill requirements of your organization and
sustain a culture conducive to digital innovation? What are
the challenges? How did you overcome these problems?
