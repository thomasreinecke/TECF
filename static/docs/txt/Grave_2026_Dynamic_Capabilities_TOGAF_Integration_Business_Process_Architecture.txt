Abstract. Designingprocessesthatalignwithstrategicgoalshasbecomeincreas-
ingly challenging in today’s fast-paced business environment. Business Process
Architecture (BPA) plays a vital role in achieving this strategic alignment and
ensuring that processes are coherent both in design and implementation. To tackle
thesechallenges,companiescanutilizetheirdynamiccapabilitiestoadapttorapid
changes. One well-known method for enterprise architecture that includes BPA
development is The Open Group Architecture Framework (TOGAF). We applied
design science research to integrate dynamic capabilities theory with the TOGAF
framework. We developed and evaluated a BPA method suitable for strategic
renewal in unpredictable environments. As a result, we have created and evalu-
ated a BPA development method that provides practical guidance for applying
dynamic capabilities theory in a real-world business context.
Keywords: Business Process Architecture · TOGAF · Dynamic Capabilities ·
Design Science Research
1 Introduction
Static business process architecture (BPA) models struggle to adapt to fast-paced and
turbulent business environments. Within the Open Group Architecture Framework
(TOGAF), BPA is a core deliverable of the Architecture Development Method (ADM)
[1]. Yet TOGAF’s prescribed Business Architecture phase offers only high-level guid-
ance on process catalogues and value-stream mappings, without prescribing how to
embedmanagerialroutinesforongoingreconfigurationofprocessesinresponsetoenvi-
ronmental change [2, 3]. As a result, firms relying on TOGAF risk strategic misalign-
ment, stalled digital transformation (DT) initiatives, and loss of competitive advantage
in volatile contexts.
Modern DT imperatives—spurred by Artificial Intelligence, the Internet of Things,
and cloud—require continuous reconfiguration of technology, data, processes, and peo-
ple to capture emergent opportunities [2, 3]. In particular, new architecture archetypes,
such as platform and ecosystem architectures, demand a high degree of co-creation [4].
Enterprise architecture (EA) artifacts, especially a high-level BPA, play a pivotal role

2 Related Work
Dynamic capabilities theory represents a mid-range strategic management framework
that elucidates how organizations intentionally adapt, integrate, and reconfigure both
internal and external competencies in response to rapidly changing environments.
Teece’s [6] foundational work characterizes dynamic capabilities as comprising three
clusters of micro foundations: (1) sensing opportunities and threats; (2) seizing oppor-
tunities through strategic investment and design; and (3) transforming the organization
by recombining assets and structures. Eisenhardt and Martin [7] refine this perspec-
tive by describing dynamic capabilities as specific, identifiable processes (e.g., product
development, partnering) whose effectiveness is context-dependent and aimed at facili-
tating rapid adaptation. Helfat and Peteraf [15] further highlight the evolutionary nature

of capabilities, emphasizing the critical roles of managerial cognition and governance
in the development of capabilities. In the context of EA, including BPA, it is more
appropriate to refer to a mobilizing capability rather than a seizing capability, as stated
by Van de Wetering [16]. Mobilizing is essential for businesses as it involves engag-
inginenterprise-wideactivitiesthatfosterinformationsharingandcollaborationamong
stakeholders.Thisapproachnotonlyenhancesteamworkwithintheorganizationbutalso
equips the firm to swiftly adapt its resources and assets in response to changing mar-
ket conditions. By prioritizing mobilization, companies can drive innovation, enhance
operational efficiency, and maintain a competitive edge in their respective industries.
By anchoring BPA development within the framework of dynamic capabilities the-
ory, we can transcend static “blueprint” models and incorporate process routines that
iteratively cycle through sensing, mobilizing, and transforming phases. This alignment
enables BPA artifacts not only to depict current (“as-is”) and desired (“to-be”) process
landscapes, but also to prescribe methodologies for organizations to continuously renew
and adapt their processes in response to environmental cues.
TOGAF’s ADM designates Phase B (Business Architecture) specifically for delin-
eatingprocesscatalogs,value-streammaps,andorganizationalroles[1].Typicaloutputs
from this phase include: (1) Baseline business architecture, which documents current
processes and value streams, (2) Target business architecture: future-state processes and
value propositions, (3) Gap analysis: identification of discrepancies between baseline
and target states, and (4) Opportunities and solutions: a roadmap of initiatives to bridge
identified gaps.
However, while these deliverables provide a high-level overview, they lack explicit
guidance for embedding continuous process reconfiguration routines. In practice, archi-
tects may adapt or bypass certain steps or neglect outputs due to constraints posed by,
e.g., legacy systems or local governance, leading to inconsistent artifacts that fail to
institutionalize iterative renewal [17].
For instance, Kotusev [17] noted, in one of his case studies, that the organization in
question utilized five EA-related processes—namely, needs assessment, project evalua-
tion, project prioritization, project design, and project execution—rather than adhering
to the typical ADM outlined by TOGAF. Grave et al. [9] observe that the actual applica-
tion of EA artifacts varies from case to case and does not consistently follow the specific
outputs outlined by TOGAF. Consequently, although TOGAF offers a solid framework
for BPA, its methodical steps, surrounding the process as well as the accompanying
EA artifacts, remain inadequately defined to support dynamic, capability-driven process
architectures.
Theintegrationofaformaltheory,specifically,dynamiccapabilities,intotheTOGAF
framework aims to address a significant gap in the existing literature. Much of the
previous research tends to discuss the relationship between dynamic capabilities and
BPA incompletely. For instance, Grave et al. [9] developed a strategic planning process
predicated on dynamic capabilities, which establishes connections between EA artifacts
andthedimensionsofsensing,mobilizing,andtransformingcapabilities.However,their
analysis neglects to account for BPA artifacts, focusing instead on, for example, a high-
level operational concept artifact that does not directly pertain to the BPA framework.

Furthermore, it is evident that BPA methodologies, as a whole, are characterized
by a lack of established best practices and structured specifications. Dijkman et al. [18]
conducted a survey involving 39 practitioners within the BPA domain, supplemented by
four empirical case studies analyzing real-world process architectures. They concluded
thatthereisnobestpracticeinthisarea,andtheirsampledidnotshowastrongpreference
for “one particular approach to business process architecture” (p. 155). In a similar vein,
Gonzalez-LopezandBustos[19]identifiedadiversearrayofBPAdevelopmentmethods,
highlighting the absence of structured specifications for these methodologies.
This discourse highlights the need for a more cohesive theoretical framework within
BPA practices, underscoring the potential of dynamic capabilities to provide the nec-
essary structure and clarity. By explicitly adopting Teece’s dynamic-capabilities theory
as our foundational framework, we can (a) delineate precise micro foundations to be
integrated within BPA artifacts, (b) systematically map each TOGAF ADM step to
corresponding sensing, mobilizing, or transforming activities, and (c) ensure that the
resulting artifacts support a dynamic approach to process architecture development that
is responsive to environmental changes.
3 Research Methodology
This study employs a DSR paradigm to develop and evaluate a BPA methodology com-
pliant with TOGAF, integrating dynamic capabilities while supporting new architecture
archetypes. We adapted Österle et al.‘s [14] four-step DSR process: (1) Analysis, (2)
Design, (3) Evaluation, and (4) Communication. During the design phase, we con-
ducted a literature review following Okoli and Schabram’s guidelines [20], tailored to a
single-researcher protocol by omitting the training of multiple coders.
We adopted a requirements-driven mapping approach, following Lapouchnian et al.
[21] to ensure rigorous alignment of each requirement with specific components of the
artifact. We renamed Österle et al.’s [14] fourth step from ‘Diffusion’ to ‘Communica-
tion’,reflectingtheemphasisonanactive,two-wayprocessthatincorporatestheoretical
rigor, stakeholder feedback, and ethical transparency, as proposed by Hevner et al. [11]
and Peffers et al. [13]. This shift ensures that artifacts are accessible and impactful.
Additionally, we position our study within the context of design-oriented information
systems research (DOIS), employing a four-step process enhanced by insights from rel-
evant literature [22]. This process was implemented as described by [14] and further
enhanced with insights from [13], as illustrated in Fig. 1.

5.1 Changes from Literature Review to Final BPA Development Method
In developing the BPA method, we began with process steps identified in the literature
review and then refined them through iterative feedback. Significant changes were made
to ensure the method would effectively build dynamic capabilities in a real organiza-
tional context. For example, the initial literature-based framework lacked an explicit
step for continuous sensing of environmental changes, a capability crucial for agility.
In the final version, we introduced a dedicated step for scanning and updating strategic
assumptions,reflectingthedynamiccapabilitiesperspectiveofsensingopportunitiesand
threats. We also reordered certain steps to better align with the TOGAF ADM sequence.
Specifically, steps related to defining the target architecture and planning migrations
were moved later in the process, after a thorough gap analysis, to align with TOGAF’s
proven logic. Another key change was the addition of feedback loops: the final method
emphasizes iteration, whereas the initial concept was more linear. This means that after
implementing portions of the architecture, the process can loop back to reassess strategy
or capabilities, embodying the transforming aspect of dynamic capabilities (continu-
ous renewal). Additionally, stakeholder input was given a more prominent role – for
instance, the final method includes workshops with business owners to validate archi-
tecture outputs, a practice not stressed in the literature review version. These changes
wereguidedbyfocus-groupevaluationswithpractitioners,ensuringthemethodevolved
from a theoretical construct into a practically applicable approach. The result is a BPA
development method that remains grounded in EA best practices (via TOGAF) while
explicitly incorporating dynamic capability-building activities.

5.4 Limitations and Future Research
This research acknowledges significant limitations. The BPA method has only been
tested in two focus group sessions with domain experts, making the current evaluation
exploratoryandqualitative,withoutcomparativebenchmarkingagainstapproachessuch
as the TOGAF ADM method. Therefore, we should exercise caution in asserting the
method’s effectiveness.
Additionally, the study’s findings are limited in generalizability, as it was con-
ducted within a government organization with specific regulatory requirements. While

--- PAGE 14 ---
186 F. Grave et al.
the method may adapt to different contexts, empirical evidence is lacking, and various
industries may face unique challenges.
The method primarily addresses agility in sensing and responding to change, while
other dynamic capabilities, such as learning and networking, receive less emphasis.
Future versions could benefit from incorporating elements such as open innovation and
stakeholder engagement.
The lack of longitudinal data is another shortcoming, as we have not observed the
method’s long-term impact on organizational agility and transformation.
Future research should pilot the BPA method in various organizations to evaluate
outcomes such as architectural change speed and stakeholder satisfaction. Comparative
studies with traditional methods could reveal differences in adaptability and perfor-
mance. Additionally, integrating tools such as intelligent automation and AI into EA is
an area for improvement.
Theoretically, further investigation into EA-driven dynamic capabilities is needed.
Our findings suggest that framing EA development as a dynamic capability is beneficial
and can lead to hypotheses that link our methods to improved innovation and agility,
which may be tested through surveys or case studies.
5.5 Concluding Remarks
By weaving dynamic-capabilities theory into a TOGAF-aligned BPA method, this
research offers both a robust theoretical framework and a pragmatic toolkit for con-
tinuous strategic renewal. The method’s structured yet adaptable approach empowers
organizations to sense market changes, mobilize resources around emerging opportuni-
ties,andtransformtheirBPAsinrapid,governedcycles.Withfurtherempiricalvalidation
and technological augmentation, it promises to reshape how EA drives organizational
agility in volatile environments.
