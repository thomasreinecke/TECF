Navigating foundational uncertainty: a dynamic-adaptive model of
enterprise digital transformation in the age of generative AI
Abstract
The advent of Generative AI (Gen-AI) has precipitated an epistemological crisis in strategic
management, challenging the foundational assumptions of enterprise digital transformation.
Traditional frameworks, operating under a “Paradigm of Rational Mastery”, view technology as a
predictable, manageable tool. This paper argues that the unique technical characteristics of Gen-
AI—specifically its “Emergent Abilities” and “Black-Box Nature”—have given rise to a novel
strategic condition termed “Foundational Uncertainty”, rendering these established models
obsolete. This study critiques the prevalent “Two-Layer Model” of digital transformation, which
is predicated on predictable technological advancement and planned “enablement”. In its place,
this paper develops and proposes a “Dynamic-Adaptive Model” designed for the Gen-AI era. This
new model reconceptualizes the firm-technology relationship as a co-evolutionary process. It
posits that firms must shift from planned implementation to continuous “Adaptive Sensemaking”,
an action-driven process of experimentation and learning to navigate the technology’s
unpredictability. At the enterprise level, strategy must become an “Evolutionary Process” that
emerges from this learning. The model’s central mechanism is a cyclical interplay of “Contention
& Adaptation”—a generative tension between top-down managerial control (exploitation) and
bottom-up, technology-driven learning (exploration) that propels organizational evolution.
Consequently, the source of sustained competitive advantage shifts from possessing static,
inimitable (VRIN) resources to cultivating a higher-order “Dynamic Adaptive Capability”—the
organizational meta-capability for reconfiguring its own learning processes to thrive amidst
unknowable change. The paper concludes by outlining the model’s significant theoretical
contributions to the literature on digital transformation, Dynamic Capabilities, and the Resource-
Based View, providing a new framework for navigating the Gen-AI frontier.
Key Words: Generative AI; Enterprise Digital Transformation; Foundational Uncertainty;
Dynamic Adaptive Capability; Organizational Ambidexterity

losing their efficacy in the face of what this paper terms Foundational Uncertainty. This distinct
strategic condition arises from the profound and seemingly irreducible unpredictability
surrounding the core technology’s structural evolution, developmental trajectories, and future
capabilities (Courtney et al., 1997; Schoemaker, 1995). Its origins are not in market dynamics but

The first technical driver of this uncertainty is the phenomenon of Emergent Abilities, which refers

despite not being explicitly programmed into the system (Wei et al., 2022). These emergent skills

and can shift the technological frontier instantaneously. Itisimportant to acknowledge the ongoing
scholarly debate regarding the nature of these abilities: whether they are genuinely “emergent”
phenomena arising from complexity, or if they are latent capabilities “engineered” into the system
architecturethat areonlyrevealed byspecificevaluationmetrics (Schaeffer etal., 2023).However,
from the perspective of strategic management, this technical distinction is secondary. For the
corporate decision-maker, the outcome is identical regardless of its origin: the evolutionary path
of the technology’s core functionalities becomes highly unpredictable, constituting a fundamental
strategic challenge. It is this managerial experience of unpredictability, rather than its ultimate
scientific explanation, that forms the crux of the problem.
AmplifyingthischallengeistheinherentlyBlack-BoxNatureofadvancedAImodels (Yudkowsky
& Soares, 2025). The decision-making mechanisms within systems like ChatGPT remain opaque,
a consequence of both their intrinsic technical complexity and proprietary commercial restrictions.
With architectures comprising hundreds of billions of parameters, it becomes nearly impossible to
tracehowspecificinputsproduceparticularoutputs.Asaresult,users—andofteneventhecreators
themselves—cannot fully elucidate the internal logic of the system. This opacity generates
significant strategic risks concerning trust, accountability, and bias detection (Pasquale, 2015;
Rudin, 2019), compelling firms to build strategies around technologies whose internal logic they
cannot comprehensively audit or understand (Shrestha et al., 2019).
The convergence of Emergent Abilities and operational opacity compels a fundamental shift in the
strategic posture of the firm. The traditional planning-action sequence is inverted, giving rise to an
Experience-Leads-Theory Paradigm. In this new paradigm, strategic insights and competitive
advantages derive not from pre-formulated theoretical plans but from empirical discoveries made
through large-scale, real-world experimentation (March, 1991; Levitt & March, 1988). This
process is more than simple trial-and-error; it represents a fundamental shift in organizational
epistemology that aligns with the theory of sensemaking. Faced with profound ambiguity,
organizations must first act—by experimenting—and then retrospectively analyze the surprising

1.2 The Theoretical Chasm: Failure of the Prevailing Paradigm of Rational Mastery
The state of Foundational Uncertainty created by Gen-AI collides directly with the foundational
assumptions of established strategic management theory. For decades, the literature has implicitly
operated under what can be termed the Paradigm of Rational Mastery. This paradigm assumes that
digital technologies, while powerful, are fundamentally predictable and manageable tools. Within
this worldview,digital transformation is a deliberate and controlled process: firms identifyspecific
business needs and then select understandable, appropriate, and stable technologies to “enable”
targeted solutions. The core assumptions underpinning this model are that technological
progression is linear and predictable—akin to climbing a ladder—and that the properties of a
technology are stable and fully comprehensible, allowing for its controlled application (Porter,
1985).
A profound theoretical tension, or chasm, emerges when the Paradigm of Rational Mastery is
confronted with the reality of Foundational Uncertainty. Theorderly,predictable world envisioned
by these models is fundamentally disrupted by Gen-AI. The phenomenon of Emergent Abilities
invalidates the assumption of incremental, predictable technological advancement, while the
technology’s Black-Box Nature shatters the presumption that it is a comprehensible and rationally
manageable tool (Knight, 2017). This sharp collision between the prevailing theoretical paradigm
and the new technological reality shakes the very foundations of “knowability” in strategic
management,precipitatingwhatthispaperdefinesasanepistemologicalcrisis.Thistermischosen
with precision. A simple “knowledge crisis” implies a lack of information, whereas an
“epistemological crisis” signifies a deeper failure in the methods and assumptions used to generate
knowledge. As research on paradigm shifts in the philosophy of science reveals, such crises
emerge when an existing framework proves incapable of explaining disruptive new phenomena.
The accumulation of “anomalies”—in this case, the unpredictable behaviors and opaque nature of
Gen-AI—that cannot be resolved through the “puzzle-solving” activities of “normal science”
forces a re-evaluation of the field’s core assumptions about how strategic knowledge can be
acquired and validated (Kuhn, 1970).

This crisis exposes the limitations of even the most sophisticated theories of adaptation (Tushman
& Anderson, 1986). Frameworks such as Dynamic Capabilities (DC) and Absorptive Capacity
(ACAP) were designed for an environment characterized by dynamic, yet ultimately
understandable, change (Teece et al., 1997). The language and logic of these canonical theories
presuppose a world that can be observed, analyzed, and acted upon in a quasi-rational manner.
However, the new environment is one in which the technology itself is a complex adaptive system
with an inherently unpredictable trajectory. The very terms used by established frameworks
become difficult to apply. For instance, how can a firm effectively “sense” and “seize”
opportunities related to a technology whose future capabilities emerge without warning, a core
tenet of DC theory? (Teece, 2007; Eisenhardt & Martin, 2000). How can it “recognize the value”
and “assimilate” a technology whose internal workings remain inscrutable, the central mechanism
of ACAP? (Cohen & Levinthal, 1990; Volberda et al., 2010). Consequently, even mainstream
theories that emphasize adaptation face profound limitations as their core mechanisms were not
designed to contend with such fundamental unknowability.
1.3 Bridging the Chasm: Research Question, Core Thesis, and Theoretical Contributions
Given the profound inadequacy of the prevailing paradigm to account for the strategic realities of
the Gen-AI era, this paper addresses the following central research question:
How does the foundational uncertainty inherent in Generative AI reshape enterprise digital
transformation, and what new co-evolutionary processes and adaptive mechanisms govern the
interaction between the enterprise and technology layers under these conditions?
In response, this paper’s core thesis is that firms must execute a paradigm shift from Rational
Mastery to Dynamic Adaptation. The primary driver of competitive advantage is no longer the
firm’s ability to strategically implement a predictable technology (“enablement”), but rather its
capacity to continuously co-evolve with a technology that behaves as an unpredictable agent. To
elucidate this transformative path, this paper constructs and proposes a new “Dynamic-Adaptive
Model”. The unique contribution of this model is its redefinition of the enterprise-technology
relationship as a co-evolutionary process driven by the generative tension of “Contention &
Adaptation”. This framing posits that the technology’s emergent behaviors will often conflict with
a firm’s established processes and strategies, creating “contention”. Strategic success, therefore,
hinges on the firm’s ability to develop organizational capabilities for rapid and effective
“adaptation” in response to this friction.
Positioned as a theory-building conceptual paper, this manuscript offers three key theoretical
contributions:

• To the Dynamic Capabilities Literature: It advances DC theory by identifying Foundational

and non-substitutable (VRIN) resources enabling superior capabilities (Barney, 1991;
Wernerfelt, 1984), the model posits that a VRIN meta-capability (the DAC) enables the firm
to generate a continuous stream of transient advantages by leveraging rapidly changing,
often commoditized technological resources. In this view, the ultimate strategic asset that is
VRIN is the organizational process of dynamic adaptation itself, ensuring the RBV’s
relevance for an era where static assets quickly lose value.
1.4 Structure, Scope, and Methodological Approach of the Paper
The remainder of this paper is structured to systematically develop and substantiate this argument.
Section Two provides a critical review of the theoretical foundations underpinning digital
transformation, deconstructing the “Paradigm of Rational Mastery” to expose the theoretical
chasm created by Gen-AI. Section Three presents a detailed articulation of the reconceptualized
“Dynamic-Adaptive Model”, culminating in a series of testable research propositions. Section
Four discusses the substantial theoretical and managerial implications derived from the model,
offering novel insights into competitive advantage and organizational design. Finally, Section Five
concludes by summarizingthe paper’s contributions, acknowledging itsdeliberate boundaries,and
proposing a clear research agenda for future investigation.
This manuscript is positioned as a theory-building conceptual paper, and its theoretical boundaries
are deliberately defined to ensure analytical clarity. First, the paper’s analytical focus is explicitly
restricted to technologies like Gen-AI that induce “Foundational Uncertainty” through their
emergent and opaque characteristics. Other, more predictable forms of digital technology may still

II. Theoretical Foundations
2.1 Conceptualizing the Paradigm of Rational Mastery: An Analytical Framework
2.1.1 The Need for a Conceptual Baseline
To clearly articulate the paradigm shift instigated by Gen-AI, it is first necessary to construct an
analytical framework that captures the core logic of digital transformation in the pre-generative
era. The profound nature of the current technological rupture can only be fully appreciated when
contrastedwiththeestablishedsetofassumptionsitsupplants.This subsection,therefore,develops
a conceptual tool designed to distill and problematize the foundational beliefs about technological
predictability and managerial control that have long governed enterprise strategy.
Drawing upon the foundational logic of the RBV of the firm and the rationalist principles
undergirding established technology adoption models, this paper proposes the “Two-Layer
Framework of Rational Mastery”. This framework is not intended as a definitive, universal theory
of transformation. Rather, it serves as a purpose-built heuristic device. Its function is to render
explicit the implicit worldview of a preceding era—a worldview in which technology was
perceived as a knowable and manageable tool, and transformation was executed as a rationally
planned project. By systematically constructing this baseline, the framework provides a structured
lens through which the radical discontinuities introduced by Gen-AI can be rigorously analyzed.
2.1.2 The Enterprise Layer: A Resource-Based Logic of Value Creation
The top layer of the framework models the internal causal chain of value creation within the firm,
grounding its logic firmly in the canonical literature of strategic management. This ensures its
theoretical robustness and alignment with established principles of competitive advantage.
The logic of this layer originates with Strategic Resources, which form the bedrock of a firm’s
competitive potential. Following the seminal work of the RBV, firm resources are defined as “all
assets, capabilities, organizational processes, firm attributes, information, knowledge, etc.

controlled by a firm that enable the firm to conceive of and implement strategies that improve its

definition is expanded to explicitly include non-traditional assets such as proprietary data,
sophisticated algorithms, and scalable digital platforms.
However, the application of classical RBV to digital assets reveals an early point of tension. Core
digital resources like data and algorithms may not always satisfy the traditional VRIN/VRIO
criteria that are thought to confer sustained competitive advantage. Data can be replicated, and
algorithms can be reverse-engineered or surpassed, challenging the assumptions of inimitability
and scarcity that underpin much of the theory. Nevertheless, within the paradigm of Rational
Mastery, these assets are still treated as the fundamental inputs to the strategic process.
Possession of strategic resources, whether digital or traditional, is a necessary but insufficient
conditionforsuccess.Theframework positsanintermediary stage—theTransformationProcess—
that represents the active, managerial bridge between resource endowment and value realization.
This process encompasses the orchestration, coordination, and strategic application of resources,
converting their latent potential into tangible market performance.
This conceptualization of the Transformation Process can be understood as the dynamic
operationalization of the “Organization” criterion within Barney’s VRIO framework (Barney,
1995; Barney & Hesterly, 2008). A firm may possess valuable, rare, and costly-to-imitate
resources, but without the organizational capacity to effectively deploy and exploit them, no
competitive advantage is achieved. The Transformation Process embodies this crucial
organizational capability; it is the engine of strategic implementation that determines whether a
firm’s resource portfolio translates into superior performance. It reflects the deliberate managerial
agency required to convert static assets into dynamic competitive actions.
The logicaloutcome of a successfulTransformationProcessisCompetitive Advantage.Consistent
with the RBV, this is defined as the state achieved when a firm implements a “value creating
strategy not simultaneously being implemented by any current or potential competitors”. This
completes the causal sequence of the enterprise layer: a firm leverages its unique bundle of
strategic resources, activates them through a deliberate transformation process, and thereby
achieves a defensible and advantageous position in the marketplace.
2.1.3 The Technology Layer: A Rationalist Logic of Capability Development
The bottom layer of the framework models the process by which raw, external technology is
assessed, internalized, and converted into a productive organizational asset. The logic governing
this layer is implicitly rationalist, reflecting the dominant managerial mindset of an era focused on
control and predictability.

The process begins with Digital Technology itself—innovations such as cloud computing, big data
analytics, or early forms of AI. At this initial stage, the technology exists as a latent force, external
to the firm. It holds significant potential but, in its raw form, generates no inherent organizational
value. It is an object of analysis, a set of possibilities to be evaluated by the organization.
The realization of this potential occurs through Technology Enablement. This is the critical stage
of managerial intervention, defined as the active and deliberate process of translating abstract
technological possibilities into concrete, practical applications that align with specific business
scenarios and strategic objectives. This stage is characterized by a high degree of managerial
intentionality, planning, and control over the technological integration process.
The underlying logic of Technology Enablement serves as a managerial-level analogue to the
individual-level decision-making processes described in influential models like the Technology
Acceptance Model (TAM) and the Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology
(UTAUT). At the individual level, TAM posits that a user’s intention to adopt a technology is
determined by their assessment of its “Perceived Usefulness” (PU) and “Perceived Ease-of-Use”
(PEOU) (Davis, 1989). Similarly, UTAUT introduces concepts like “Performance Expectancy”
and “Effort Expectancy” to explain adoption behavior (Venkatesh et al., 2003).
Within the paradigm of Rational Mastery, managers conduct a similar rational calculus at the
organizational scale. They evaluate a new technology’s potential to enhance firm performance (its
organizational PU or Performance Expectancy) against the anticipated costs, complexities, and
risks associated with its implementation (its organizational PEOU or Effort Expectancy). This
structured, rational evaluation of a technology’s costs and benefits precedes the strategic decision
to adopt and integrate it into the firm’s operations.
The successful outcome of the enablement process is the formation of a durable Technology
Capability. This represents the firm’s embedded and institutionalized ability to apply a specific
technology to solve business problems, create value, and drive innovation. Once developed, this
capability ceases to be an external object and becomes an internal asset, ready to be integrated into
the firm’s portfolio of strategic resources, thus linking the technology layer back to the enterprise
layer (Bharadwaj, 2000).
2.1.4 The Nexus of Transformation: The Trigger of Predictable Convergence
The framework posits that the enterprise and technology layers often operate on distinct, parallel
tracks. Technology evolves according to its own internal logic, often described as a “ladder-like”
progression of incremental improvements, largely independent of the specific, immediate needs of
any single enterprise. Concurrently, firms focus on solving practical business problems using their
existing capabilities.

2.1.5 The Paradigm’s Achilles’ Heel: The Assumption of Knowability
The Two-Layer Framework of Rational Mastery provides a coherent, structured, and logical
representation of digital transformation as it was widely understood before the current
technological shift. Its primary analytical contribution lies in its ability to codify this paradigm,
making its internal logic explicit. The very act of ordering the transformation process into distinct
layers, linear causal chains, and a discrete trigger point underscores the paradigm’s foundational
reliance on order, rationality, and predictability.
However, the framework’s internal coherence in a predictable world is also the source of its
fragility in an unpredictable one. The entire logical structure—from resources to advantage, and
from technology to capability—is predicated on a world in which technology, though complex, is
ultimately knowable, manageable, and can be mastered through deliberate strategic processes.
Digital transformation is thus framed as an act of rational mastery.
Theparadigm’scriticalvulnerability—itsAchilles’heel—istheimplicitassumptionthatmanagers
possess, or can acquire, sufficient and symmetrical information about a technology’s capabilities,
costs, and developmental trajectory to make a rational “go/no-go” decision at the moment of
convergence.Thisassumptionofknowabilityisthelynchpinholdingtheentirerationaliststructure
together.Itpresupposesthattheobjectofanalysis—thetechnology—isstableenoughtobestudied,
understood, and then implemented in a controlled manner. It is precisely this foundational
assumption of knowability that the advent of Gen-AI does not merely challenge, but decisively
invalidates.

Moore's Law), which allowed for structured technology roadmapping and strategic planning. In
stark contrast, Gen-AI introduces a novel strategicmeta-condition: Foundational Uncertainty.This
isnotmerelyanincreaseinmarketorcompetitivevolatilitybutanintrinsic, irreducibleuncertainty
rooted in the technology’s core properties. This uncertainty can be deconstructed into three
interconnected and self-reinforcing dimensions that collectively redefine the strategic landscape

Dimension 1: Radical Unpredictability from Emergent Capabilities
The primary driver of this new uncertainty is the phenomenon of “emergent capabilities”—
abilities that are absent in smaller models but appear suddenly and non-linearly once a model
surpasses an indeterminate scale threshold (Wei et al., 2022). Documented examples include
sophisticated multi-step reasoning, the generation of functional computer code, and even
multilingual translation in languages for which the model received minimal explicit training data.
These capabilities are not the result of direct programming but appear to arise spontaneously from
the scaling process itself.
A significant academic debate interrogates the nature of this emergence. One perspective posits it
as a genuine, unpredictable property of scaling complex systems, akin to phase transitions in
physics. A compelling counter-argument, however, suggests that emergence may be a “mirage”
created by the use of nonlinear or discontinuous evaluation metrics (Schaeffer et al., 2023).
According to this view, underlying capabilities may improve smoothly and predictably with scale,
but performance on specific, complex tasks only crosses a threshold of practical utility abruptly.
Further nuancing this debate is the observation that many advanced behaviors are not purely
emergent but are, to some degree, intentionally engineered into the model’s design. This
engineering is accomplished through the architectural choices and training objectives that create
an “inductive bias”—a predisposition for the model to learn in certain ways (Vaswani et al., 2017).
However, the act of engineering intent does not eliminate unpredictability; it merely reframes it.
While the goal to instill a certain capability is explicit, the ultimate efficacy and behavior of that
capability at unprecedented scale remain fundamentally uncertain. The relationship between
architectural design and specific downstream performance is not fully predictable a priori (Tay et
al., 2022). An engineered behavior may only become practically effective after crossing an
unknown scale threshold, exhibiting the same abrupt, seemingly emergent performance jump that
complicates forecasting.
For the purposes of strategic management, the final resolution of these scientific debates is
secondary. Whether a genuine property of scaling, a metric-induced artifact, or the surprisingly
effective result of an engineered bias, the practical outcome for firms is identical: the sudden,
unforeseeable arrival of powerful new technological capabilities. This radical unpredictability

Dimension 2: The Inversion of Scientific Method—An Experience-First Paradigm
Gen-AI development is characterized by a profound inversion of the traditional scientific and
engineering paradigm, in which theoretical understanding precedes practical application. In the
current landscape, empirical advancements—the creation of remarkably capable systems—have
dramatically outpaced foundational theory. Organizations are now deploying models without a
complete, first-principles explanation for their internal reasoning mechanisms or the precise
pathways through which they generate outputs.
This “experience-leads-theory” dynamic compels organizations into an exploratory, discovery-
driven mode of innovation that is fundamentally experimental. The deployment of a Gen-AI
system is no longer the final step of a planned development process but is instead an experiment
in itself. This reality effectively transforms the organization into an uncontrolled, live laboratory,
where employees become de facto researchers and core business processes become experimental
testbeds. Consequently, innovation strategy must shift away from predictive modeling and toward
the management of a portfolio of real-world experiments, emphasizing rapid learning, adaptation,
and the containment of inevitable failures.
Dimension 3: The Governance Challenge of Algorithmic Opacity
The third dimension of Foundational Uncertainty is the inherent opacity of Gen-AI models,
commonly referred to as the “black-box” problem (Burrell, 2016). Due to their immense
complexity—with reasoning encoded across billions or even trillions of unintelligible numerical
parameters—even their creators cannot fully trace or explain the specific reasoning path from a
given input to a corresponding output. This technical inscrutability is often compounded by the
proprietary nature of leading foundation models, which adds a layer of commercial secrecy.
This algorithmic opacity poses a profound challenge to the fundamental pillars of corporate
governance, accountability, and trust (Rahwan et al., 2019). It severely complicates root-cause
analysis for errors or harmful outputs, makes the assignment of legal liability ambiguous, and
undermines efforts to ensure compliance with regulatory and ethical standards. When a Gen-AI
system produces a biased, factually incorrect, or otherwise damaging output, the inability to
provide a clear, auditable explanation for that failure erodes trust among all stakeholders—

intentionally engineered behaviors.
The inherent opacity of these newly discovered contextual applications and behaviors then feeds
back into the cycle. While a firm may uncover a valuable new use case, its inability to fully explain
the model’s underlying reasoning for that use case deepens the technological mystery and
necessitates further rounds of experimentation to establish trust and ensure governance. This
dynamic fundamentally alters the relationship between the firm and technology. In prior
technological paradigms, the firm was positioned as a proactive agent deploying technology as a
largely predictable tool to execute a predetermined strategy. Under the condition of Foundational
Uncertainty, however, technology increasingly acts as an unpredictable agent whose capabilities
are co-created at the point of use, continuously shaping and reshaping the strategic landscape. This
compels the firm into a perpetually reactive and adaptive posture. Navigating this new reality,
where strategy is less about planning a path and more about building the capacity to respond to a
constantly shifting terrain, is the central strategic challenge of the Gen-AI era (Agrawal et al.,
2022).

This subsection transitions from the preceding descriptive analysis to a structured, critical

the established Two-Layer Model—a framework anchored in an ontology of rational mastery—
with the defining characteristics of the Gen-AI paradigm, which introduces an ontology of

Tension 1: Predictable Convergence vs. Radical Unpredictability
The Two-Layer Model, and the strategic thinking it represents, rests on the premise that digital
transformation is initiated at a moment of predictable convergence. This view presumes that
organizations can adopt a posture of strategic patience, waiting for technology capabilities to
become sufficiently understood, mature, and stable before rationally deploying them to solve well-
defined business challenges. Implicit in this logic is the assumption that technological
advancement follows a manageable, somewhat linear trajectory, allowing for ex-ante analysis and
planned adoption.
However, the advent of the Gen-AI paradigm thoroughly disrupts this foundational assumption.
The defining feature of this new technological wave is not its predictable evolution but its radical
unpredictability. Emergent capabilities—novel functionalities not explicitly programmed by their
creators—arise abruptly and non-linearly, defying traditional forecasting or strategic planning
models. The very nature of the technology has undergone an ontological shift; it is no longer
merely a complicated tool, which is knowable if complex, but a complex adaptive system, which
is inherently unpredictable. Consequently, the strategic calculus for organizations is inverted.
Firms can no longer afford to wait for technological maturity, as critical, game-changing
capabilities may emerge suddenly and be leveraged first by more agile competitors. This
invalidates a strategy of planned deployment, demanding a fundamental shift in the firm’s
technological interaction model from one of planned enablement to one of continuous learning
(Jarrahi, 2018).
Tension 2: Planned Transformation vs. Emergent Evolution
The traditional paradigm of digital transformation emphasizes Technology Enablement as a
deliberate, controlled, and largely linear process of deploying a known technology to achieve
clearly defined outcomes. In this mechanistic view, managers assume they can systematically
design and implement integration processes to extract predictable value, much like the deployment

This resource-centric logic is fundamentally undermined by the economics and architecture of the
Gen-AI paradigm. Foundational uncertainty erodes the strategic value of static assets, as the most
powerful technological resources, namely the foundational models themselves, are often
universally accessible through APIs (Cockburn et al., 2019). Likewise, the vast, generalized
datasets used for their initial training are increasingly becoming commoditized. In this new
environment, competitive advantage migrates from stocks (what a firm possesses) to flows (what
a firm does). The source of value is no longer the possession of a proprietary asset but the
organizational velocity of learning and adaptation. The key differentiator becomes how effectively
and rapidly a firm can leverage these common resources to learn, experiment, and create value
within its specific context. This represents a critical theoretical shift from viewing advantage as
stemming from a dynamic capability—which can still be misconstrued as a static organizational
attribute—to seeing it as residing in a dynamic process that is inherently performative and
continuous. This shift redefines the locus of competitive advantage, moving it from what a firm
has—a portfolio of static VRIN resources—to what a firm does—its engagement in a dynamic,
iterative process of sensing, seizing, and reconfiguring.

2.4 Moving Forward: The Imperative for a Reconceptualized Model
The Two-Layer Model, while a significant theoretical advancement for its time, is predicated upon
an ontology characterized by predictability, control, and rational managerial oversight. It was
designed for a world where technology, however advanced, was ultimately a knowable and
manageable variable in the strategic equation. In stark contrast, the Gen-AI paradigm embodies an
ontology defined by unpredictability, opacity, and the necessity of continuous exploration. This
fundamental ontological divergence between the established theoretical framework and the
emerging technological reality generates a notable theoretical gap.
Existing models of digital transformation—crafted for contexts of manageable and predictable
technological evolution—are now demonstrably inadequate for explaining contemporary

respond to the three theoretical tensions identified above.

epistemological crisis for strategic management, dismantling the foundational assumptions of
technological predictability and control that have long guided firms (Arend, 2024; Knight, 1921;
Townsend et al., 2018). This crisis exposes the critical limitations of the prevailing theoretical
framework—termed here the “Paradigm of Rational Mastery”—which conceptualizes strategy as
a deliberate, top-down process of applying known technologies to solve predefined problems. This
chapter deconstructs this outdated paradigm and, in its place, constructs a new theoretical

3.1 Reconceptualizing the Technology Layer: From Planned Enablement to Adaptive
Sensemaking
Under the condition of Foundational Uncertainty, the firm’s fundamental relationship with
technology undergoes a profound transformation. The interaction shifts from the controlled
applicationofaknowntooltothecontinuousinterpretationanddiscovery ofanunknown,dynamic
entity. This shift necessitates a reconceptualization of the core process within the technology
layer—transitioning from a logic of planned implementation to a new logic of adaptive
sensemaking.
The traditional concept of “Technology Enablement” rests on the assumption that the capabilities
of a technology are largely understood prior to deployment. Under this model, the primary
challenge is one of integration—fitting a known solution to a known problem. However, Gen-AI
renders this logic obsolete. Its inherent opacity, often referred to as the “black-box” nature, means
even its creators cannot fully explain its decision-making pathways,while its emergent capabilities
make future performance impossible to forecast based on current understanding. A firm, therefore,
cannot rationally plan for a technology whose potential and risks cannot be known a priori. The
only logical response to this epistemological shift is to reconceptualize the technological process
as one of Adaptive Learning and Experimentation, defined as a continuous, iterative cycle aimed
at actively probing, discovering, and making sense of the capabilities, limitations, and emergent
behaviors of an opaque technological system.

adaptive posture. The causal arrowflips: insteadofthe firmdeliberatelyimplementingtechnology,

4.1.3 Re-anchoring the RBV: From Static Possession to the Dynamic Process of Adaptation
The third theoretical contribution of the Dynamic-Adaptive Model lies in its critical update to the
RBV of the firm, re-anchoring the theory’s relevance in hyper-turbulent environments by shifting
the locus of sustainable competitive advantage from the possession of strategic resources to the
organizational process of adaptation itself. The traditional RBV, with its emphasis on resources
that are VRIN, faces significant limitations in the Gen-AI context. Foundational digital resources,
such as access to powerful generative models via APIs or scalable cloud infrastructure, are
increasingly abundant and are no longer inherently rare or inimitable (Cockburn et al., 2019).
Furthermore, the RBV has been criticized as a relatively static theory (Kraaijenbrink et al., 2010),
struggling to explain how advantage is built and maintained in highly dynamic environments
where technology continually reshapes the value of other resources.
The Dynamic-Adaptive Model directly addresses these limitations by inverting the traditional
causal logic of the RBV. The classic RBV posits a causal chain flowing from the possession of

VRIN resources to the development of firm capabilities, which in turn leads to sustainable

The model proposes an alternative causal logic: a firm’s VRIN meta-capability (the DAC) enables

technological resources. This process results in sustained adaptive fitness, which manifests as a
recurring stream of temporary competitive advantages.
In this updated logic, the “Dynamic Adaptive Capability” becomes the primary, enduring strategic
asset that meets the VRIN criteria. The organizational process of adaptation itself is the ultimate
resource. It is valuable because it enables the firm to navigate Foundational Uncertainty and create
value from commoditized technologies. It is rare because most firms remain trapped in the
outdated Paradigm of Rational Mastery, struggling to build the necessary culture of
experimentation and learning. It is inimitable because it is a socially complex, path-dependent, and
causally ambiguous process deeply embedded in a firm’s culture and routines; a competitor cannot
simply copy an organization’s unique way of managing the generative tension between top-down
control and bottom-up learning. Finally, it is non-substitutable because in an environment of
endemic unknowability, there is no simple strategic substitute for the ability to learn and adapt.
This reframing allows the core principles of the RBV to remain highly relevant, but it re-applies
them at a higher level of abstraction—from a static “thing” to a dynamic “process”.
This shift implies a fundamental redefinition of the nature of competitive advantage itself. The
traditional strategic goal of building a single, sustained competitive advantage—a durable fortress
of superiority—is replaced. The model suggests that any specific advantage derived from a
particular technology configuration is likely to be transient. Therefore, the ultimate objective is
not to maintain a sustained state but to generate a continuous flow. The firm’s long-term success
is measured not by the durability of one fortress, but by its ability to create an ongoing stream of
transient advantages, consistently positioning itself to win. The strategic focus shifts from building
a durable advantage to mastering rapid mobilization and redeployment, achieving what can be
termed “Sustained Adaptive Fitness”. This is the organizational capacity to consistently thrive in
a changing environment, not by being unbeatable at one point in time, but by being consistently
better at adapting over time. This reframing provides a more realistic and actionable strategic
objective, making the RBV profoundly relevant for the age of Gen-AI.

4.2.1 From Grand Planning to Experimentalism: The Imperative of Adaptive Learning
This first recommendation is a direct prescription drawn from the “Adaptive Learning &
Experimentation” mechanism at the core of the Dynamic-Adaptive Model. In an environment
marked by Foundational Uncertainty, where the potential applications and impacts of new
technologies are unknown and emergent, learning cannot occur through abstract analysis and
prediction alone; it requires direct action, feedback, and iteration. As the model argues, the
traditional planning-action sequence is inverted, necessitating an “Experience-Leads-Theory
Paradigm” where firms must first act to generate the data from which they can retrospectively
understand their environment. Therefore, managers must shift resource allocation away from
large-scale, multi-year IT implementation projects—an approach rooted in the Paradigm of
Rational Mastery that is dangerously ill-suited for a landscape where underlying technology may
become obsolete mid-project—and adopt a portfolio-based approach to innovation.
This involves reallocating significant resources to fund numerous small, agile, cross-functional
teams focused on continuous, low-cost experimentation. The primary goal of this experimentalist
approach is not immediate ROI, but rather to maximize organizational learning and deepen its tacit
knowledge of the emerging technological paradigm. This practice is already being adopted in
dynamic sectors, with companies establishing internal “AI playgrounds” for safe exploration,
hosting “AI Jams” to encourage creative, bottom-up use cases, and systematically validating AI
platforms through a multitude of targeted, time-boxed experiments.
This shift toward experimentalism, however, creates a significant internal challenge by colliding
directlywithestablishedsystemsofcorporategovernance.Traditionalcapitalbudgetingprocesses,
such as stage-gate reviews or Net Present Value (NPV) analysis, are designed to minimize
uncertainty and select projects with the most predictable positive returns (Trigeorgis & Reuer,
2017). They are structurally incompatible with a learning-first approach where outcomes are
inherently unpredictable. Implementing this recommendation, therefore, is not merely about
encouraging teams to experiment; it requires senior leaders to fundamentally redesign the firm’s
financialandstrategiccontrolsystemstolegitimize“learning”asavalid,fundableprojectoutcome,
even without a clear short-term ROI. This creates a new burden on middle managers, who become
caught in the cross-currents between top-down pressure for predictable results and the bottom-up
need for open-ended exploration. Their role must evolve from “project manager” to “portfolio
manager of learning”—a micro-level manifestation of the very “Contention & Adaptation” cycle
that the model posits at the macro-organizational level.

4.2.2 From Map to Compass: The Practice of Adaptive Strategic Management
This recommendation represents the practical application of the model’s concept of an
“Evolutionary Process” guided by “Adaptive Strategic Management”. In an era of Foundational
Uncertainty, the landscape is constantly shifting, rendering any detailed strategic map obsolete
almost as soon as it is created. A compass, in contrast, provides consistent direction while allowing
the flexibility to navigate unforeseen obstacles and opportunities. The model posits that strategy
must “co-evolve with technological learning through emergent, iterative adjustments”, a process
for which a fixed map is useless. Leaders must, therefore, redefine the purpose and structure of
strategy.
Strategy should no longer be framed as a fixed five-year roadmap, but as a combination of a clear
and enduring strategic intent (the “compass direction”, e.g., “to become the most AI-augmented
consultancy in our industry”) and a dynamic portfolio of strategic options and initiatives that can
be activated or abandoned as learning accrues. The primary goal of the strategic process must shift
from achieving predictive accuracy to maximizing organizational agility and the capacity for
learning. This approach closely aligns with the principles of agile roadmapping, which prioritize
“outcomes over outputs” and employ flexible time horizons, such as “Now, Next, and Later”,
rather than rigid, date-driven deadlines. This structure provides directional clarity without
compromising adaptability, ensuring that daily work remains aligned with the long-term vision
while preventing the organization from becoming locked into an outdated plan.
Adopting a “compass” strategy requires leaders to develop a higher tolerance for ambiguity and to
communicate with a new kind of clarity. The traditional “map” approach provides a sense of
certainty and control, which is psychologically comforting for stakeholders. The “compass”
approach, by contrast, explicitly acknowledges uncertainty.This creates a difficult communication
paradox for leadership. They can no longer project an aura of omniscient certainty about the path
forward. Instead, they must project confidence in the organization’s ability to learn and adapt. The
source of their credibility shifts from “I have the right plan” to “We have the right process for
finding the plan”. This has profound implications for external stakeholders, particularly investors
and boards of directors, who are often conditioned to expect detailed five-year plans and
predictable earnings. Leaders must undertake the difficult work of educating these stakeholders,
reframing the strategic conversation from “meeting the plan” to “improving the quality of the
organizational learning engine”.

5.1 Conclusion: From a Paradigm of Rational Mastery to One of Dynamic Adaptation
This paper has embarked on an intellectual journey to address a fundamental schism between
established models of enterprise digital transformation and the new reality ushered in by Gen-AI.
The emergence of this technology class represents not merely an incremental advance, but a
paradigm shift—one that has precipitated an epistemological crisis for strategic management. It
confronts firms with a novel and profound challenge that destabilizes the very foundations upon
which strategic knowledge and decision-making have traditionally been built.
The core problem motivating this inquiry is the inadequacy of existing strategic frameworks in the
face of what this paper terms “Foundational Uncertainty”. This concept was introduced to
distinguishthe currentenvironment fromtraditional notionsofmarket or technologicaluncertainty,
which, though challenging, were often amenable to probabilistic forecasting and linear planning.

This ontological rupture invalidates the predictive, execution-focused “Paradigm of Rational

The age of Gen-AI compels a fundamental shift in strategic thinking—from the rational mastery

pragmatic guide for management practice, ultimately helping scholars and leaders answer the

limitations discussed here are not intended as apologies for oversight but rather as deliberate,

theoretically grounded decisions that clarify the paper’s contribution while providing a foundation

First and foremost, the Dynamic-Adaptive Model is a specific theory constructed for contexts
characterized by “Foundational Uncertainty”. Therefore, the model’s explanatory power and
applicability are highest when a new technology simultaneously exhibits high “operational opacity”
and a significant potential for non-linear, “emergent capabilities”. While this paper develops the
model in the context of Gen-AI, its logic may be equally applicable to other future technological
waves with similar characteristics, such as the advent of Artificial General Intelligence (AGI),
quantum computing, or frontier biotechnology. Conversely, for digital technologies with more
predictable and linear evolutionary paths—such as cloud computing or standard data analytics—
the traditional “Paradigm of Rational Mastery” and its associated strategic planning models may
remain a more parsimonious and effective explanatory framework. Future research should
empirically test these boundary conditions.
This delineation reveals a more complex reality for large, diversified enterprises. Such firms do
notfaceasingletechnologicalenvironmentbutaportfolioofthem.They maysimultaneouslyneed
to manage stable, predictable technologies (like their ERP systems) with rational, plan-driven
methods while engaging with frontier technologies (like Gen-AI) through dynamic, adaptive
processes. This creates a profound internal challenge of operating with multiple, often
contradictory, strategic paradigms at once. The cultures, incentive systems, and capital budgeting
processes required for Rational Mastery are often antithetical to those required for Dynamic
Adaptation. This implies the existence of a yet-unexplored meta-capability: the ability of a firm to
become “paradigm-ambidextrous”,managingtheinternal tensions thatarise fromthese conflicting
logics.
Second, the model presented here operates at a macro level of analysis, focusing on the firm and
its primary organizational layers. This is a deliberate theoretical choice, designed to first map the
new firm-level strategic imperatives before delving into the underlying mechanisms. In doing so,
the model intentionally “black boxes” the micro-foundations—the specific cognitive, behavioral,
and social dynamics at the individual and team levels that enable the macro-level phenomena of
Contention and Adaptation. While the model explains what must occur at the organizational level,
it does not provide detailed specifications on how individual managers and teams enact these
complex processes. This deliberate scoping decision, far from being a limitation, connects directly
to the enduring “micro-macro link” problem in organizational theory and explicitly sets the stage
for the most promising avenues for future research.

Research Avenue 1: Empirical Validation and Process Observation
In direct response to the conceptual nature of this study, the most immediate priority is empirical
research to test the propositions of the Dynamic-Adaptive Model. This undertaking is best
approached through a phased research program that begins with rich qualitative exploration and
progresses to large-scale quantitative testing.
The first phase should consist of in-depth, longitudinal case studies of firms actively navigating
the integration of Gen-AI. Such qualitative work is essential to provide a “thick description” of
how the “Contention & Adaptation” cycle manifests in real-world artifacts and processes, such as
strategic review documents, resource allocation debates, and team meetings. This research would
not only serve to validate the model’s core constructs but also to refine and enrich them with
empirical detail, observing the nuanced ways in which generative tension is managed or
mismanaged in practice.
Building on the rich insights from this qualitative phase, the second phase would involve
developing and validating robust survey-based scales to operationalize the model’s core constructs,
particularly the “Dynamic Adaptive Capability”. Once validated, these scales could be deployed
in large-sample quantitative studies to formally test the model’s propositions. Such research could
examine the statistical relationships between a firm’s DAC and key dependent variables such as
firmperformance, innovation output,and overalladaptive fitness,therebyestablishingthe model’s
