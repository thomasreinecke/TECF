Abstract
Since its inception, digital government has been a struggle
between transformational aspirations and contested re-
forms grounded within a traditionalist governance ethos.
Following a conceptual situating of such tensions, we
focus on three interrelated thematic sets of digital gov-
ernance reforms: i) organizational governance and en-
terprise architecture; ii) COVID‐19 and hybrid work ar-
rangements; and iii) the escalating risks and complexities
of cybersecurity. This article argues that the pervasiveness
of traditional Westminster principles—notably informa-
tion secrecy and hierarchical control—has shackled the
emergence of an alternative governance ethos more
aligned with digital innovation and systemic openness. In
order to forge the latter, three critical enablers of systemic
transformation must be embraced: first, a more collabo-
rative and open political mindset; second, an alternative
governance architecture championed by a new organiza-
tional entity; and third, the forging of a more diverse and
empowered public service to strengthen digital govern-
ance adaptation and anticipatory capacities.

INTRODUCTION
Since its inception, digital government (formerly known as e‐government) has long been
shaped by soaring rhetoric on the one hand, and the tough slogging of contested and incre-
mental reforms on the other hand. Conceptually, tensions between these two vantage points
have been underpinned by an ethos of Traditional Public Administration—often depicted as
the machinery of government—and more digitally‐laden prisms of public sector governance
reforms, notably Digital Era Governance and Public Value Management. Along this contin-
uum, one cannot discard New Public Management, given the growing role of the private sector
in devising digital infrastructure and solutions, and advising and working with and within
governments.
After seeking to better explain these conceptual parameters and situate them within the
Canadian context, this article will focus on three interrelated thematic sets of digital govern-
ance investments and reforms: i) organizational governance and enterprise architecture and the
orchestrating role of central agencies in particular; ii) COVID‐19 and the dramatic rise and now
significant regression of hybrid work arrangements in public sector operations; and iii) the
escalating risks and complexities of cybersecurity as a holistic governance challenge not only
for the Government of Canada but for the entire country. With respect to mindset, across each
of these three realms this article argues that the pervasiveness of traditional Westminster
principles—notably information secrecy and hierarchical control, have greatly shackled the

In drawing from the lessons of past blockages and ongoing tensions, and in seeking the

performance that are further contextualized in the next section.
BACKGROUND: PRESSURES AND DRIVERS OF REFORM
“Digital government” is a fluid and evolutionary term. Soon after the Internet's mainstream
emergence, early notions of “e‐government” were predicated upon a linear and mainly
incremental extension of the public sector's governance architecture into the Internet era
(Roy 2006, 2008). By contrast, digital government advocates have a more transformative and
dynamic prism driven by a complex alignment of technological change and governance
transformation (Clarke, Lindquist, and Roy 2017; Clarke 2019; Roy 2019; Lindquist 2022).
However, many observers have suggested that it is important to consider if and how digital
government draws upon, disrupts, and also potentially aligns some elements of traditional
public administration (TPA), and competing theories such as new public management (NPM),
public value management (PVM) and new public governance (NPG). Furthermore, we must
add to this conceptual sandbox, digital era governance (DEG), a template that Lindquist and
Huse (2017) rightfully point out could also be layered on any of the prior mentioned theories—
since it incorporates elements of each one as well (Dunleavy et al. 2006). Later, Lindquist (2022)
provided a thoughtful and more rigorous dissecting of DEG and the competing values of all of
these various typologies, explaining how various “cosmologies” of public sector governance
draw from each of them and take shape in ways that reflect a myriad of historical and insti-
tutional factors intertwined with specificities of different jurisdictions.
What is especially notable about DEG is the co‐existing impulses of more centralized and
decentralized‐networked governance (re‐integration, needs based holism, and digitization
processes) that draw from, in different ways from Dunleavy et al. (2006) and the other afore-
mentioned typologies (Clarke 2019; Lindquist 2022). These tensions, in turn, lead to competing
tensions as to whether the GC should be viewed as a “single enterprise” or instead a mosaic of
at least partially unique departmental and agency entities nonetheless underpinned by co-
ordinating and collaborative mechanisms (and thus some element of centralizing authority).
Such tensions are relevant to the analysis below.
Space does not permit any detailed and comparative examination of all these typologies, so
what follows presumes basic familiarity with the main contours of various reform movements,
especially the underlying logic of TPA.1
Most specific to the Government of Canada (GC) is
Clarke's rigorous expose of the invariable and complex tensions between structures and cul-

and mechanisms inhibiting digitally‐driven innovation and wider governance adaptation, sty-
mying the emergence of “digital era open government” (ibid.). This article seeks to update such
an assessment within the context of these varying conceptual currents and rivaling, and more
current pressures for reform.
DISSECTING THREE DIMENSIONS OF RECENT REFORMS
In seeking to better illustrate and explain some of the most important aspects of the GC's
ongoing digital adaptation and struggles, what follows will focus on three interrelated themes
of ongoing digital governance reforms: (1) organizational governance and enterprise archi-
tecture; (2) hybrid workspace models for GC operations; and (3) the complexities of cyberse-
curity governance and the growing importance of AI to cyber‐readiness and digital and data‐
driven governance innovation more widely.
Organizational governance and enterprise architecture
Shaped by the complexities of partially competing digital governance cosmologies and tradi-
tional and more novel paradigms of public sector reforms, the enterprise architecture of digital
government is bound to be multifaceted in terms of actors and agendas and the mindsets and
mechanisms for steering and reconciling such agendas within a common, integrative and agile
and adaptive governance framework. Any such framework will largely be taken as the GC in
terms of scope, even as growing interdependencies within the broader public must also be
recognized.

What is striking about the GC's efforts in this regard is the overarching dominance of the
Treasury Board Secretariat (TBS) in terms of leadership and functionality. For example, even at
the political level, arguably the most prominent federal Minister of Digital Government was
also President of the Treasury Board. The Office of the Chief Information Officer (OCIO) is
likewise housed within TBS, along with the Office of the Chief Human Resource Officer
(check), a combination that would prove vital to organizational responses to COVID‐19 with
respect to hybrid work practices (discussed further below). Similarly, the Open
Government unit is also found within TBS, as was until recently, the Canadian Digital Service
(CDS). There are, of course, important elements of digital governance outside of TBS focused
on internal infrastructure (Shared Services Canada) and external service delivery (Service
Canada). The former is a separate agency with legislative underpinnings, while the latter has
always been something of a fluid organizational mirage (and external brand) within the much
larger department Employment and Social Development Canada (ESDC), which has respon-
sibilities for the vast majority of federal services delivered directly to individuals (Roy 2017).
Yet, led by its CIO Branch, it has always been largely the purview of TBS to articulate digital
government's vision and policies, and to seek to orchestrate mechanisms for government‐wide
planning and coordination.
While defenders of such an arrangement invoke the government‐wide scope of TBS
authority, the main shortcoming is that TBS also personifies the core principles of TPA in terms
of managerial control, notably in terms of financial planning, resource allocation, and spending
accountability. As the main entity responsible for approving and monitoring separate depart-
mental agendas and actions, the central agency fundamentally lacks the capacities for enabling
innovation—particularly through novel forms of intra‐organizational collaboration
(Clarke 2019; Roy 2019; Roy 2022). Ongoing failures and frustrations of TBS‐based federal CIOs
underscore this point (Pamma 2023; May 2023).
A specific example of TBS limitations is Canadian Digital Service (CDS), an entity forged
within TBS meant to be a catalyst for citizen‐centric service design on an integrative scale
across mainly siloed sets of public services. Driven by the creation of a new Minister for Citizen
Services, the 2023 shifting of CDS to ESDC gives rise to the wider question of whether TBS was
really the optimal placement for CDS to begin with. Even as shifting CDS to ESDC from TBS
may be an encouraging step, few would contend that ESDC is a natural catalyst and platform
from which to drive government‐wide digital reforms, given its longstanding struggles with
multi‐channel service delivery and the underwhelming performance of Service Canada in-
itiatives (Roy 2017). Interestingly, shifting CDS to ESDC aligns in some respects with recent
changes announced by the new British Government. But, in contrast to ESDC's predominantly
service purview, the British reforms of its own digital architecture are much more ambitious
and substantive, featuring a consolidation of three key digital government bodies (Government
Digital Service, the Digital and Data Office and the Incubator for AI) within a newly expanded
lead Department (Department for Science, Innovation and Technology) animated by a clear
overarching digital mission. This mission is championed by the Deputy Prime Minister and
supported by a new set of cross‐governmental boards of senior officials to pursue digital pri-
orities (Johnstone 2024).
There are two important caveats to this CDS/TBS critique, both elaborated upon below,
stemming from the COVID‐19 pandemic and persistent and widening cybersecurity challenges.
The global pandemic clearly prioritised urgent service delivery over innovative design, thus
marginalizing CDS's mission to forge newer and more mobile‐friendly service models, and the
expanding array of cybersecurity risks arguably reinforce a control‐laden, defensive mindset to

COVID‐19 and hybrid work arrangements
At the height of the COVID‐19 pandemic in 2020 and much of 2021, many federal public
servants worked predominantly from home for much, if not all, of their work week. To
some degree, as COVID‐19 has receded, hybrid workplace models have partially endured. In
contrast to pre‐COVID‐19, there are today more degrees of flexibility for remote work design—
and even some open positions for recruitment offer locationally flexible (the latter increasingly
essential to specific skill sets tied to emerging technologies as well as non‐technical but equally
essential skills in fields such as creative design). The GC's Digital Academy, furthermore, has
offered a learning series of events devoted to hybrid work models, and the Office of Chief
Human Resources Officer has conveyed its support for extending hybrid workspace solutions
into a post‐COVID‐19 future. However, there are four important limitations.
First, despite some flexibility afforded to departments and agencies, the heavy handedness
of TBS in announcing an across‐the‐board return‐to‐office mandate of three days per week in
2024 has created the perception of prioritizing a reversion to pre‐COVID‐19, in‐person work-
place traditionalism (albeit with mixed logics as to why). Such top‐down ordainment has
created antagonism with public sector unions, especially given that a greatly expanded federal
public service (according to TBS, 357,247 public servants in 2023, up from 257,034 in 2015) is in
many ways incapable of housing all of its staff on an in‐person basis, and giving managers little
time to forge feasible transitions organizationally and technologically. There are common ex-
amples of public servants commuting to office buildings only to mainly partake in virtual
meetings from awkwardly devised work settings. Moreover, the federal government has itself
championed lessened physical office space as one response to both climate change and a
housing shortage. Related research from Carleton University further strengthens the case for
hybrid and remote work arrangements as a driver of carbon emissions reduction, a notable
variable given the federal government's own pre‐COVID‐19 commitment to flexible work
options as part of its “greener government” strategy (Roy 2022; Mae Jones 2024).
Second, while some limited degrees of flexibility remain and may well endure, what is
arguably even more telling is the emphasis on senior leadership to function predominantly on
an in‐person basis more so than middle level managers and front‐line workers. Indeed,
alongside TBS pronouncements in this regard, one finds the Privy Council Office (PCO) shifting
from being a champion of hybrid arrangements (both for itself and the wider public service)
(May 2022). Instead, PCO has more recently sought to emphasize the importance of in‐person
gathering as an enabler of strong public sector performance. Not only is such logic contested in
many respects (i.e., PCO's lack of empirical data to justify such conclusions), this sought‐after
reversion to traditionalism greatly subordinates virtual governance adaptation—and thus the
sorts of systemic changes sought by advocates of deeper digital reforms (more aligned with
elements of PVM and DEG as discussed in the prior section).
Third, lessening hybrid's importance also threatens to compromise progress on diversity
and inclusion: a reversion to traditional workplace attendance for senior leadership denies

Cybersecurity and Artificial Intelligence
Since 2023, the Canadian Centre for Cybersecurity (CCCS: a federal entity within the Public
Safety portfolio) has sought to shed light on the growing cybersecurity epidemic, labelling
cybersecurity a matter of national security already putting lives at risk. For government, the
dauntingly unprecedented and two‐fold cybersecurity challenge is to orchestrate collective
readiness for the country as a whole in concert with a variety of stakeholders, while ensuring
the stability and resilience of public sector operations (Roy 2024; TAG 2024). In the latter
realm, the GC alone estimates that its networks face more than three billion hostile actions
daily. While most are harmless automations, just one breach can bring a cascade of data,
financial, and public safety risks.
Cybersecurity is often under‐appreciated by a political class largely unaware or disin-
terested. At the federal level, even as the CCCS report underscores the urgent need for national
action, the absence of a senior Office devoted to cybersecurity is a stark contrast to Canada's
closest allies, notably the US and Australia (Roy 2024). In Australia, there is a federal Minister
directly responsible for cybersecurity, along with a National Cyber Security Coordinator (a
Deputy level public servant). This Australian model is particularly important in seeking to
better align federalist and national leadership through formalized agreements for inter‐
jurisdictional cooperation (ibid.).
The absence of a senior leadership architecture devoted to cybersecurity is a major con-
straint for the GC which itself seemingly concurs. A published 2022 midterm review of its own
national cybersecurity strategy (2018–2024) found significant shortcomings: the evaluation
notes that federal actors and actions are disjointed, while externally the forging of collaborative
governance mechanisms has been underwhelming (ibid.). These challenges are becoming more
pervasive as Artificial Intelligence (AI) becomes evermore intertwined with cybersecurity, a
point that the CCCS has emphasized recently as well. A report prepared for Public Safety
Canada by the Transparency Advisory Group further underscores this link—and as its name
implies (created within the context of the Public Safety Canada's own Transparency

TWO OPTIONS ‐ AND THE NECESSARILY PREFERRED
APPROACH
Increasingly, the GC finds itself at a crossroads in terms of its digital capacities and ambitions.
In choosing a path forward, two options present themselves: continuance and adaptation. What
follows argues that the second route will be essential going forward, and that an adaptive
strategy based on this posture should have three pillars.
Continuance vs adaptation: why the latter is needed
This path entails incremental reforms and piecemeal measures that include some well‐intended
reform aspirations, albeit without the necessary overarching political and senior managerial
mindset on the one hand, and refurbished and modernized governance mechanisms on the
other hand. Although insufficient, it does bear noting that many of the necessary dimensions to
digital transformation have been at least articulated by the GC in recent years: the digital
charter, open government action plans, a data governance roadmap, and a new Minister of
Citizen Services are also cases in point. At the time of writing this article, Public Safety Canada
is said to be very close to publishing an updated national cybersecurity strategy to address its
own shortcomings as well as emerging challenges, while in September 2024 the RCMP released
its first detailed blueprint and engagement strategy for its National Technology Onboarding
Program.
Nevertheless, the main constraint with continuance is the absence of a holistic governance
architecture, including political and managerial elements. Such an architecture would stitch
together these elements, align them within a robust and integrative operational infrastructure,
and identify accountability mechanisms genuinely focused on innovation and public value
creation rather than control‐laden safeguards and oversight processes. Accordingly, continu-
ance implies incremental stutter‐steps to specific aspects of GC operations, with a pervasive
sense of laggardness shackling wider experimentation and systemic change. Several short-
comings are surely likely to persist, including: an aging and antiquated digital infrastructure
(an ongoing challenge repeatedly exposed by the federal Auditor General); under‐utilized
platforms of internal data sharing and open data dissemination; minimal service innovation
(especially in the realm of mobile‐friendly platforms); a growing sense of cyber‐insecurity;

Three critical enablers of systemic adaptation
In choosing the path of adaptation, what sort of enablers can lead to systemic digital govern-
ance reforms both culturally and structurally? As a starting point and foundation for genuine
adaptation and digital transformation, three main elements must be embraced: (1) a political
mechanism with meaningful, collaborative and open Ministerial leadership; (2) a new orga-
nizational entity devoted to instilling the GC with a digitally‐infused transformational mindset;
and (3) a commitment to levering digital tools as a basis for public service empowerment and
governance innovation with greater diversity and inclusion playing an instrumental role in
doing so.
(1) A political mechanism with meaningful, collaborative and open Ministerial leadership
The first element is an expanded fusion of prior incarnations of the Digital Minister (a
Cabinet post which has inexplicably disappeared in recent years) and a form of Cabinet
sub‐Committee meant to articulate a government‐wide vision of digital transformation and
oversee specific plans meant to realize such a vision. In contrast to a Committee of Cabinet,
however, any such body must be transparent in its form and functionality—and outwardly
adept in working with a range of external stakeholders across all government levels and
other sectors as well. Even while the President of TBS could be one participant on such a
body, they should be one voice of many with digital aspects to public sector operations and
performance (including internal infrastructure, skills and workforce development, service
innovation, and emerging priorities such as AI).
(2) A new organization to instil GC with a digitally‐infused transformational mindset
To lessen TBS's current dominance of so many aspects of digital governance and man-
agement (stemming from a mindset rooted in primarily traditionalist notions of machinery
of government), an alternative organizational entity must be forged—with separate polit-
ical representation to champion this alternative mindset more in keeping with open and
adaptive governance systems. Fortunately, many such models exist across the OECD world
(close democratic and economic comparators), with one notable example being Australia's
Digital Transformation Agency (DTA). Its mission is to “provide strategic and policy
leadership and expert investment advice and oversight to drive government digital trans-
formation that delivers benefits to all Australians” while its core values (collaboration,
transparency, excellence, future focused, respect) are in many ways a stark contrast to the
core values of TPA‐stylized central agencies. In short, DTA acts as a catalyst for the sort of
digital and openness ethos that is incapable of organically emerging within the confines of
traditional machinery of government.
What matters most here is the hiving off of specific digital leadership and expertise away
from traditional central agencies into an alternative venue without the historical inertia of
TPA. The CDS experiment within TBS was well‐intended but inadequate, much as today's
ongoing and heightening cybersecurity challenges necessitate closer collaboration across
multiple federal government actors, a set of tasks poorly suited to a traditional CIO office

insufficient within the GC, particularly in terms of digital innovation and governance
adaptation (Clarke 2019; Roy 2019).
Additionally, the Australian's DTA invocation of transparency as a core value is a
tremendously important contrast to the secrecy‐laden inertia of traditional central agencies
such as Canada's TBS and its many inherent contradictions among its various many
mandates (open government, access to information, data security etc.)—it is clearly unfair
and unrealistic to expect TBS to be a catalyst for systemic openness. Even as the DTA has
hardly reconciled all such elements within and across the Australian federal government, it
provides an alternative starting point—and a basis for exploring the potential contributions
of systemic openness sought by many reform advocates (Boots and Clarke 2024).
(3) Levering digital tools for public service empowerment and governance innovation
This emphasis on systemic openness (as articulated scholastically by Clarke, Francoli,
Eaves, Millard, Gasco and others) as a basis of a renewed digital mindset is also
becoming especially important with the advent of AI. While the GC has laudably
adopted transparency as a core principle of AI governance development in a general
sense (i.e., its core principles of “responsible AI usage”), there is much about AI,
especially in the national security realm that remains opaque and shielded from public
purview (TAG 2024). As the AI world itself evolves within a contested set of proprietary
and open‐source models, having an alternative government entity with openness as its
cultural and structural starting point (not the case for TBS) is an essential aspect of GC
learning and adaptation—and its devising of new oversight and regulatory capacities on
the one hand, and new internal deployments of AI on the other hand.
With respect to the latter, the strategic linkages to human resource governance reforms and
workforce skills and development are also germane to government's adaptive capacities in
better generating public value through AI‐driven innovation. Accordingly, the important efforts
of the GC's Digital Academy (a modestly resourced entity within the Canada School of Public
Service) could be better leveraged and expanded upon as learning and skills development
become more outward and open‐sourced in many respects as well (Roy et al. 2019). As the GC
has itself recognized—at least in terms of its external partnerships with researchers and fre-
quently in its own pronounced intentions—a more diverse and inclusive workforce is essential
to digital innovation and adaptation (ibid.; Harrington and Cukier 2023; TAG 2024).
Reflecting the rivaling conceptual logics discussed earlier in this article, tensions between
secrecy and openness are certain to shape AI deployments. The risk of AI fostering more
insular forms of technocratic governance stems less from nefarious intentions (that can

accountability to align such flexibility with outcomes) are absolutely essential attributes of
recruitment and retention efforts now and going forward.
CONCLUSION
The Government of Canada has long been reasonably effective at articulating its digital am-
bitions, with many important elements of digital reforms well‐identified. Yet where it has
consistently fallen short lies in an unwillingness to devise and deploy an alternative governance
architecture more aligned with an ethos of digitization. As the Australian example shows, such
an architecture (which in Australia also extends to a national digital health framework of the
sort that advocates in Canada can only view with envy) does not mean fully replacing a
traditional Westminster architecture but rather challenging it. At the same, such an alternative
governance architecture stems first and foremost from a level of political commitment to digital
reform currently on display in Australia, the UK and even to some degree the US (in terms of
its cybersecurity and AI initiatives) that is sorely lacking in Canada (the rather trepid creation
of a new Minister of Citizen Services notwithstanding).
Along with political leadership—and a new organizational entity to nurture such leadership
into systemic governance innovation, there is also a need to seek empowerment of the public
service rather than new means of control. The botched evolution of hybrid arrangements
reflects a fundamental penchant for inward control, risk mitigation and perception manage-
ment (that is inherent within the closed and centralized ethos of traditional public adminis-
tration and its “machinery” of government), rather than a willingness to further enable senior
managers working in starkly different entities to experiment and develop solutions most
appropriate for meeting specific and unique performance mandates and objectives (and for
collaborating with one another to pursue shared and/or integrative objectives). In reflecting key
elements of Clarke's expose of TPA inertia, and the necessarily multi‐faceted prism of DEG
