A B S T R A C T
The integration of Artificial Intelligence (AI) into Enterprise Architecture (EA) offers transformative potential for
advancing Sustainable Innovation Ecosystems (SIEs). However, AI's velocity often clashes with traditional, rigid
governance. Using a sequential mixed-methods design – a systematic literature review followed by interviews
with ten industry experts – this study maps AI's strategic impact across the EA-IE landscape. Our findings reveal
that AI's value is primarily concentrated at the Business and Data layers, where it reconfigures ecosystem actors
and processes. We uncover an EA paradox: while practitioners eschew formal frameworks as too rigid, they rely
on architectural principles as dynamic capabilities to manage structural inertia. Theoretically, we advance from
descriptive findings to causal mechanisms by proposing three testable propositions that explain how organiza­
tions can balance architectural stability with the agility required for AI adoption. Practically, the study offers a
diagnostic tool for aligning AI adoption with organizational resilience. We conclude by outlining a roadmap for
future research that utilizes dynamic multi-agent simulations to explore adaptive governance strategies for AI-
enabled SIEs. This study contributes a localized, empirically grounded perspective on governing AI within
complex adaptive systems, reframing EA as a flexible, human-centric coordination tool for innovation.
1. Introduction
The conceptualization of an Innovation Ecosystem (IE) has matured
into dynamic networks of interconnected actors, resources, and pro­
cesses organized across distinct functional layers to achieve broader
sustainability goals (Granstrand and Holgersson, 2020; Jacobides et al.,
2018; Neto et al., 2024; Oh et al., 2016; Oliveira-Duarte et al., 2021).
Value creation and innovation within these ecosystems are driven by the
horizontal flow of interactions and resource exchange between these
elements (Carrara and Freisinger, 2024). The integration of technolog­
ical advancements, particularly Artificial Intelligence (AI), into inno­
vation processes, combined with the intentional design and cultivation
of IEs, is increasingly recognized as a critical determinant of achieving
desired outcomes (Aldoseri et al., 2024; Huang et al., 2025; Obreja et al.,
2024; Secundo et al., 2025). Contemporary research increasingly em­
phasizes the role of IEs in fostering sustainable entrepreneurship and
development (Bakry et al., 2024, 2025; Bărbulescu et al., 2021; Leitão
et al., 2018). This shift reflects a paradigm change, where sustainability
considerations are integral to ecosystem analysis, leading to the emer­
gence of a term Sustainable IE (SIE) (Liu and Stephens, 2019; Pham and
Vu, 2022; Sultana and Turkina, 2023). A prerequisite for the sustainable
development of these ecosystems is their resilience, which we concep­
tualize not merely as recovery, but as the system's capacity to absorb
shocks, adaptively reorganize, and transform its core functions in
response to emergent shifts in technology or market dynamics (Bakhtiar
et al., 2026; Bakry et al., 2024). In this study, this resilience is assessed
qualitatively through the experts' evaluations of organizational readi­
ness, adaptive governance strategies, and socio-technical capacity.
In contrast to the horizontally oriented, collaborative nature of SIEs,
Enterprise Architecture (EA) offers a distinct, vertically layered
approach. EA, in this context, can be defined as a structured represen­
tation of an organization (or ecosystem) that aligns business strategy
with IT infrastructure, organized into vertical layers, e.g., business,
application, data, technology (Gampfer et al., 2018), to ensure coher­
ence and efficiency (Kotusev et al., 2022). This vertical alignment is
primarily aimed at ensuring internal coherence, operational efficiency,
and the agility required for organizations to adapt their operations
effectively to evolving market dynamics (Hoogervorst, 2004; Tamm
et al., 2022). The necessity for a robust and well-defined EA framework
becomes particularly pronounced when considering the unique

et al., 2021; Nardello et al., 2015; Neto et al., 2024). Drawing upon the
established understanding of EA's role in supporting innovation
(Nardello et al., 2015), its application to SIEs necessitates a specific

AI is now widely acknowledged as playing an indispensable role in
ensuring the long-term vitality and dynamism of SIEs (Chatterjee et al.,
2020; Sultana et al., 2023; Zimmermann et al., 2021). Kamalabai et al.
(2024) emphasize the growing expectation for EA to be central in the
sustainable incorporation of AI systems and in leading organizational
transformation efforts. Consequently, AI's pervasive influence on
contemporary innovation (Huang et al., 2025; Mariani et al., 2023;
Secundo et al., 2025; Sun et al., 2022) and sustainable development
(Goralski and Tan, 2020; Jankovic and Curovic, 2023; Leal Filho et al.,
2023; Sipola et al., 2023) makes its strategic integration within EA and
SIEs crucial. The structure of EA, with its distinct business, application,
data, and technology layers, provides a well-defined pathway for the
structured adoption and scalable implementation of AI-driven processes
across the various interconnected elements of SIEs. While existing
research has explored the significance and multifaceted impacts of AI
and AI-driven processes within the realm of SIEs (Huang et al., 2025;
Secundo et al., 2025; Sultana et al., 2023; Sun et al., 2022; Zimmermann
et al., 2021), and other studies have examined the transformative in­
fluence of AI on EA and its respective frameworks (Afriliana et al., 2022;
Bakar et al., 2024; Chen and Zhao, 2024; Denni-Fiberesima, 2024; Guo
and Gao, 2024; Rittelmeyer and Sandkuhl, 2021), a research gap persists
in the extant literature.
Specifically, there is a lack of comprehensive research that system­
atically merges the horizontal layers inherent to SIEs with the vertical
layers' characteristic of EA into a cohesive, multidimensional analytical
framework. Furthermore, the existing body of knowledge has yet to fully
explore the potential of AI-driven processes to enhance the dynamics of
these combined dimensions, thereby fostering both sustainable devel­
opment and accelerated innovation within these complex adaptive
systems. Successfully deploying AI at scale across an organization de­
mands arobust strategyand governance.EA offers structure,ensuring AI
initiatives are strategically integrated with business objectives, pre­
venting fragmented implementation. Therefore, this study develops and
empirically grounds a multidimensional framework to address this gap.
This framework explicitly integrates EA's vertical layers with SIEs' hor­
izontal dimensions. It treats EA artifacts (e.g., modular architectures and
governance principles) as boundary objects that align diverse stake­
holders across business, technology, and sustainability domains. Our
primary aim is to explore how AI can be integrated across the vertical
layers of EA and the horizontal layers of the SIE to enhance organiza­
tional resilience and advance sustainable innovation. In addressing the
complexity of these systems, we explicitly clarify that our study ap­
proaches governance from the perspective of the focal actor (the en­
terprise) operating within the broader complex adaptive system (the
ecosystem). We posit that while no single actor controls the entire SIE,
the focal enterprise utilizes EA as an internal governance mechanism to
adapt to, and influence, the external complexity of the ecosystem. To
guide our inquiry, this research is structured around the following
Research Questions (RQ).
RQ1: How do industry experts perceive the strategic importance of
AI at the different intersections of EA layers and SIE components?
RQ2: What are the practical strategies, socio-technical barriers, and
governance approaches that organizations are currently employing
to integrate AI into their EA?
RQ3: How can these empirical insights be synthesized into a cohe­
sive framework to help organizations assess and enhance the resil­
ience of their AI-enabled SIEs?
To answer these questions, we employ a sequential mixed-methods
research design. The initial phase consists of a Systematic Literature
Review (SLR) to synthesize existing knowledge and identify founda­
tional concepts at the nexus of AI, EA, and SIEs. The insights from the
SLR inform the second phase: an empirical study involving 10 senior
industry experts with diverse backgrounds in technology, business, and
sustainability. This phase utilized a structured protocol that gathered
quantitative data via a matrix-based survey to assess the strategic
importance of AI and was supplemented with in-depth, semi-structured
interviews to explore real-world integration strategies, challenges, and
best practices.
Our analysis yields several findings that inform both theory and
practice. Quantitatively, we find a strong consensus that AI's strategic
value is overwhelmingly perceived to be at the Business and Data layers,
rather than the foundational technology layers. Crucially, we identify a
lexicon gap, suggesting that while practitioners may lack familiarity
with formal EA terminology (explicit knowledge), they demonstrate a
high degree of tacit architectural thinking developed through opera­
tional experience. Qualitatively, our study uncovers an EA paradox,
wherein practitioners value architectural principles (e.g., governance,
modularity) but largely eschew formal EA frameworks as too rigid for
the pace of AI. We theorize this paradox as a strategic transition from
governance by rules to governance by principles, serving as a dynamic
capability to navigate the tension between AI agility and structural
inertia.
Furthermore, the most significant barriers to adoption were consis­
tently identified as socio-technical, specifically, the human elements of
skills, culture, and resistance to change, rather than purely technological
challenges. These findings have several implications. Theoretically, they
contribute to a more nuanced, socio-technical understanding of gover­
nance in complex adaptive systems by operationalizing the EA-IE
intersection through three formal, testable propositions (P1, P2, P3)
that define the framework's boundary conditions. Practically, they
provide actionable guidance for managers on where to focus AI efforts
and highlight the importance of investing in human and organizational
capabilities. Finally, this research moves beyond conceptual models by
providing a formalized roadmapfor future research. We proposea multi-
agent simulation grounded in Belief-Desire-Intention (BDI) logic and the
Overview, Design concepts, and Details (ODD) protocol to examine the
emergent behaviors of these AI-enabled SIEs.
The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. Section 2 re­
views the literature on the core concepts, establishing the theoretical
foundation for our study. Section 3 provides a detailed account of our
mixed-methods research design, including participant selection, the
data collection protocol, and the analysis strategy. Section 4 presents the
integrated quantitative and qualitative results derived from our expert
study. Section 5 discusses the theoretical and practical implications of
these findings, outlines the study's limitations, and proposes a forward-
looking research agenda. Finally, Section 6 offers concluding remarks.
2. Research background
2.1. Innovation ecosystems and sustainable processes
The conceptualization of innovation has moved well beyond linear,
firm-centric models, evolving toward the recognition of SIEs as complex
adaptive systems that are fundamental for economic progress and value
creation (Granstrand and Holgersson, 2020; Jacobides et al., 2018). IEs
are characterized by interdependent actors, heterogeneous resources,
and non-linear interactions, where novelty arises not from isolated
agents but from the synergistic interplay of diverse participants (Oh
et al., 2016; Pushpananthan and Elmquist, 2022). Adopting an
ecosystem lens allows researchers to account for the systemic dynamics
and co-evolutionary processes that underpin innovation (Baldwin et al.,
2024). Within this systemic view, we specifically focus on the focal actor
perspective. We conceptualize the enterprise not as a passive element,

but as an adaptive agent that uses EA to navigate and influence the
broader complex adaptive system. This perspective has stimulated
increasing scholarly efforts to deconstruct the complexity of ecosystems,
whether through identifying the specific roles and activities of actors
(Carrara and Freisinger, 2024; Dedehayir et al., 2018), mapping rela­
tional and structural configurations (Panetti et al., 2020), or developing
strategies that align innovation initiatives with the distinct architecture
of ecosystems (Adner, 2006). In parallel, the notion of innovation plat­
forms has gained traction as an essential mechanism for enabling
collaboration and knowledge exchange, thereby reinforcing the inter­
connectedness of ecosystem thinking (Dedehayir et al., 2018; Han et al.,
2022; Jacobides et al., 2018).
A pivotal evolution in this literature has been the integration of
sustainability as a defining dimension of IEs, giving rise to the notion of
SIEs. This reconceptualization recognizes that ecosystems are increas­
ingly expected to deliver not only economic value but also social in­
clusion and environmental responsibility (Bakry et al., 2024, 2025; Liu
and Stephens, 2019). This shift toward Sustainable Development Goals
(SDGs) introduces governance tensions between multinational orches­
trators’ competitive interests and collective benefits (Nylund et al.,
2021). Intermediaries are crucial for reconciling these competing logics
through cross-sectoral partnerships and governance mechanisms that
prevent fragmentation (Leitão et al., 2018; Oliveira-Duarte et al., 2021;
Sultana and Turkina, 2023).
Digital transformation and circular economy principles further
accelerate the transition toward sustainable production models (Alka
et al., 2024; Liao et al., 2024). However, the complexity of these systems
necessitates sophisticated analytical approaches; consequently, scholars
have modeled ecosystem evolution (Neto et al., 2024; Paasi et al., 2023)
and applied frameworks like panarchy to explain adaptive cycles of
renewal (Boyer, 2020). Within this context, resilience is a defining
attribute of viability, driving the development of novel measurement
frameworks (Bakhtiar et al., 2026). Collectively, this research highlights
that governing SIEs requires addressing complexity through structured
frameworks that steer innovation toward competitiveness and inclusive,
environmentally responsible development (Baldwin et al., 2024; Leitão
et al., 2018; Nylund et al., 2021; Oliveira-Duarte et al., 2021).
2.2. Enterprise architecture and sustainable processes
EA has evolved from a purely technical alignment tool into a
comprehensive management approach capable of orchestrating
enterprise-wide sustainable transformation (Gampfer et al., 2018;
Kotusev and Kurnia, 2021). Its capacity to provide integrated perspec­
tives on socio-technical systems allows organizations to embed sus­
tainability into core operations, enhancing coherence and minimizing
redundancy (Kotusev and Kurnia, 2021; Lapalme, 2012). EA provides
structured methodologies to realize strategic benefits and balance eco­
nomic objectives with social imperatives (Shanks et al., 2018; Tamm
et al., 2022). Sustainability is now a central dimension of this trans­
formation (Alghamdi, 2024b), supported by specialized frameworks
such as Green EA and sustainable government models that align digital
transformation with environmental goals (Alghamdi, 2024b; Liu and
Stephens, 2019; Thirasakthana and Kiattisin, 2021; Vandevenne et al.,
2023).
In addition to high-level frameworks, EA offers concrete tools and
techniques that facilitate sustainable innovation. The use of architecture
patterns, for instance, provides reusable design solutions to recurring
sustainability challenges, such as energy-efficient IT infrastructures or
circular supply chain processes (Alghamdi, 2024b; García-Escallón
et al., 2021; Vandevenne et al., 2023). EA artifacts, ranging from
capability maps to system models, function as boundary objects that
bridge the communication gaps among stakeholders with divergent
priorities and vocabularies, such as business strategists, IT architects,
and sustainability officers (Kotusev et al., 2022, 2023). These artifacts
are critical for fostering shared understanding and coordinated action,
which are prerequisites for implementing complex, cross-functional
sustainability initiatives.
EA's role extends across diverse contexts, from integrating systems
engineering in supply chain management (Nuerk and Dařena, 2023) to
enabling Small and Medium-sized Enterprises (SMEs) with limited re­
sources to participate in SIEs through parsimonious digitalization
models (Pham and Vu, 2022). These applications demonstrate that EA's
value for sustainable innovation is accessible to both large corporations
and smaller actors. Furthermore, the evolution of EA toward intelligent
digital systems (Gampfer et al., 2018; Zimmermann et al., 2020) and the
integration of emerging technologies like AI, Internet of Things, and
analytics (Afriliana et al., 2022; Aldoseri et al., 2024; Mariani et al.,
2023) expands its strategic relevance (Tamm et al., 2022). By enhancing
real-time monitoring and adaptive decision-making, EA serves as both a
blueprint for digital transformation and a critical enabler of the struc­
tural integrity, agility, and transparency (Hoogervorst, 2004; Tamm
et al., 2022) required to navigate modern enterprise complexities
(Mariani et al., 2023; Nardello et al., 2015).
2.3. Artificial intelligence and innovation ecosystems
AI introduces a transformative layer to SIEs, fundamentally reshap­
ing value creation processes, inter-actor relationships, and the structural
logic of ecosystems (Secundo et al., 2025). AI-enabled ecosystems differ
from traditional digital ecosystems through their rapid, data-driven in­
teractions and new modes of collaboration that span industries and
organizational boundaries (Arenal et al., 2020; Stahl, 2022). Unlike
earlier technologies, AI augments human intelligence, fostering a
co-evolution of capabilities that enhances creativity and green value
creation (Huang et al., 2025; Obreja et al., 2024; Salminen et al., 2024).
Strategically, AI-powered innovation rests on three pillars: intelligent
automation, data-driven decision-making, and ecosystem-level collab­
oration (Aldoseri et al., 2024). These pillars restructure value chains and
produce distinct global development pathways depending on local
governance choices (Chen et al., 2023).
AI also expands the toolkit for managing complexity in SIEs. Intel­
ligent platforms and services enable open innovation, foster knowledge
exchange, and scale experimentation (Corrales-Garay et al., 2024;
Zimmermann et al., 2021). Such platforms have been shown to catalyze
sustainable growth (Barile et al., 2026) while also lowering barriers for
smaller actors, as tailored frameworks allow micro, small, and
medium-sized enterprises to participate in AI-enabled IEs (Qu and Kim,
2025). This democratization of AI access is critical for broadening
innovation participation and ensuring inclusivity within digitalized
ecosystems. However, this acceleration necessitates governance frame­
works to ensure fairness, accountability, and transparency (Minkkinen
et al., 2023; Stahl, 2022; Sun et al., 2022). Ultimately, AI is a catalyst for
next-generation ecosystems, capable of driving sustainable trans­
formation if steered by responsible and inclusive governance
frameworks.
2.4. Artificial intelligence and enterprise architecture
Addressing the complexity of AI within SIEs requires structured
governance and integration mechanisms, a role for which EA is uniquely
positioned (Sipola et al., 2023; Stahl, 2022). The imperative is no longer
simply to adopt AI technologies, but to embed them sustainably into the
organizational core, ensuring that they reinforce long-term strategic
objectives rather than remain isolated pilots (Kamalabai et al., 2024;
Raisch and Krakowski, 2021). However, this integration challenges
traditional EA practices, demanding a shift toward more adaptive, dy­
namic, and intelligent architectures (Chatterjee et al.,2020; Kotusev and
Kurnia, 2021) capable of supporting rapid change and continuous
learning (Rittelmeyer and Sandkuhl, 2021; Zimmermann et al., 2020).
By acting as a bridge between strategic intent and operational reality, EA
translates sustainability-oriented AI strategies into resilient practices,

3. Research methodology
This study is framed by the EA-IE governance matrix (Fig. 1), which
conceptualizes the SIE as a complex adaptive system. In this model, the
focal enterprise acts as an adaptive agent that utilizes the vertical layers
of EA (Business, Application, Data, Technology) to maintain structural
core stability. This internal order is balanced against the emergent
environment of the IE (Actors, Resources, Processes), which is driven by
the macro-pressures of AI and Sustainability.
The 12 intersections of the matrix represent socio-technical gover­
nance interfaces systematically derived from the synthesis of our SLR.
Specifically, we cross-referenced the foundational vertical layers of EA
with the horizontal, collaborative dimensions of IEs identified in the
literature to map the core socio-technical tensions. To ensure theoretical
transparency, each interface was configured based on established
theoretical anchors: the Business layer intersections (e.g., strategy
alignment, circular model integration) are grounded in ecosystem
orchestration and value co-creation theory (Adner, 2006; Jacobides
et al., 2018); the Application and Data layer intersections (e.g., platform
governance, data sovereignty, sharing) reflect digital platform gover­
nance and the role of architecture as a boundary object across IEs
(Kotusev et al., 2023; Paasi et al., 2023); and the Technology layer in­
tersections (e.g., green compute, interoperability standards) align with
modular systems and sustainable infrastructure research (Tilson et al.,
2010; Zimmermann et al., 2023). These 12 points provide the opera­
tional definitions for how adaptive resilience – defined as the capacity to
transform internal structures while maintaining ecosystem alignment –
is executed. For instance, the intersection of Technology and Processes
operationalizes interoperability standards as a requirement for
cross-organizational AI functionality, while the Business-Actor interface

To address our RQs, we adopted a sequential mixed-methods
research design, beginning with the SLR to establish the theoretical
foundation, followed by an empirical study to develop and validate our
proposed framework. This approach allows for a comprehensive inves­
tigation, grounding theoretical development in both existing literature
and contemporary professional practice.
3.1. Systematic literature review
The first phase of our research involved the SLR. We followed the
rigorous, multi-stage process advocated for management and informa­
tion systems research (Kitchenham et al., 2009; Tranfield et al., 2003),
ensuring our procedure is transparent and replicable, in line with the
PRISMA 2020 statement principles (Page et al., 2021). The SLR was
guided by the aim of providing the theoretical foundation for our RQs.
Specifically, it addressed two key objectives: 1) to synthesize how the
concepts of AI, EA, and SIE are conceptualized and empirically linked in
existing literature, thereby informing RQ1 on the perceived strategic
importance of AI at the intersections of EA layers and SIE components;
and 2) to identify the mechanisms through which AI-driven processes
influence the relationship between the structural layers of EA and the
collaborative elements of SIEs, directly contributing to RQ2 and framing
the development of the cohesive framework proposed in RQ3.
We conducted a comprehensive search in June 2025 across two
primary academic databases, Scopus and Web of Science (WoS), selected
for their extensive coverage of high-quality, peer-reviewed literature in
technology, management, and sustainability sciences. To mitigate pub­
lication bias and capture relevant grey literature, such as influential
working papers and reports, our search was supplemented by a struc­
tured query of Google Scholar (Haddaway et al., 2015). The search
query was constructed around four conceptual pillars identified from
our preliminary investigation. The following search string was applied
to the abstract field to maintain a focused yet comprehensive scope:
((“innovation ecosystem”) AND (sustainable OR sustainability)) OR
((“enterprise architecture” OR “enterprise architecture framework”) AND
(sustainable OR sustainability)) OR ((“innovation ecosystem”) AND (AI OR
“artificial intelligence”)) OR ((“enterprise architecture” OR “enterprise
architecture framework”) AND (AI OR “artificial intelligence”)).
The initial search yielded 1100 results from Scopus, 449 from WoS,
and the first 997 contextually relevant results from Google Scholar. After
automatic and manual removal of duplicates, 658 unique records
remained. The study selection proceeded in two stages, conducted
independently by two authors to ensure reliability. First, we screened
the titles and abstracts based on predefined inclusion and exclusion
criteria. To maintain interdisciplinary validity and ensure rigorous
quality appraisal, we applied a three-tiered quality assessment frame­
work to each candidate study: (1) source reliability, requiring that the
study be published in a peer-reviewed journal or full conference pro­
ceeding indexed in major academic databases (Scopus, WoS) with a
preference for journals ranked in the Q1/Q2 tiers; (2) methodological
transparency, excluding studies that failed to provide a clear description
of their research design, data collection, or theoretical derivation; and
(3) theoretical depth, prioritizing studies that offered novel conceptual
extensions of the AI-EA-SIE intersection. By explicitly excluding non-
peer-reviewed materials, extended abstracts, and editorials, we
ensured that the 12 governance interfaces identified in our conceptual
framework (Fig. 1) were derived from high-impact, theoretically
grounded evidence.
This led to the exclusion of 587 articles that were non-relevant to the
objectives (n = 354), lacked sufficient detail for evaluation (n = 175), or
only reused existing frameworks without novel contribution (n = 58).
Any disagreements between the two reviewers were resolved in
consensus meetings, with a third author acting as an arbitrator when
necessary. This process resulted in a final corpus of 71 studies deemed
highly relevant for full-text analysis and synthesis. These studies were
then categorized to structure our analysis: 1) IEs and sustainable pro­
cesses (n = 24), 2) EA and sustainable processes (n = 17), 3) AI and IEs
(n = 14), and 4) AI and EA (n = 16).
3.2. Empirical framework development and validation
3.2.1. Research design and rationale
To address our RQs, we adopted a sequential explanatory mixed-
methods design (Creswell and Plano Clark, 2018). This approach is
well-suited for complex socio-technical inquiries, as it allows for an
initial quantitative exploration of broad patterns, followed by an
in-depth qualitative investigation to explain and enrich those findings
(Yin, 2017). The rationale for this two-phase design is grounded in the
need to first systematically map what architectural intersections are
perceived as most critical for AI integration (the quantitative phase) and
then to understand why and how these intersections are being addressed
in practice, including the associated strategies, challenges, and future
implications (the qualitative phase). The quantitative component is not
intended for statistical generalization; its purpose is descriptive and
heuristic. It serves to identify and prioritize the perceived importance of
the governance interfaces shown in Fig. 1, thereby providing a struc­
tured starting point for the subsequent qualitative interviews.
The initial quantitative phase provides a structured, comparable
dataset on the perceived strategic importance of AI, while the subse­
quent qualitative phase offers the rich, contextual narrative needed to
interpret these ratings and explore emergent themes. By integrating
these two data types, this study aims to produce a more comprehensive
understanding than could be achieved through a single method alone,
thereby enhancing the validity and practical relevance of our findings.
3.2.2. Participant selection and sample characteristics
Given the nature of the research topic, we employed a non-
probabilistic purposive sampling strategy, specifically targeting senior
industry leaders through expert sampling (Patton,2015). We established
rigorous selection criteria to ensure the participants' deep knowledge
and relevant experience (Okoli and Pawlowski, 2004). Nominees were
required to: 1) hold a senior executive or strategic role (e.g., CEO, CIO,
Founder, Director); 2) possess a minimum of five years of practical
experience in managing digital transformation, AI adoption, or enter­
prise strategy; and 3) have active engagement in technology-driven
innovation. Crucially, to ensure relevance to the SIE context, a supple­
mentary criterion required that all nominees had actively participated in
projects with explicit Environmental, Social, and Governance (ESG) or
circular economy components within the last three years. This ensured
that their strategic insights were grounded in the practical realities of
sustainable innovation, regardless of their primary job title.
Our final sample comprised 10 senior industry experts, intentionally
selected to provide a heterogeneous and holistic perspective on the
research problem. This small-sample, expert-centric approach is well-
established in technology management research, where the goal is
deep, reliable assessment from highly knowledgeable sources rather

3.2.3. Data collection protocol and procedure
Data were collected between May and July 2025 using a compre­
hensive, semi-structured protocol designed to systematically capture
both quantitative and qualitative data. The protocol was administered
through a mix of in-person meetings, online video/audio interviews, and
online surveys to accommodate the demanding schedules of the senior
expert participants. The protocol was structured into four distinct parts
to ensure a logical flow and consistency across all data collection modes.
● Part 1: This initial section gathered demographic and professional
information, including job title, expertise, and years of experience,
after obtaining informed consent.
● Part 2: Question 1 (Q1) was developed to calibrate each expert's
background, this part used a 5-point Likert-type scale (from “no
knowledge” to “expert”) for participants to self-assess their famil­
iarity with the study's four core concepts. This served as both a
contextual primer and a validation of their expertise.
● Part 3: This section formed the core quantitative component of the
study using Question 2 (Q2). Participants were asked to evaluate the
importance of AI integration on a 6-point Likert scale (from “not at
all important” to “very important”) across a 4 × 3 matrix. The matrix
mapped the vertical layers of EA (Business, Application, Data,
Technology) against the horizontal components of an IE (Actors,
Resources, Processes). The purpose was to systematically map the
perceived strategic importance of AI across the architectural
landscape.
● Part 4: The final and most extensive part of the protocol consisted of
open-ended questions (Q3, Q4, Q5, Q6, and Q7) designed to elicit
rich, narrative responses. These questions were organized into five
thematic areas to explore the “why” and “how” behind the quanti­
tative ratings:
1. AI's role in sustainable innovation (current applications, impact
on competitive advantage, alignment with sustainability).
2. AI-EA integration strategies and challenges (current strategies,
framework usage, management of technical/organizational
hurdles).
3. Organizational readiness and resilience (assessment factors,
measures to enhance adaptability).
4. Key adoption barriers (primary obstacles, specific ethical and
governance challenges).
5. Future perspectives and recommendations (best practices, pre­
dictions for the evolution of EA and IEs).
3.2.4. Data analysis strategy
To systematically analyze the collected data, we employed a parallel
analysis approach, with quantitative and qualitative data first examined
separately and later integrated during the final interpretation stage. This
approach is consistent with the sequential explanatory mixed-methods
design (Creswell and Plano Clark, 2018) and ensures that both strands
of data retain their integrity before being synthesized into a holistic
interpretation aligned with our RQs.
The quantitative data were analyzed using Python and appropriate
statistical libraries to compute mean scores, standard deviations, and
visualizations. This allowed us to identify EA-IE intersections with the
highest and lowest perceived strategic importance, assess levels of
consensus and divergence among experts, and map patterns relevant to
RQ1. We explicitly note that these results are not inferential: descriptive
statistics merely summarize our sample and cannot be generalized to a
larger population.
The qualitative data, consisting of transcribed open-ended responses
and interview transcripts, were analyzed using reflexive thematic
analysis, following the six-phase process outlined by Braun and Clarke
(2022) and Braun et al. (2014). This process involved: 1) data famil­
iarization through iterative reading; 2) systematic, line-by-line coding of
the dataset; 3) generating candidate themes from the codes; 4) review­
ing and refining the themes against the coded extracts and full dataset;
5) defining and naming final themes; and 6) producing an analytic
narrative supported by rich, illustrative quotations. This analysis was
particularly central for addressing RQ2, as it provided insights into the
socio-technical barriers, governance approaches, and practical strate­
gies for AI integration into EA. In the final integration stage, qualitative
insights were systematically compared with and used to elaborate on the
quantitative patterns, producing a cohesive, multi-layered interpreta­
tion of the findings (Fetters et al., 2013). For example, where quanti­
tative results highlighted the Business Layer–Actors intersection as
highly important, qualitative narratives explained why this is the case,
detailing how AI augments human capabilities, supports
decision-making, and reshapes roles within IEs. This explanatory inte­
gration also informed the synthesis of a multidimensional framework
(RQ3), combining the quantitative prioritization of strategic layers with
the qualitative identification of enablers and barriers.
To enhance credibility and trustworthiness, we employed researcher
triangulation implemented through a negotiated agreement approach
(Campbell et al., 2013). Two researchers independently coded the raw
data to minimize subjective bias. Subsequently, a series of consensus
meetings were held to compare coding structures, debate divergent in­
terpretations, and refine thematic definitions. This iterative process
continued until 100% agreement was reached on the final themes. We
prioritized this dialogic consensus over statistical metrics (such as
inter-coder reliability scores), aligning with the interpretivist philoso­
phy of reflexive thematic analysis advocated by Braun and Clarke
(2022). The combination of quantitative rigor and qualitative depth thus
enabled a robust and contextually grounded interpretation of the data.

4.2. Qualitative insights: thematic analysis of expert interviews
A thematic analysis of the qualitative data revealed four primary,
interconnected themes that encapsulate the current state, challenges,
and future trajectory of integrating AI into EA for SIEs.
Theme 1. AI's evolving role as a catalyst for sustainable innovation
The interviews clearly indicate that AI's role in innovation is un­
dergoing a significant and rapid evolution, moving beyond its initial
application in narrow, back-end functions to become a central and
strategic driver of business value. Experts described a clear trajectory
from using machine learning for basic data processing and process
optimization to leveraging sophisticated AI, particularly Generative AI,
for high-value strategic activities. This includes competitive analysis,
simulating and evaluating new development models, and accelerating
the design of new products. This shift marks a transition from AI as a tool
for efficiency to AI as a platform for creativity and strategic foresight.
The tangible benefits are significant, with experts reporting increased
sales through recommendation engines, reduced operational costs via
predictive analytics, and enhanced agility to react to market changes.
For some, AI has become the very core of their business model, enabling
them to disrupt traditional industries like consulting by offering scal­
able, AI-driven solutions that deliver value faster than incumbent
competitors.

a growing priority, though the maturity of this integration varies. The
most prevalent approach is leveraging AI for operational efficiency, a
direct contribution to environmental sustainability. This includes opti­
mizing logistics, reducing the energy intensity of production processes,
minimizing computational waste, and even powering data centers with
renewable energy. A more advanced, strategic approach involves using
AI as a tool to design and enable sustainable solutions. Examples include
monitoring carbonfootprints, improving supply chaintransparency,and

Theme 2. EA-AI integration paradox: principles over frameworks
A striking and consistent theme that emerged from the interviews
was a paradox concerning the role of formal EA frameworks. While a
minority of experts, often from more structured or regulated industries,
reported the active use of established frameworks like TOGAF or
Zachman toguide theirAIintegration, theclear majority statedthat they
do not use formal frameworks at all. Instead, they rely on lightweight
variants, loose references, or simply a set of guiding architectural prin­
ciples. This suggests a widespread perception that traditional, heavy­
weight EA methodologies are too rigid and bureaucratic to keep pace
with the rapid, iterative nature of AI development. Specifically, this
transition towards principle-based governance is most pronounced in
highly agile environments where the demand for rapid AI deployment
outpaces traditional bureaucratic cycles. However, as the experts
implicitly warned, if these guiding principles are not firmly established,
this pursuit of agility can inadvertently lead to the proliferation of
“shadow AI” where decentralized teams adopt unsanctioned AI tools,
bypassing official governance structures entirely.
However, despite this explicit move away from formal frameworks,
the experts' descriptions of their integration strategies revealed a strong,
implicit adherence to core architectural principles. This shared archi­
tectural thinking consistently involves a structured, phased approach: 1)
starting with business value – the first step is almost always identifying
specific business capabilities or pain points where AI can add measur­
able value, this ensures that AI initiatives are strategically aligned and
not technologically driven; 2) phased and iterative rollouts – experts
universally advocated for starting with small-scale pilot projects to
prove value, learn from failures, and build organizational momentum
before attempting to scale, and 3) prioritizing a robust foundation –
there was a clear recognition that successful AI depends on a solid
technical foundation, with an emphasis on robust data pipelines, API-
first or modular architectures, and the use of scalable cloud services.
This paradox suggests that in the context of AI, the practical value of EA
lies less in its formal proceduralism and more in its underlying discipline
as a way of thinking – a cognitive tool for managing complexity,
ensuring strategic alignment, and governing change in a fast-moving
environment. Consequently, navigating this paradox requires robust
mechanisms specifically at the Data-Process interface, ensuring that
automated AI workflows are governed by flexible principles rather than
rigid procedural rules. This architectural thinking effectively functions
as the governance mechanism for the matrix defined in Fig. 1. Even in
the absence of formal frameworks, experts are intuitively managing
specific interfaces, such as automated compliance and co-creation
workflows (Application-Processes), to ensure that rapid AI iterations
do not compromise the EA's.
Theme 3. Overcoming socio-technical barriers to adoption
The experts were unanimous in their assessment that the most
significant and difficult barriers to AI adoption are not purely technical
but are deeply socio-technical. The human element was consistently
identified as the single most critical challenge. This includes overcoming
employee fear and resistance to change, which can slow down or derail
projects, as well as addressing the persistent talent and skills gap that
makes it difficult to find qualified experts who can bridge the divide
between technology and business domains. Additionally, a critical
operational risk was identified regarding the entry of sensitive corporate
data into public AI assistants, a practice that has forced several organi­
zations to deactivate AI tools, thereby stifling the very innovation they
were intended to support. To navigate these human challenges, experts
emphasized the importance of early and continuous stakeholder
engagement, communication to demystify AI, and extensive training
programs that focus not only on technical skills but also on adapting
workflows and understanding ethical implications. This socio-technical
tension explicitly highlights the critical importance of the Business-
Actor interface within our framework, where strategic AI alignment
must directly address human capital and organizational culture rather
than just technological deployment. The risk highlights a breakdown at
the data sovereignty and ethical governance (Data-Actors) and data
sharing agreements and APIs (Data-Resources) interfaces. The qualita­
tive insights suggest that socio-technical barriers are most acute when
the governance interfaces intended to protect data integrity are
bypassed by emergent AI usage patterns.
The second major category of barriers is centered on data and
governance. Poor data quality, fragmented data silos, and the difficulty
of integrating with legacy systems were cited as foundational technical
obstacles that can cripple AI initiatives before they even begin. Along­
side this, establishing an effective AI governance framework was seen as
essential but highly complex. This includes managing several ethical
challenges such as algorithmic bias, ensuring transparency and
explainability (black box problem), and navigating complex regulatory
landscapes. The lackof clear accountability for AI-driven errors creates a
governance vacuum that can lead to legal and reputational damage for
the focal enterprise within its broader ecosystem.
To build the necessary organizational readiness and resilience, ex­
perts recommended a holistic approach. This begins with a compre­
hensive assessment of readiness across multiple dimensions: data,
technology, skills, culture, and financial capacity. The most effective
resilience measures identified were practical and iterative, such as using
phased rollouts to minimize disruption, establishing robust monitoring
and feedback loops, and, most importantly, embedding human-in-the-
loop designs for critical processes. These measures serve to strengthen
the interoperability standards (Technology-Processes) and platform
governance roles (Application-Actors) interfaces, ensuring that the
technology layer effectively supports the human actors in the SIEs. This
latter point was seen as crucial for maintaining human oversight,
building trust, and ensuring that AI serves as an augmentation of, rather
than a replacement for, human intelligence.

a dynamic operating system for change or a real-time strategic dash­

them to focus on higher-level strategic thinking.

4.3. Integrating quantitative and qualitative findings
The integration of findings follows a convergent mixed-methods
design, where qualitative narratives are used to unpack the descriptive
patterns identified in the heatmaps. By mapping findings onto the EA-IE
governance matrix, we see that the areas of highest quantitative
importance (the Business and Data layers) are also the zones of highest
qualitative complexity. Quantitatively, experts rated the Business and
Data layers as the most strategically important for AI integration, while
the Application and Technology layers received lower scores, indicating
their perception as enablers rather than direct value drivers. However,
the moderate standard deviations across these layers suggest that indi­
vidual expert variation is significant. Qualitative insights reveal that this
variation is not random but contingent on the specific sectoral context of
the expert. These patterns align with the notion that AI's greatest impact
occurs at the interface of organizational strategy and operational pro­
cesses, where it shapes decision-making, actor collaboration, and inno­
vation performance. This convergence between numeric ratings and
expert perspectives underscores a collective understanding of AI as a
strategic rather than a purely technical phenomenon.
Qualitative insights enrich these findings by contextualizing why
such patterns emerge and refining the average view provided by the
means. Across interviews, experts consistently described the Business
and Data layers as translational zones, where AI bridges human exper­
tise with machine intelligence. In the context of our framework, these
translational zones represent the ecosystem strategy alignment and data
sovereignty and ethical governance interfaces. For instance, one
respondent explained that “AI helps translate business intent into execut­
able workflows far faster than any framework could”. Another emphasized
that “our architecture evolves daily – AI forces us to rethink design on the
fly”. These reflections explain why the Business layer received the
highest consensus: there is a universal strategic urgency to leverage AI
for process agility. These reflections illustrate how AI operates as a dy­
namic coordination mechanism across enterprise layers, aligning with
socio-technical perspectives that view innovation as co-produced
through human and technological interactions. Conversely, the rela­
tively lower importance and higher disagreement attributed to the
Technology layer reflects a sector-specific divide: digital-native experts
viewed infrastructure as a commodity (leading to lower scores), whereas
experts from manufacturing sectors viewed it as a high-risk bottleneck
(leading to higher scores and increased variance).
Synthesizing both datasets reveals a layered complementarity that
enhances interpretive validity. The quantitative trends identify where
AI's influence is concentrated, while the qualitative narratives explain
how these intersections materialize in practice. This integration exposes
the EA paradox – the tension between maintaining structural consis­
tency and embracing adaptive flexibility. As reflected in the experts'
statements, the 12 governance interfaces defined in Fig. 1 serve as
boundary objects that enable communication and coherence across
functional, data, and technological domains. By operationalizing EA as a
set of adaptive interfaces rather than a static blueprint, the focal actor
can successfully bridge the gap between its internal structural core and
the external emergent AI-enabled SIEs.
5. Discussion and limitations
The findings of this study provide insights into the intersection of AI,
EA, and SIEs. Our results highlight three main dynamics: 1) a pro­
nounced strategic prioritization of AI at the Business and Data layers; 2)
the reliance on architectural principles without consistent use of formal
EA frameworks; and 3) the persistent predominance of human and
organizational barriers over technical challenges. These empirical pat­
terns validate the analytical utility of the EA-IE governance matrix,
particularly regarding the EA's role in providing the structural core
needed to manage ecosystem emergence. The qualitative data suggests
that isolated pilots often fail because they lack grounding in the auto­
mated compliance and co-creation interface, leading to a disconnect
between AI capability and ecosystem workflows. Furthermore, the
quantitative assessment indicates that the data sharing agreements and
APIs nexus is a primary predictor of an enterprise's resilience within the
SIE. This supports the complex adaptive systems view that these specific
governance interfaces, such as green compute access and identity
management and circular business model integration, are vital for
agentic adaptation, allowing enterprises to balance internal stability
with the chaotic potential of AI-driven innovation.
Together, these findings reveal a misalignment between the future
vision of EA as a dynamic, AI-augmented discipline and the current
fragmented practices in organizations. This gap resonates with earlier
observations that organizations often struggle to fully capture AI's
transformative potential because of inertia in governance, skills, and
cultural readiness (Goralski and Tan, 2020; Leal Filho et al., 2023;
Murire, 2024; Tuncer and Varoglu, 2025). In particular, the reliance on
architectural thinking without formal frameworks underscores the
enduring tension between flexibility and standardization in digital
transformation (Afriliana et al., 2022; Kotusev and Kurnia, 2021).
However, it is imperative to balance this optimism with a recognition
of AI's externalities, revealing what we characterize as the EA Paradox.
Our analysis reveals a critical sustainability tension: while AI optimizes
resource efficiency in the ecosystem (through sustainable resource
mobilization strategies), its own computational demands generate a
significant carbon footprint, potentially offsetting environmental gains.
Furthermore, experts highlighted socio-technical risks, specifically
algorithmic bias and the black box nature of automated decisions, which
can undermine trust and social inclusivity among ecosystem actors. This
emphasizes the need for robust data sovereignty and ethical governance
at the Data-Actors intersection. Therefore, governing AI-enabled SIEs
requires a dual focus – leveraging AI for innovation while actively
monitoring and mitigating its environmental and ethical liabilities
through the defined matrix interfaces.
Our results extend prior work that has highlighted the socio-
technical nature of AI adoption, where organizational culture, trust,
and governance are often more decisive than technical capabilities
(Denni-Fiberesima, 2024; Secundo et al., 2025; Zimmermann et al.,
2023). By emphasizing human-centric barriers, our study adds empirical
weight to the notion that technology affordances are only realized when
coupled with managerial courage and adaptive capabilities (Chatterjee
et al., 2020). The experts’ vision of a living EA also reflects the growing
trend toward digital architectures that are not static repositories but
adaptive, evolving systems (Boyer, 2020; Jacobides et al., 2018). Such a
vision resonates with emerging industry perspectives that position
AI-enabled EA as a catalyst for enterprise transformation, functioning as

5.1. Theoretical and practical implications
Theoretically, our findings contribute to the complex systems
perspective. The empirical evidence of a socio-technical system, where
human and organizational factors are the primary barriers, reinforces
that a purely technical view of AI integration is insufficient. Our dis­
covery of the EA integration paradox – whereby practitioners apply
architectural principles without formally adopting frameworks – sup­
ports theories of EA as a social practice and a cognitive tool for man­
aging complexity (Kotusev and Kurnia, 2021). EA artifacts and
principles, in this light, function as boundary objects that facilitate
communication and shared understanding across diverse stakeholder
groups (Kotusev et al., 2023). This pattern resonates with the duality
identified in digital transformation research, where organizations must
balance standardization for control and flexibility for innovation (Tilson
et al., 2010; Volkoff and Strong, 2013). Hence, rather than rejecting EA,
practitioners reinterpret it as a flexible governance language adaptable
to rapid AI-driven change.
This paradox can be theoretically explained through the lens of
organizational agility versus structural inertia. While formal EA frame­
works provide necessary stability, they often introduce structural inertia
that conflicts with the high-velocity requirements of AI innovation.
Consequently, organizations adopt a governance by principles approach
as a dynamic capability. This mechanism allows them to maintain
architectural coherence through shared cognitive heuristics – what we
term architectural thinking – while bypassing the bureaucratic rigidity
of heavy-weight frameworks. To operationalize this mechanism, directly
emerging from the experts' qualitative accounts of balancing AI velocity
with structural stability (Theme 2), we propose three testable
propositions.
Proposition 1. The adoption of formal EA frameworks is negatively
correlated with AI implementation speed in high-velocity industries, but
architectural principles (as weighted heuristics) positively correlate with long-
term system resilience. This transition primarily occurs within highly agile
organizations facing extreme AI velocity. However, as our empirical data
suggests, if these principles are not firmly established, organizations face a
severe risk of “shadow AI”, where decentralized units adopt unsanctioned AI
tools, bypassing architectural oversight and data sovereignty.
Proposition 2. Reflecting the expert insights from Theme 2 and Theme 3,
the EA Paradox is most prevalent in SMEs and agile-native firms, where
resource constraints and the need for speed necessitate a decoupling of
governance from formal structure.
Proposition 3. As AI maturity moves from isolated pilots to ecosystem
orchestration, the reliance on tacit architectural thinking remains high, but
the need for explicit data-layer standardization increases to manage cross-
actor interoperability.
This shift effectively decouples governance from formal structure,
enabling the agility required for rapid AI experimentation without
sacrificing long-term system integrity. These propositions provide a
roadmap for testing the relationship between organizational size, in­
dustry maturity, and AI type within the EA-IE governance matrix.
Theoretically, our framework extends the complex adaptive systems
perspective by operationalizing how intelligent agents (human and non-
human) interact within architectural boundaries to produce emergent,
adaptive organizational behaviors. This reinforces the validity of
analyzing the complex adaptive system from a focal-actor standpoint,
demonstrating that EA serves as a crucial internal governance mecha­
nism for survival and co-evolution within external ecosystem
complexity. This finding also empirically validates the need for EA to
evolve beyond rigid structures toward a more dynamic capability
(Gampfer et al., 2018), which is essential for governing the emergent
and unpredictable nature of IEs (Jacobides et al., 2018; Boyer, 2020).
Furthermore, the human-centric challenges we identified resonate with
strategic management theories on IT affordance, which posit that the
value of technology is not inherent but is actualized through human
agency, knowledge, and even courage (Chatterjee et al., 2020). Finally,
the findings contribute to sustainability-oriented innovation research by
demonstrating how AI can be mobilized not only for operational effi­
ciency but also for strategic circularity and green value creation (Nylund
et al., 2021; Huang et al., 2025).
Practically, our results suggest several takeaways. First, organiza­
tions should focus their AI integration efforts on the Business and Data
layers, where the perceived strategic value is highest (Adner, 2006).
Second, managers must recognize that the critical success factors for AI
adoption lie in governance, culture, and capability-building, requiring
significant investment in change management, upskilling, and data
governance (Jankovic and Curovic, 2023; Murire, 2024; Tuncer and
Varoglu, 2025). Third, adopting human-in-the-loop approaches pro­
vides a dual benefit: safeguarding trust and resilience while enabling
human-AI co-creation (Salminen et al., 2024; Zimmermann et al., 2023).
Finally, the finding that architectural principles can be successfully
applied without formal frameworks suggests that organizations can
benefit from cultivating a culture of structured, strategic thinking
without the bureaucratic overhead of rigid methodologies, a point
particularly relevant for SMEs and SIE participants operating under
resource constraints.
5.2. Future research directions
Building directly on our empirical findings, specifically the need to
navigate the EA paradox (Theme 2) and overcome socio-technical
workforce barriers (Theme 3), and the work of Chen and Zhao (2024)
and Li et al. (2024), we propose a future research agenda centered on
developing a multi-agent simulation framework for SIEs. Because our
exploratory empirical study (n = 10) revealed that static EA frameworks
struggle to capture the rapid, non-linear evolution of AI, we recognize
that dynamic modeling is the necessary next methodological step.
Therefore, rather than presenting a disconnected conceptual exercise,
we explicitly scope our future research agenda around multi-agent sys­
tems as a direct response to our findings. These systems are uniquely
well-suited to studying the emergent behaviors and adaptive capacities
of the complex ecosystems identified by our experts (Paasi et al., 2023),
reflecting the transformative role of AI (Secundo et al., 2025). To move
from a conceptual model to a formal representation, we define each
agent (Ai) as a tuple {Si, Ac, Ri}, where Si represents the agent's internal
state, Ac denotes the action space, and Ri is the reward function. This
framework allows the SIE to be modeled as a complex adaptive system
where diverse agents interact under evolving environmental conditions
and governance constraints.
To advance this modeling endeavor, we recommend designing
diverse, stakeholder-informed agent profiles that capture both technical
and strategic roles across the ecosystem. Examples include: 1) a business
strategist agent guided by market foresight and value co-creation logic,
as highlighted by Sun et al. (2022); 2) a sustainability officer agent
optimizing ESG and circularity trade-offs based on the principles in Alka
et al. (2024) and Bakry et al. (2024); 3) a chief architect agent tasked
with governance, security, and ethical oversight, drawing on Bakar et al.
(2024) and Minkkinen et al. (2023); and 4) an employee agent repre­
senting the human workforce, whose decision logic includes digital
skills, change resistance, and internal motivation, informed by Chat­
terjee et al. (2020). We propose using the BDI architecture for agent
design (Georgeff et al., 1999). In this logic, each agent optimizes a
distinct utility function: the business strategist maximizes Return on
Investment (ROI) subject to market volatility, while the sustainability
officer maximizes an ESG score subject to operational budget
constraints.

Emerging literature on AI-driven intelligent agents provides a strong
foundation for such an agenda. Intelligent agents are increasingly used
to model sustainability challenges, such as a manufacturing system (Kim
et al., 2020), and to simulate organizational governance and
decision-making in complex systems (An et al., 2023; Hughes et al.,
2025; Liang et al., 2022). Incorporating these insights can make agent
profiles more realistic and adaptive, allowing them to learn and evolve
with their environments, rather than merely executing fixed rules.
We further propose adapting the five simulation scenarios developed
by Chen and Zhao (2024) to reflect the distinctive challenges and
resilience demands of SIEs. To ensure methodological rigor and repro­
ducibility, researchers should implement these scenarios using the ODD
protocol (Grimm et al., 2020). These include: 1) sustainable business
model transformation, such as simulating shifts toward circular or
regenerative models; 2) green value chain restructuring, exploring the
environmental and coordination costs of different sourcing strategies; 3)
data chain analysis for ESG transparency and reporting; 4) composable
and modular digital architecture, emphasizing agility and scalability;
and 5) green infrastructure upgrading, including low-carbon IT,
renewable integration, and edge computing solutions, see Vandevenne
et al. (2023). By simulating these scenarios in an agent-based environ­
ment, researchers could analyze how architectural rules, decision logics,
and emergent interactions influence outcomes such as system resilience,
innovation diffusion, and sustainability performance under conditions
of external volatility.
A particularly valuable application of such simulations lies in testing
alternative governance paradigms. The interaction rules should be
governed by the EA Paradox principle observed in our empirics. Spe­
cifically, modeling governance not as rigid constraints (hard rules), but
as weighted penalty functions (principles) that influence, rather than
dictate, agent behavior. For instance, simulations could compare rules-
based, principle-based, and hybrid governance principles, evaluating
their relative effectiveness in promoting innovation, sustainability, and
adaptive recovery (Bakhtiar et al., 2026). Agent-based models also offer
a controlled environment to introduce external shocks, such as climate
events, regulatory shifts, or geopolitical disruptions, and to observe how
different governance logics shape recovery trajectories. In this way,
future research could not only extend the methodological repertoire of
EA and sustainability studies but also provide practical decision-support
tools for policymakers and organizations navigating the uncertainties of
AI-enabled transformation.
We provide a conceptual outline for this framework in Fig. 4.
Grounded in our empirical identification of diverse stakeholder tensions
(Theme 3), this model would simulate the interactions of these hetero­
geneous actors (e.g., strategists, employees), each with unique goals and
behaviors. These agents would operate within a simulated environment
subjected to external shocks. The agents' interactions would be governed
by a set of governance rules and principles. Researchers could then
measure the emergent outcomes (e.g., ecosystem resilience, innovation
rate, sustainability score) to test hypotheses, such as: Does principle-
based governance lead to higher resilience than rules-based gover­
nance in the face-of market shocks? This multi-agent system model
would provide a virtual laboratory to make the complex, socio-technical
governance of AI-enabled SIEs actionable and testable.
Operationalizing the model would require integrating insights from
AI readiness and maturity models with digital twin architectures that
support data exchange between simulated and real environments.
Future research could employ hybrid methodologies – combining
computational simulations with case studies – to calibrate and validate
agent behaviors. For instance, by using real enterprise data, researchers
could test how specific governance mechanisms influence innovation
rates, ESG performance, and system resilience.
Theoretically, the model contributes to the understanding of socio-
technical governance by linking micro-level agent behavior to macro-
level ecosystem adaptation. It operationalizes the complex systems
view of SIEs and provides a tool to explore emergence, feedback, and
self-organization within AI-driven architectures. Practically, it offers
decision-makers a low-risk experimental space to evaluate trade-offs
between efficiency, compliance, and sustainability before implement­
ing large-scale digital transformation strategies.
5.3. Limitations
This study's findings should be considered in light of its limitations.
The primary limitation is the small, purposive sample size of ten experts.
While this qualitative approach yielded rich, in-depth data consistent
with an exploratory study (Patton, 2015), the results are illustrative and
not statistically generalizable. Specifically, the quantitative metrics re­
ported (means and standard deviations) should be interpreted strictly as
descriptive heuristics for identifying consensus patterns within this
expert group, rather than as inferential statistics applicable to the
broader population. The use of a 6-point Likert scale was intended to
force a choice and reveal strategic concentrations. However, these re­
sults provide ordinal data suitable for prioritization and comparison, not
for high-inference claims about absolute strategic concentration. The
insights therefore represent analytical generalization – depth of under­
standing within expert contexts – rather than statistical generalization to
all enterprises (Yin, 2017). Future research should aim to validate these
findings through large-scale surveys or through comparative, in-depth
case studies of specific AI ecosystems, like the analyses conducted by
Arenal et al. (2020) in China or Sultana et al. (2023) in Montreal, to
provide deeper contextual understanding. Moreover, the study captured
perspectives at a single point in time, while both AI technologies and
governance frameworks evolve rapidly. Longitudinal studies would be
valuable to capture how organizational strategies adapt as AI and sus­
tainability pressures intensify.
Second, the study's contextual boundedness may affect trans­
ferability. All participants operated within European innovation eco­
systems characterized by strong regulatory and sustainability
frameworks. This geographic focus serves as a leading-edge context

Third, the uneven distribution of knowledge and expertise among
participants revealed a structural challenge. Several experts reported
only basic or working knowledge of EA, despite holding advanced
expertise in IEs, AI, or sustainability. This imbalance reinforces prior
observations that EA often remains siloed within IT functions and is not
well embedded across organizational domains (Lapalme, 2012; Niemi
and Pekkola, 2017). However, it is crucial to distinguish between the
explicit knowledge of formal frameworks (e.g., TOGAF specifications),
which this question measured, and the tacit architectural knowledge
derived from years of strategic leadership. Our qualitative analysis
confirms that while experts may lack fluency in academic EA termi­
nology, they demonstrate a high proficiency in architectural thinking –
the ability to govern complex socio-technical systems. Therefore, the
low self-reported familiarity reflects a gap in lexicon, not in competence,
preserving the validity of their strategic insights. Similarly, differences
in sustainability orientation (e.g., corporate ESG focus vs. product
innovation focus) could shape how value was conceptualized. To miti­
gate this, triangulation between quantitative and qualitative data was
used to ensure thematic consistency, yet these biases remain an inherent
limitation.
Fourth, the responses underscore organizational resistance and
human factors as major barriers. Multiple experts cited employee fear of
job loss, skill gaps, and cultural resistance to AI adoption. This resonates
with broader literature on digital transformation, which identifies
workforce anxieties and change fatigue as critical inhibitors (Raisch and
Krakowski, 2021; Trenerry et al., 2021). Moreover, experts acknowl­
edged that even when AI delivers measurable efficiency gains, sustain­
ing adoption requires continuous communication, training, and building
trust around fairness and transparency. Without such measures, AI risks
amplifying inequalities between employees who can adapt quickly and
those who struggle with digital upskilling.
Fifth, a recurring theme is the fragility of data governance and
quality. Experts repeatedly described siloed, inconsistent, or messy data
as a bottleneck, with significant implications for the reliability of AI
insights. This echoes existing concerns that poor data stewardship not
only reduces AI's value creation potential but also amplifies ethical and
legal risks around bias, privacy, and accountability (Gebru et al., 2021;
Stahl, 2022). Particularly concerning is that several organizations
admitted to bypassing formal EA frameworks, instead relying on light­
weight or ad-hoc principles. While this may enhanceagility, it also raises
the danger of fragmented governance and shadow AI deployments,
which could compromise long-term resilience. This lack of formalization
suggests that future research should focus on the governance vacuum
created when agile AI initiatives bypass the EA of the enterprise.
Sixth, there are sustainability paradoxes. While some organizations
reported success in using AI to optimize energy efficiency and resource
utilization, others expressed concern over the carbon footprint of AI
infrastructures and the misalignment of AI deployments with climate
goals. One expert explicitly noted the difficulty of reconciling AI's
growing energy demands with organizational sustainability targets. This
contradiction reflects a broader tension in literature, where AI is
simultaneously positioned as a tool for green innovation and as a
contributor to digital environmental burdens (Huang et al., 2025; Van­
devenne et al., 2023; Vinuesa et al., 2020).
Seventh, the findings point to governance and accountability gaps.
Several participants stressed the lack of clarity regarding responsibility
for AI-driven errors, compliance with evolving regulations, and the
ethical implications of automated decision-making. This aligns with
calls for more robust governance frameworks in AI-enabled enterprises,
particularly to manage explainability and accountability (Floridi and
Cowls, 2022). The fact that many organizations adopt lightweight ver­
sions of TOGAF or Zachman, rather than comprehensive governance
models, suggests a pragmatic but potentially risky trade-off between
speed and accountability.
6. Conclusions
This research explored the challenge of integrating AI into enter­
prises through the lens of EA to foster SIEs, a challenge of growing ur­
gency for modern organizations. Guided by three RQs, the study
develops and empirically grounds a formal, multidimensional frame­
work that advances both theoretical and practical understanding by
moving from descriptive observations to testable mechanisms.
With respect to RQ1, our findings reveal a clear consensus among
experts that AI's strategic value lies primarily at the business and data
layers, where it directly shapes actors, processes, and data flows. Criti­
cally, our analysis identifies a lexicon gap: while experts may report
moderate familiarity with formal EA frameworks (explicit knowledge),
they demonstrate a high level of tacit architectural thinking proficiency.
This distinction validates the expert assessments by prioritizing proce­
dural competence in managing complex digital transformations over
terminological fluency. This reflects a recognition that while technology
and infrastructure are essential enablers, the transformative power of AI
emerges when it intersects with business strategy and innovation
practices.
In response to RQ2, the results show that organizations are pursuing
pragmatic integration strategies that emphasize architectural principles,
such as modularity, governance, and alignment with business value,
while often bypassing formal EA frameworks viewed as too rigid. We
theorize this EA Paradox as a strategic adaptation through the lens of
dynamic capabilities, where organizations transition from governance
by rules to governance by principles to overcome structural inertia. At
the same time, practitioners face persistent socio-technical barriers:
human resistance, cultural inertia, skills gaps, and data governance is­
sues consistently overshadow purely technical challenges. This un­
derscores that successful integration depends as much on organizational
readiness and human-centric governance as on technological capability.
Regarding RQ3, the synthesis of quantitative and qualitative insights
culminates in a cohesive framework operationalized through three
testable propositions (P1, P2, P3). These propositions define the bound­
ary conditions of the EA Paradox by accounting for organizational size,
industry velocity, and AI type (exploratory vs. exploitative). This
framework highlights both where AI delivers the most strategic impact
and which socio-technical factors must be managed to ensure sustain­
able innovation.
Taken together, the study contributes in two key ways. First, it
bridges high-level theories of SIEs with the granular realities of
enterprise-level AI integration, offering a socio-technical perspective on
governance in complex adaptive systems. Unlike existing models, our
framework explicitly integrates the vertical EA layers with the hori­
zontal SIE dimensions, treating EA principles and artifacts as boundary
objects that align diverse stakeholders. Second, it provides a foundation
for future research by formalizing a multi-agent simulation roadmap. By
adopting the BDI logic and the ODD protocol, we provide a mathemat­
ically grounded path for examining the dynamic interplay of stakeholder
roles, architectural principles, and emergent system behaviors. Looking
ahead, these insights suggest that organizations must prepare for a
paradigm shift: AI is poised to transform EA from a static discipline into
a living, adaptive function that continuously evolves with real-time
data. This transition will redefine the role of enterprise architects as
curators of AI-driven insights and stewards of ethical, human-centered
