Leveraging business-IT alignment through enterprise
architecture—an empirical study to estimate the extents
Morteza Alaeddini1 • Hamed Asgari2 • Arash Gharibi3 • Mona Rashidi Rad4
Published online: 21 March 2016
 Springer Science+Business Media New York 2016
Abstract Achieving business-IT alignment (BITA) as a
long-term and appraising management issue can be
accomplished in a few ways, enterprise architecture (EA)
being one of them. This paper attempts to give a critical
understanding of the effects of performing EA on different
aspects of BITA maturity through a global survey. A total
of 236 respondents from 60 countries, a relatively large
response for a survey, were selected. The main purpose of
the research is to examine these impacts and to identify
directions for innovative practices in the future, the unique
contributions of this work. A questionnaire designed on the
Luftman’s maturity model as well as various other statis-
tical methods, including PLS path modeling, Wilcoxon
matched-pairs signed-ranks test and Mann–Whitney U test,
are applied to understand how the EA can deliver benefits.
The implications of our findings in this study as well as its
limitations are discussed from different viewpoints to
enable both academics and practitioners to detect the flaws
in the existing EA frameworks and propose improvements.
Keywords Enterprise architecture (EA)  Business-IT
alignment (BITA)  Luftman’s maturity model  Partial
least squares (PLS)
1 Introduction
In the increasingly turbulent and competitive environments
of the 21st century, IT supports several business strategies
and can also shape new strategies to tap innovation within
organizations [1]. Surveys conducted on the chief concerns
of senior managers reveal that aligning business and IT
remains among the top-ranked issues [2–4].
As reviewed by Alaeddini and Salekfard [5] and Chen
[6], business-IT alignment (BITA) can be understood and
achieved via three different approaches, including ‘archi-
tecture’ [7–13], ‘governance’ [14–16] and ‘relationship’
[17–20]. The first means, i.e. enterprise architecture (EA),
ensures that it is possible to achieve a suitable alignment
within all the enterprise layers by identifying and speci-
fying business processes integrated with business goals and
requirements; information manipulated within these pro-
cesses; IT applications developed to create, read, update
and delete information; and technology established to
support the IT applications [5].
This study aims at complementing the present knowl-
edge regarding the impacts of EA on different aspects of
BITA, which have not been measured prior. Although
many scholars like Chen et al. [7], Gregor and Hart [8],
Wegmann et al. [12, 21], Kang et al. [22], van der Raadt

We have started a two-phase study on the performance

2 Literature overview
Previous and recent studies, which were examined to
develop a theoretical framework for identifying and testing
the EA effects on BITA, are mentioned in this Section.
This is followed by the research proposition developed on
the basis of the literature review.
2.1 Business-IT alignment
Based on the literature reviewed in this study [1, 6, 20, 27–
29], BITA can be defined as managing and utilizing IT in
an organization to respond to business needs, achieve
business goals and acquire competitive advantages. Studies
reveal that organizations with IT-enabled growth are not
only positively affected by economic impacts [30], they
can also achieve a better strategic match, a more efficient
IT architecture with more core competencies, as well as
better decision-making and faster competitive reactions
[31].
As discussed by Alaeddini and Salekfard [5], all the
methods and frameworks developed by scholars to align
business and IT can be contemplated at three levels as
proposed by Camponovo and Pigneur [32]:
1. Alignment of IT with the organizations’ business goals
and strategies (current business level) can be observed
in most of the BITA frameworks [11, 17, 28, 33–37].
2. Aligning the IT features with the requirements of the
organizations’ external environment (external needs
level) as seen in the models developed by Clarke [38],
Wegmann [39] and Yetton et al. [40].
3. Using IT in succeeding organizations in their future
changes (future business level) as Chen et al. [7] and
van Eck et al. [41] have considered in their own
models.
Terms and conditions of IT and how to apply it in any
organization can be explained through the levels. All the
three levels are considered in BITA maturity models and
translated into maturity levels, which will be described
further.
2.2 BITA maturity
The assessment of BITA maturity provides organizations
with an effective tool to evaluate the performance of their
own activities to align business and IT [27]. BITA maturity
models not only generally address the issues required to
achieve BITA [29], they also make it possible for an
organization to identify its position and plan improvement
measures [27] based on the results of that assessment.
Today, numerous articles, studies and models are
available on the topic of BITA maturity assessment within
organizations. The widest accepted BITA maturity models,
most frequently cited in the related research, are presented
by Luftman [27], Tapia [29], Baker et al. [42], Chang et al.
[43], Gutierrez et al. [44], Papp [45], Silva et al. [46] and
Tapia et al. [47]. This makes it difficult to define which

model is the most adequate to use. Some models are
apparently much more accepted than others although there
is still no consensus on the best one. In our experience, the
Luftman’s model [27] can be used in a survey to identify
where a company stands in terms of maturity and once this

improvement of the strategic alignment model proposed by
Henderson and Venkatraman [17], This model is also
useful in evaluating where a particular firm is standing
what it needs to do to attain and sustain BITA, as well as to

[48]. Furthermore, as this model was found to be the most
highly cited in the literature when compared with the other
BITA maturity models [5], it has been selected as the basic
foundation of this study to evaluate the maturity level of
BITA by the respondents.
The Luftman’s model is the result of his own research,
which he commenced by extending the works of Hender-
son and Venkatraman [17] followed by more detailed
studies on the facilitators and inhibitors of alignment [49].
Using the same background, he concluded that the har-
mony prevalent among the elements of the model identified
by Henderson and Venkatraman [17] is impacted by six
principal components.
‘Communications’ between the IT and business
departments is the first component of the Luftman’s
model, which many BITA models [17, 33, 36, 38, 50]
desire to improve. ‘Competency/value measurements’ for
assessing both IT and business departments is the second
component, which some BITA frameworks [28] have
developed to cover. The third component is the IT
‘governance’, which involves specifying the decision
rights and accountability framework to encourage desir-
able behavior in IT usage [51, 52] and is applied to
achieve BITA [6, 14–16]. The fourth component is the
‘partnership’ of the business and IT departments, which is
the key to motivate the development of some recent BITA
frameworks [34, 35, 40]. ‘Scope and architecture’ of IT to
support the business, is the fifth component of the model,
which is the main motivation of several BITA frame-
works [6, 7, 11, 39, 41]. The last component is the human
resources or ‘skills’, which some BITA frameworks [17,
35] have merely touched upon.
As practiced in many maturity models, the scores an
organization achieves for these six components of maturity
are based on the five-point CMMI-based levels [53] as
mentioned below [27]:
1. Initial/ad-hoc process
2. Committed process
3. Established focused process
4. Improved/managed process
5. Optimized process.
2.3 Enterprise architecture as a BITA approach
Scholars have unpacked EA as a set of models and defi-
nitions that can describe the structure of an enterprise, its
subsystems and the relationships between them, which
include the relationships with the external environment,
terminology to employ and guiding principles for design
and future evolution [54–57]. These descriptions are clas-
sified under four different aspects termed architecture
layers—business, information, application and technology
[58, 59]. Furthermore, EA is implicitly defined in the EA
frameworks, which include tools, techniques, artifact
descriptions, process models, reference models and guid-
ance, used by architects to give an enterprise-specific
architectural description.
Developing EA is an iterative process in which the EA
models and artifacts are converted to incorporate new
business trends, technology trends, capabilities and inno-
vations by using appropriate frameworks [59–61]. Inves-
tigation of the EA frameworks reveals that they implicitly
ensure that organizations achieve a specified level of BITA
maturity [62]. As mentioned prior in the introduction,
theoretically, an organization can realize BITA via EA
through analyzing and understanding the following:
• The business processes of the organization based on its
business goals and requirements
• Information that will be created, updated or manipu-
lated through these processes
• Applications which can save or change this information
• Technology that can support these applications
• Matrices for addressing the relationships among the
four issues mentioned above
The literature review shows that many scholars have
addressed the relationship between EA and BITA in their
studies. For the first time, Veasey [63] presented an EA
framework covering all the BITA aspects; however, the
first EA method to align business and IT was presented
4 years later by Wegmann et al. [12] later on completed by
him [21]. In addition, Kang et al. [22] presented a con-
ceptual system for strategy alignment under EA, and van
der Raadt et al. [23] produced an EA measurement model
to cover agility and alignment within the organizations.
Based on the literature recently reviewed by us, some new
studies have been conducted in this field. Engelsman et al.
[64] proposed an EA modeling language based on the
existing works on business goals and requirements mod-
eling to support the modeling of the underlying motivation
of EA in terms of stakeholder concerns and the high-level
goals that address these concerns, as well as to illustrate
how EA can benefit from analysis techniques from the
requirements engineering domain. In another study, Elhari

and Bounabat [24] presented a platform for modeling EA
and for assessing strategic alignments based on the internal
EA metrics. This platform attempts to reveal the role of EA
in BITA assessment. Besides, Clark et al. [25] proposed a
light-weight method and introduced a language for EA
simulation to overcome BITA as an ongoing challenge
facing organizations. These scholars had proposed new
methods and frameworks for performing EA specifically
focusing on BITA.
Another group of scholars in this field have regarded EA
as the main approach to align business and IT. Gregor and
Hart [8] proposed ways by which EA can enable BITA in a
real case, and Xueying et al. [65] explained the benefits of
EA to BITA, as well as BITA concerns. Moreover, we
found certain new and related studies in our recent litera-
ture reviews. Tamm et al. [66] focused on the organiza-
tional benefits offered by EA and presented the EA benefits
model too, to assess its impact on organizational alignment,
information availability, resource portfolio optimization
and resource complementarity. Seigerroth [67] presented a
conceptualization of the research area of EA focusing on
transformation and the alignment of business and IT,
whereas Fritscher and Pigneur [26] showed how the EA
models, with a strong focus on the business model strategy,
can assist in IT alignment, at the level of both the business
model and IT infrastructure. Furthermore, Bradley et al.
[68] proved the presence of a positive relationship between
the stage of EA maturity and ability to manage external
relationships, ability to lower the operational costs and
strategic agility. Shortly thereafter, Bradley et al. [69]
showed that EA maturity directly influenced the effec-
tiveness of the IT resources of an organization to achieve
strategic goals and indirectly when the IT alignment was
incorporated as a mediating variable.
In comparison with the two research groups mentioned
above, some studies have paid great attention to BITA and
assessed EA as one of the triple approaches to leveraging
it. Business model, business architecture and IT architec-
ture are the three significant layers considered by Chen
et al. [7] in their alignment model. In another similar study,
Saat et al. [70] proposed a meta-model for BITA by
focusing on the quality of the IT systems, business and IT
governance.
Other scholars have focused more on the organizational
issues by applying EA as an appropriate tool. From this
point of view, Varghese and Kurien [71] considered both
EA flexibility and IT delivery efficiency as the two drivers
that aid in achieving sustainable competitive advantages.
Furthermore, EA and BITA were considered the basis of
the models proposed by Plazaola et al. [72] and Wang et al.
[73] to plan each organization’s IT actions. One year later,
Aier and Winter [74] proposed and demonstrated an
alternative bottom-up approach through a case-study to
present a hybrid term ‘alignment architecture’ for aligning
business architecture and IT architecture. Later, the model
presented by Haki and Forte [75], utilized FEA [58] and
PMBOK [76] to direct service-oriented architecture in
organizations. Recently, Foorthuis et al. [77] presented the
results of a large statistical analysis on many benefits that
EA delivers for both individual projects and the organiza-
tion as a whole.
3 Research theory, method and data
3.1 Proposition development
In the first phase of study, we identified a significant
relationship between the EA and each of the six aspects of
BITA in some Iranian organizations. In the second phase,
as a complementary research, our main objective was to
develop a few real hypotheses based on the results of the
preliminary phase and to strongly approve/reject them
through the results of a global survey.
As mentioned in our previous paper, many scholars have
addressed a relationship between the EA and business-IT
communications [78–83], a relationship between the EA
and performance indicators [84–88], a relationship between
the EA and IT governance [89–91], a relationship between
the EA and business-IT partnership [88, 92, 93], a rela-
tionship between the EA and IT architecture [6–8, 12, 21–
23, 63, 65, 71–73, 75], and a relationship between the EA
and human skills [94] as the six main components of BITA.
Therefore, the first hypothesis of the research stated in this
study is:
Hypothesis 1 Conducting EA positively impacts the
BITA maturity.
This hypothesis includes six aspects, as mentioned
below, which should be construed through asserting them.
If some of the hypotheses 1.a to 1.f are true, then auto-
matically hypothesis 1 will hold true. It means hypothesis 1
will be falsified if all sub-hypotheses (1.a to 1.f) are
rejected.
Bahrami et al. [78] introduced EA as an applicable tool
to simulate the businesses of various organizations.
Besides, he introduced some of the likely future directions
in both areas. Ross [82] explained how the ability to rec-
ognize the learning acquired in EA can enable firms to
invest wisely and maximize the benefits ranging from
faster IT response times to increased strategic impact from
IT. Later, El Kourdi et al. [80] proposed a framework for
knowledge discovery via the EA repository. Furthermore,
Buckl et al. [79] projected a Knowledge Management
(KM) perspective on the prevalent EA management
approaches. Jafari et al. [81] also explored the role of

Schelp and Stutz [87] suggested a multi-perspective
framework, based on the concept of balanced scorecards,
for providing guidance on where to identify and quantify
the value of EA from a management perspective. Then,
Velitchkov [88] justified the inclusion of EA as a
prospective vehicle in balanced scorecards used for IT
strategy management and measurement. Furthermore, a
few selected key performance indicators were localized to
assess the benefits of EA. Cotton et al. [84] examined those
aspects of the architecture that are most valuable to the
stakeholders in the two areas of system effectiveness val-
ues and architecture quality values. Gustafsson et al. [85]
used the EA models to present a set of performance indi-
cators and attempted to analyze an organization’s perfor-
mance. A study of some business performance
measurements by EA helped us arrive at hypothesis 1.b:
Hypothesis 1.b EA will positively influence the demon-
stration of IT values on the business in terms that the
business understands (competency/value measurements,
termed CVM).
Lindstrom et al. [89] argued that the CIO’s need for
decision support should guide the proper development of
an EA framework. They also presented a review on the
manner the existing EA frameworks address the concerns
of the CIOs. They focused on senior IT managers’ deci-
sion-making with respect to their application portfolios and
the role of EA therein. Molinaro et al. [90] presented an
approach for a model that supports IT governance through
the BITA using EA concepts, focusing on efficiency,
effectiveness and security. Nakakawa et al. [91] considered
some challenges in EA development that indicate the need
for collaborative decision-making to be deployed during
creating the architecture. Recently, Foorthuis et al. [77]
identified compliance assessment and formal approval as
some important EA practices that have a major impact on
realization of BITA in organizations. On reviewing these
studies, EA appears to be in a position to help
organizations in their decision processes and prioritization
of their IT efforts. Therefore, hypothesis 1.c can be stated
as:
Hypothesis 1.c EA will positively influence the IT
decision-making, IT prioritization and IT resource alloca-
tion (governance, termed GOV).
Ross et al. [93] asserted that the IT infrastructure and
digitized business processes that automate an organiza-
tion’s core capabilities are the foundation for executing its
business strategies, with EA being the approach to achieve
this foundation. Ireland [92] presented a model, including
the hierarchy of vision, mission, organizational strategy
and objectives involved in the operations planning and
portfolio management. He stated that EA would act as a
collaborative program between the aspects of business
planning and business operations and enable the techno-
logical infrastructure of the organization. Velitchkov [88]
provided the rationale and concept for integration of the
models and modeling objects of EA and IT strategies.
Recently, Foorthuis et al. [77] identified Explicitly linking
to business goals as an important EA practice that have a
major impact on realization of BITA in organizations.
These studies show that EA could be a facilitator in
defining and executing business strategies and creating
mutual understanding between the business and IT. Thus,
hypothesis 1.d is stated as:
Hypothesis 1.d The relationships between the business
and IT units in an organization, including their partnership
on the risks and benefits (partnership, termed PRT), will be
positively influenced by EA.
As reviewed in the literature, several scholars have
studied the relationship between architectural issues,
especially EA and BITA [6–8, 12, 21–23, 63, 65, 71–73,
75]. Moreover, regarding the earlier definition of EA, it is a
highly detailed description of the structure of an enterprise,
which comprises the enterprise components and externally
visible properties of those components and the relation-
ships existing between them. It describes the terminology,
composition of the enterprise components and their rela-
tionships with the external environment, including the
guiding principles for the analysis, design and evolution of
an enterprise. This leads to hypothesis 1.e:
Hypothesis 1.e EA has a positive impact on the stan-
dardization, integration and management of the business
needs (scope and architecture, termed SAR).
Vail [83] revealed that KM as part of human resource
skills can relate to EA and integrate with it. Furthermore,
Fairhead and Good [94] identified the major stakeholders
in EA and suggested the places they may occur in an
organization and how they may be connected with EA.

Moreover, in the first phase of our study, we found that

these different effects that can assist in investigating the
suppositions mentioned above in the future, the second
hypothesis of the research stated in this study is:
Hypothesis 2 The effects of EA on the BITA aspects are
different, and can be arranged in descending order from
‘scope and architecture’ to ‘partnership’ to ‘governance’ to
‘competency/value measurements’ to ‘skills’ to

Figure 1 shows the research model of the study. As
presented here, we are dealing with a formative structure
[95–97]. According to the four primary decision rules
stated by Jarvis et al. [97] for determining if a construct is
reflective or formative, our arguments for choosing the
formative approach include the following reasons:
1. Considering the nature of the measures, direction of
causality was from items to construct because indica-
tors were defining characteristics of the construct.
Looking at the elements of the Luftman’s compo-
nents/topics of Table 1, it strongly is clear that BITA
aspects are defined by their own measures.
2. Measures were not interchangeable, since they would
measure different things and would not share a
common theme. This means that dropping one of the
indicators would affect the meaning of the construct.
Table 1 shows that they are different measures in terms
of their content and should therefore be assumed not
interchangeable.
3. It was not necessary for indicators to covary with each
other. As understood from Table 1, a change in one of
the measures does not necessarily lead to changes in
the other measures.
4. Measures did not require the same antecedents and
consequences because they were definitions of the
construct.
As an example to prove that the construct is formative,
‘protocol rigidity’ is something very different from
‘knowledge sharing’, they refer to different characteris-
tics/tactics and they need not necessarily be present simul-
taneously and need not inevitably covary, which in this case
means their maturity covaries. Likewise, it is obvious that
dropping one of the measures will affect the meaning of the
construct, as something misses that was present before. For
example, one can drop ‘continuous improvement’ from
CVM, which will mean the construct’s meaning is altered
since there is no interchangeable/identical other item. It also
seems highly likely that the indicators of a given construct
have different antecedents and consequences. For example,
we would say that the level of ‘career crossover’ is very
dependent on how specialized and knowledge intensive the
work is, whereas ‘locus of power’ and ‘management style’
are determined more culturally.

3.4 Test of validity and reliability
3.4.1 Content validity
Content validity involved the systematic examination of
the test content to determine whether it covered a repre-
sentative sample of the behavior domain to be measured
[112], and referred to this question, ‘What was the extent to
which the items in the tool sample represented the com-
plete range of the attribute under study?’ Content validity
evidence involves the degree to which the content of a test
matches the content domain associated with the construct.
As mentioned earlier, a well-known framework was
considered for the original source of the metrics to assess
the BITA maturity and the information proceeding from it
needed to be valid because the variables were derived from
an instrument that had been successfully applied in a
similar study type [107, 108]. Moreover, the carefully
phrased responses in the questionnaire were constructed to
enable the participants to select the best response from a
significant range of items. Scrupulous selection of those
responses ensured the content validity of the study [104,
112] as well as the use of a renowned tool accepted by most
of the experts in the field of study [5, 48], to test the
research propositions [113]. Furthermore, no question was
mandatory to prevent respondents from answering ques-
tions they do not really know the answer to. Consequently,
respondents could leave out as many or little items as they
pleased.
3.4.2 Construct validity
Construct validity is the degree to which the variables
employed in a study accurately measure the concepts they
purport to measure [114]. It basically refers to this ques-
tion, ‘Are the theory and information sources used in the
study adequate?’ This implies that the construct validity
indicates the extent to which operationalization of a con-
struct actually measures what the theory states it does.
Given the objective of formative constructs is to retain
the unique variance of each measure and not just the shared
variance among measures [115], and owing to the fact that
common factor analysis or methods which focus on com-
mon variance are useless for formative constructs [116–
118], item weights calculated through the PLS analysis was
applied to evaluate the reduced dimensionality of the
measures [115, 117].

3.4.4 Reliability
The reliability of a study design refers to the ability of an
instrument to consistently measure an attribute [113] and
implies that the experiment is replicable [114].
Traditional notions of reliability are not meaningful
within the context of formative constructs [116, 118].
Internal consistency or reliability is unimportant in forma-
tive models because measures are examining different
facets of the construct [96, 117]. Instead, multicollinearity,
which is desired among measures for reflective constructs,
is a problem for measures of formative constructs [97, 117].
Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) of each formative
indicator is shown in Table 3. The VIF values of all
indicators in both states were less than the excellent value
3.3 [117] except for four questions MQ-29 and MQ-32 in
SAR, and MQ-36 and MQ-38 in SKL for ‘before EA’;
also, three items MQ-2 in COM, and MQ-37 and MQ-38
in SKL for ‘after EA’, which were not higher than 5.0
(indicator for a potential collinearity problem) [122], and
because the calculated condition indices ranged between 9
and 30, the constructs did not suffer from multi-
collinearity [123].
3.4.5 Common method bias
Despite the fact that common method bias could exist when
data is compiled from individual respondents, we took
several steps to ensure that such bias did not affect the
results. We measured all the key variables in our model
using systemized data. Data for the variables included
actual figures as reported by the respondent. Notwith-
standing that this data was collected as part of our survey,
the scope of bias in reporting was minimized because of the
objective nature of the measures.
To further address the issue of common method bias, we
followed the recommended procedures to detect such bia-
ses and rule out the likelihood of our results being affected
by them. We first performed Harman’s one-factor test
[124]. When all the variables were entered into an
Exploratory Factor Analysis, no single overarching factor
emerged. Moreover, the first factor explained only 38.06 %
of the total variance for ‘before EA’ and 31.61 % for ‘after
EA’.
Although the variables here could very well be related,
following the method of Lindell and Whitney [125], we
assessed the bivariate correlations of the constructs used in
the study against two marker variables: the respondents’
tenures in their organization and the respondents’ field
experiences. The respondents’ tenures in the organization
were defined by their organizational positions (0 : staff and
1: manager) and the respondents’ experience in IT/IS was
derived from the relation of their organizational units with
the field of IT/IS (0: business and 1: IT). The correlations
are reported in Table 9. None of the correlations reported
were high enough to suspect common method bias, with
the highest correlation value being only 0.14 between the
pairs of each respondent’s tenure in the organization and
organizational size. Based on the results mentioned above,
we concluded that no significant common method bias was

To examine the structural model, PLS generates stan-

structural model and are interpreted as in ordinary least

Furthermore, bootstrapping provides a basis for evalu-
ating parameter estimates and their confidence intervals
based on multiple estimations. Bootstrapping, using 5000
samples with replacement, was used to assess the signifi-
cance of the path coefficients [137, 138].
Statistically significant t values indicate that even with a
large number of recombined existing data points there is
sufficient nonrandom variation in the data to yield
stable coefficients for the predictors in the model. To
summarize, PLS offers results with small data samples,
does not require multivariate normality and converges best
on the least unbiased estimators with increasing sample
size and statistically significant results. Rather, It is inap-
propriate in PLS to use any overall goodness-of-fit (GoF)
measures, as is used in the covariance structure analysis
modeling, because PLS makes no distributional assump-
tions [139]. Since the GoF is also not applicable to for-
matively measurement models and does not penalize over-
parametrization efforts [122, 140], we did not use this
measure.
Table 6 displays the path coefficients, the t statistic and
their significance levels, p values, and the bootstrap con-
fidence intervals, which provide additional information on
the stability of the coefficient estimates. The confidence
interval is the range into which the true population
parameter will fall assuming a certain level of confidence
(95 %) [122]. We reported them because the construction
of the interval is based on the standard errors obtained from
the bootstrapping procedure.

that EA would be associated significantly with BITA and
its components. In fact, the relationships between EA and
all BITA components had magnitudes of 0.508 or more and
were statistically significant because of their high t values.
Thus, hypotheses 1.a to 1.f and therefore, hypothesis 1
were supported by the data. In addition, hypothesis 1 also
was tested separately. In other words, validating the effect

from the EA to the BITA construct. Applying PLS-SEM
showed the coefficient 0.735 with the t statistic of 28.789
and the standard error of 0.026. This means that hypothesis
1 was strongly approved.
Next, the relationship between the firm size and BITA
had a magnitude of 0.021 and was statistically significant
(t value of 2.591). On the contrary, as revealed in Fig. 4,
path coefficients from geographic location, firm industry
and EA framework to BITA were so close to zero and were
not statistically significant due to their low t values. It
statistically means that leveraging the BITA through EA
will not be affected by the firm’s location and industry or
the EA framework used by the firm.
Moreover, sorting the values assigned to the relation-

of EA on BITA were graded in descending order from
‘scope and architecture’ to ‘partnership’ to ‘governance’ to
‘communications’ to ‘competency/value measurements’ to
‘skills’.

5 Discussion
This study represents an attempt to statistically confirm the
significant positive impact of conducting EA on BITA for
all the variables of its maturity and provide ample evidence
to support the assumption that EA plays an effective role in
BITA improvement. Generalizing the results of the previ-
ous exploratory study that was restricted to enterprises
operating in Iran has been done through this study. By
viewing the principal stakeholders of EA in organizations
in terms of business managers, IT managers, business staff
and IT staff, the following observations emerge.
With respect to the business manager’s role, the aspect
of ‘scope and architecture’ shows the highest degree of
improvement, followed by ‘partnership’, ‘governance’ and
‘communications’. Competency/value measurements and
skills show the least degree of improvement from the
business managers’ viewpoint. Similarly, in the IT man-
agers’ opinion, the aspect of ‘scope and architecture’ holds
the highest degree of improvement, followed by the other
aspects as sequentially, just as discussed for business
managers. These priorities with similar results in the
viewpoints of business and IT staff are listed in Table 8. A
between-groups comparison of the data shows that sepa-
rating the respondents into high and low levels in both
business and IT fields has not resulted in bias in the results.
Furthermore, conducting the Mann–Whitney U test as a
nonparametric means comparison tool [143, 144] showed
no significant difference (p value[0.05) between the
groups ‘‘business manager versus business staff’’ and ‘‘IT
manager versus IT staff.’’

IT participants (70 %?) at the cost of business participants
may occur (i.e. IT participants knew that the survey was

people are accountable to others with known preferences,
their judgments tend to assimilate those preferences.
Business participants in this survey had been selected from
the EA related communities, and therefore, they would be
sufficiently familiar with this term. Statistically, an exam-
ination of the findings reveals the results of Mann–Whitney

of enterprise architecture in aligning business and infor-
mation technology. To achieve this goal, some business/IT
managers and staff from various types and different sizes

6.1 Lessons learned
Prior studies are used to introduce this subject, in partic-
ular, the manner in which developing EA plays an
important role in aligning business with IT in each orga-
nization. This paper assesses this role by evaluating the
main aspects of BITA based on the Luftman’s model (i.e.
communications, competency/value measurement, gover-
nance, partnership, scope and architecture and skills) in
some organizations on a world-scale, which have suc-
cessfully performed EA.
Generally, the results of conducting EA were found to
be positively related to all aspects of the BITA concept.
Such a conclusion, that EA can basically utilize alignment
maturity as a good tool to assess how organizations can
improve their business-IT harmony, was supported by the
results obtained from this study. The alignment of IT with
business has been hailed as a critical element of IT man-
agement and it has indubitably proven to be so in this
study. Besides confirming the main propositions of the
research, other lessons learned from this study are listed
below:
• Wide usage of TOGAF as an EA framework, whose
main focus is on the IT capabilities, shows that EA is an
effective tool for enabling the true perspective on IT
capabilities that business units need for identification,
planning, execution and management of the right
projects, initiatives or requirements to maximize the
benefit for the enterprise.
• Theoretically, EA structures the priorities and alloca-
tion of resources to create accountability and to ensure
that activities are performed in conformity with policies
and procedures. It also creates a snapshot of both the
existing and target information systems and data to
support organizational activities (business). Business
people participating in this study showing similar
opinions with IT personnel reveals that focusing on
business elements is the key to achieving business
transformation and alignment via EA.
• PerformingEAcouldmediallyimprovetheBITAmaturity
level from the initial/ad hoc (1) and committed (2) to the
established focused (3) and improved/managed (4) pro-
cess. This demonstrates that EA cannot solely improve all
theaspectsofBITAandthatgreaterBITAmaturitywillbe
realized by the contribution of two other alignment
approaches, i.e. ‘relationship’ and ‘governance’.
• The effect of EA on BITA was not equally dealt with in
all aspects. ‘Scope and architecture’ was the most
affectedcomponentofBITAinthissurvey,while‘skills’
was identified as the least. This, perhaps, could be due to
theexistenceofEAasaplanningactivity,whosefocusis
not on training and leveraging human skills.
6.2 Industrial and academic impacts
This paper shows that researchers working on EA are
conducting significant research for the industry, as EA can
enable companies to improve in BITA. This study also
enhances their ability to sharpen the focus of their studies.
For instance, as EA shows significant correlation with
BITA maturity, researchers can assist practitioners by
studying the maximum levels of the maturity items that can
be implemented successfully through the EA efforts. This
will also be of interest to academics and developers of the
EA frameworks to conduct more studies in this field and
eliminate the existing weaknesses from the current frame-
works, which are occasionally enumerated by Urbaczewski
and Mrdalj [146]; Tang et al. [147] and Sessions [148]. For
example, as measured by recent scholars, frameworks
Zachman and FEA do a rather poor job in the area of
focusing on using technology to drive business value.
Furthermore, this paper will be of value for Chief
Information Officers, practitioners and consultants in the
fields of EA and IT/IS planning to appreciate the BITA
aspects that cannot be achieved well via EA and to search
for supplementary or alternate mechanisms. As discussed
above, some issues such as ‘competency/value measure-
ments’ and ‘skills’ are given far less consideration in the
EA frameworks and methods than they deserve. These are
the prime points of improvement that need to be considered
in the use of EA.

6.3 Limitations and implications
Inalettersenttotherespondents,weaskedthemiftheyhave
successful implementation of any EA iterations, they are
allowed to answer the questions. At the time of receiving
replies, we were satisfied with their statements. The absence
of some questions inthe questionnaire to prove their success
in EA is one of the limitations of this study.
The retrospective assessment of the situation prior to the
EA development restricts the validity of the findings. The
respondents had been requested ex-post regarding the vari-
ous BITA maturity levels, ‘before’ and ‘after’ conducting
EA in their organizations. Thus, these results could be
biased, as they needed to recall the maturity level of their
organizations from the past, when they might not have even
been employed in those organizations. A concurrent study
during the implementation of EA in some firms is therefore
needed.Intheotherword,thelimitationrelatedtomeasuring
the values of both states at the same time may result in bias.
Ideally, the study would have measured at least two times.
Wehavedealtwithcontingenciesthatmighthaveappeared
due to other improvement activities, simply by asking the
respondents to exclude them in their responses. Dealing with
contingencies should normally be measured using separate
questions and then used as control variables in the statistical
model. More control variables should also be added. Some
importantvariablesaretheothercausesthatinfluencethesub-
constructs, amongst others the non-EA approaches for BITA:
governance and relationships—and related concepts such as
management support and strategic programs.
Not having included any quantitative measure in the
questionnaire that could influence the respondents, could
be considered as another limitation. Although we took all
the necessary precautions to reduce the social desirability
bias proposed by Snijders and Matzat [149], the respon-
dents would answer ‘low’ before EA and ‘high’ after EA
on all BITA measures, if they believed that was the case—
perhaps without complete knowledge of the true values.
Objective measures always increase the validity of the
research model; therefore, retesting the hypotheses using
this new tool is a must.
Another limitation that needs mention is the potential
bias introduced by using the EA discussion groups on
LinkedIn as the sole source for finding respondents.
Choosing a single source of respondents made it difficult to
propose any generalizations about how individuals from
business or IT populations could have participated in this
study. Therefore, further searches are necessary to identify
new participants who desire to start new EA practices,
through international conferences and the like.
6.4 Future studies
First, analyzing two less significant BITA aspects, i.e. the
skills of the IT personnel and business/IT core compe-
tencies, which should be more prominent in the EA
frameworks, as well as the other aspects mentioned prior,
so that they clearly describe their effects on BITA during
performing EA can be used for future studies. A new
emerged EA framework based on these analyses will pay
more attention to the neglected aspects and therefore,
ensure more BITA maturity level.
Second, designing a full quantitative or a mixed method
to evaluate the BITA maturity level, followed by examin-
ing the effects of the three different approaches of BITA
achievement, including architecture, governance and rela-
tionship, on this level, can be considered as another field of
research in the future.
Third, merging and changing current EA maturity
models to be adapted with the top affected BITA aspects
with the purpose of providing a new maturity model for
EA can be studied in future works. Comparing EA
maturity scores derived through this new method with
BITA maturity levels of the studied organizations may be
utilized as a suitable means for calibrating the model.
Fourth, the current study was performed notably with
regard to the first step of a research plan composed of the
four steps listed below:
1. Examining the effect of successfully architecting IT
(i.e. EA) on BITA
2. Examining the effect of maturely governing IT (i.e.
ITG) on BITA
3. Examining the effect of strategically planning IT (i.e.
ITSP) on BITA
4. Prioritizing the approaches mentioned above and
proposing a portfolio of necessary IT actions based
on the organizations’ conditions to compensate for one
another’s weaknesses and enhance the BITA maturity
level
The authors will extend their results to steps 2 and 3, to
emphasize the importance of this study and enlarge their
samples for better validation and error control. Following
that, an empirical study on step 4 will be necessary.
